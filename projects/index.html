What these projects are

These six subjects are study proposals for the end of the taught year. They are not an official MVA module. They do not replace the assessment written on a course page or on master-mva.com.

Each subject joins at least one semester 1 course with one semester 2 course. Three follow the example routes on the home page. Three follow the official tracks Santé, I&I, and RR. A route is reading guidance. A track is an official label.

Work for five weeks, about eight hours a week. A group has 3 or 4 people. The solo variant keeps the question and drops one arm of the comparison. Use a laptop. Prefer a public data set. Use a synthetic set when a real set’s licence is unclear.

Pages that are still skeletons are not linked. specs/coherence-objectives.md lists them. The home page already points at this page.

Learning theory route

Courses

  • Theoretical foundations of deep learning, semester 1. Use the chapters on expressivity, generalisation, and double descent.
  • Kernel methods for machine learning, semester 2. Use kernel ridge regression and the chapter on deep learning with kernels.
  • Large random matrices, semester 2. Use the chapter on kernel random matrices.

Convex optimisation, optimal transport, stochastic calculus, sequential learning, and stopping times sit on this route in the home page. Their notes are unfinished, so this proposal does not link them.

Research question

On a laptop-scale classification set, how close is a small neural network to a kernel method, and does the spectrum of the Gram matrix help you read the test error?

Use a subsample of MNIST, or the digits data shipped with scikit-learn. Train a small fully connected network. Fit kernel ridge regression and random Fourier features on the same split. Plot the spectrum of the Gram matrix.

Success is one table of test errors for the three methods on that split, with the split fixed in advance, and one spectrum plot that you can relate to the random-matrix chapter.

Milestones

  1. Week 1. Freeze the data, the split, and the success table before any training.
  2. Week 2. Fit the kernel method and show one plot that it runs on the frozen split.
  3. Week 3. Train the small network on the same split.
  4. Week 4. Add random features, plot the Gram spectrum, and repeat the test error on at least three seeds.
  5. Week 5. Write the report and rehearse the talk. Say what would have made you drop the claim.

Deliverables

  • Code that rebuilds the table and the spectrum plot from a fixed seed.
  • A report. 6 to 10 pages for the group, 4 to 6 pages solo.
  • A talk. 15 minutes for the group, 10 minutes solo.

Evaluation

This rubric is a proposal. It is not the Kernel Methods exam, the data challenge, or the homework weights on that course page.

Criterion Weight What earns the marks
Question, tied to the linked chapters 20 % The report names the chapters and says what result would weaken the claim
Method and code 30 % A classmate can rerun the code. The kernel method is implemented in the group’s code
Comparison 30 % The same split is used for every arm, and the test error is reported over seeds
Report and oral 20 % The report stays inside the page limit, and the talk fits its slot

The weights sum to 100 %.

Variants

  1. [Solo · ≈ 40 h] Network against kernel ridge. Drop random features. Keep the spectrum plot for the kernel you fit.
  2. [Group of 3 or 4 · ≈ 40 h each] Three arms. One person owns the kernel solver, one the network, one the spectrum and the seeds, and the fourth writes the comparison if the group has four people.

Vision and imaging route

Courses

  • Image denoising, semester 1. Use a classical denoiser and the comparison with a small convolutional denoiser.
  • Object recognition and computer vision, semester 1. Use the chapter on convolutional networks for recognition.
  • Deep learning for image restoration and synthesis, semester 2. Use the chapters on denoising and on perception versus distortion.

Further written pages on the same route, if you change the data to 3D or to satellite images: generative models for images, point clouds and 3D modelling, remote sensing. 3D computer vision has no page yet. The semester 1 deep learning page is still a skeleton.

Research question

You add noise to images, denoise them, then measure whether a recogniser trained on clean images recovers its score. Pixel error and recognition accuracy need not rank the denoisers in the same order. The restoration notes treat those two numbers as different criteria.

Use a few hundred images you are allowed to redistribute, or the digits data in scikit-learn, with synthetic Gaussian noise. Train the recogniser only on clean images. Compare one classical denoiser with one small convolutional denoiser.

Success is a table, on one frozen split, with pixel error and recognition accuracy for the noisy images and for both denoisers.

Milestones

  1. Week 1. Freeze the images, the noise level, the split, and the two metrics.
  2. Week 2. Implement the classical denoiser and the clean-image recogniser.
  3. Week 3. Train the small convolutional denoiser on the same kind of noise.
  4. Week 4. Fill the table. Add one ablation, for example a second noise level.
  5. Week 5. Write the report and rehearse the talk.

Deliverables

  • Code that rebuilds the table from a fixed seed.
  • A report. 6 to 10 pages for the group, 4 to 6 pages solo.
  • A talk. 15 minutes for the group, 10 minutes solo.

Evaluation

This rubric is a proposal. It is not the official assessment of the denoising, recognition, or restoration courses.

Criterion Weight What earns the marks
Question, tied to the linked chapters 20 % The report treats pixel error and recognition accuracy as separate criteria
Method and code 30 % A classmate can rerun both denoisers and the recogniser
Comparison 30 % One split, both metrics, and at least one ablation
Report and oral 20 % The report stays inside the page limit, and the talk fits its slot

The weights sum to 100 %.

Variants

  1. [Solo · ≈ 40 h] One denoiser against the noisy baseline. Keep the recogniser and both metrics. Drop the second denoiser.
  2. [Group of 3 or 4 · ≈ 40 h each] Classical, network, and recognition. Split the two denoisers, the recogniser, and the table across the group.

Generative AI and LLM route

Courses

  • Probabilistic graphical models and deep generative models, semester 1. Use the chapter on energy-based and score-based models.
  • Diffusion and multiscale models, semester 2. Use the chapters on score diffusion and on learning the score.
  • Large language models for code and proof, semester 2. Use the chapters on transformers and on pretraining.
  • Training and deploying large-scale models, semester 2. Use the opening chapters on distributed training for the cost accounting, at laptop scale.

Foundations of large language models, the introductory deep learning page, stochastic calculus, and reinforcement learning are on this route and are still skeletons. This proposal does not link them.

Research question

Train a small score-based sampler on a laptop-scale image set, and write the cost the way the large-model course counts cost. Parameters, number of images, and a rough FLOP estimate are enough. In the same weeks, train a tiny transformer on a small text or code sample you are allowed to redistribute, and write the same three numbers.

You do not need a cluster. A sampler that produces recognisable samples on digits, and a transformer that overfits a tiny corpus, are enough if the accounting is honest.

Success is a page of samples, a loss curve for each arm, and a one-page cost note that uses the same three quantities for both arms.

Milestones

  1. Week 1. Freeze both data sets and the three quantities you will report.
  2. Week 2. Implement the score-matching loss and show a loss curve.
  3. Week 3. Sample from the diffusion model. Start the tiny transformer.
  4. Week 4. Finish both loss curves and the cost note. Check the FLOP estimate by hand on one layer.
  5. Week 5. Write the report and rehearse the talk.

Deliverables

  • Code for both arms, with fixed seeds.
  • The cost note, either inside the report or as a one-page appendix.
  • A report of 6 to 10 pages for the group, or 4 to 6 pages solo, and a talk of 15 minutes for the group, or 10 minutes solo.

Evaluation

This rubric is a proposal. It is not the official assessment of the four linked courses.

Criterion Weight What earns the marks
Question, tied to the linked chapters 20 % The cost note uses quantities from the large-model notes, computed at laptop scale
Method and code 30 % Both training loops rerun from a seed
Comparison 30 % The same three cost quantities are reported for both arms
Report and oral 20 % The report stays inside the page limit, and the talk fits its slot

The weights sum to 100 %.

Variants

  1. [Solo · ≈ 40 h] Diffusion arm only. Keep the cost note. Drop the transformer.
  2. [Group of 3 or 4 · ≈ 40 h each] Diffusion and a tiny language model. Split the sampler, the transformer, and the cost check across the group.

Track Santé

Courses

  • Medical image analysis, semester 1. Use a classical segmentation or classification method from the notes.
  • Deep learning for medical imaging, semester 2. Use the segmentation or classification chapter, and the chapter on validation.
  • Biostatistics, semester 2. Use the chapter on validating a predictive model, and name one bias the notes discuss.

The hospital immersion is the official paired project with a physician. It has its own assessment. This proposal does not replace it. Computational statistics is a Santé course labelled Learning, if you want the interval to come from a Monte Carlo chapter you have already studied.

Research question

On a public medical set that fits a laptop, compare a classical method with a small network. A MedMNIST subset is a sufficient example. Report a held-out score with a bootstrap interval. In one paragraph, name one bias from the biostatistics or validation chapters, such as selection, site shift, or shortcut learning, and say what you did about it.

Success is the table of scores with intervals on a frozen split, plus that paragraph.

Milestones

  1. Week 1. Freeze the data, the split, the score, and the bias you will discuss.
  2. Week 2. Implement the classical method and one baseline number.
  3. Week 3. Train the small network on the same split.
  4. Week 4. Compute the bootstrap interval and write the bias paragraph from the numbers you have.
  5. Week 5. Write the report and rehearse the talk.

Deliverables

  • Code that rebuilds the table and the interval.
  • A report of 6 to 10 pages for the group, 4 to 6 pages solo.
  • A talk of 15 minutes for the group, 10 minutes solo.

Evaluation

This rubric is a proposal. It is not the official assessment of the medical imaging courses, of biostatistics, or of the hospital immersion.

Criterion Weight What earns the marks
Question, tied to the linked chapters 20 % The bias paragraph names a bias from the notes and ties it to your split
Method and code 30 % Both the classical method and the network rerun from a seed
Comparison 30 % The held-out score has a bootstrap interval on a frozen split
Report and oral 20 % The report stays inside the page limit, and the talk fits its slot

The weights sum to 100 %.

Variants

  1. [Solo · ≈ 40 h] Classical method and the interval. Drop the network. Keep the bias paragraph.
  2. [Group of 3 or 4 · ≈ 40 h each] Classical, network, and the interval. Split those three pieces across the group.

Track I&I

Courses

  • Statistical learning for geosciences, semester 1. Use the inversion chapter as the baseline.
  • Learning physical phenomena, semester 2. Use a Gaussian-process or polynomial-chaos surrogate, then calibration and uncertainty quantification.

The other I&I pages are written, and they are different industrial settings. Swap one in only if you rewrite the question to match that page. Deep learning and signal processing has detection and reconstruction use cases. The machine intelligence of images follows a camera pipeline. Network optimization is about flows, capacity, and placement of network components, not about physical fields.

Research question

Build a synthetic forward model, either a blur or a small discretised diffusion. Draw a sparse set of observations. Invert them with a simple baseline you can relate to the geosciences chapter. Then replace repeated calls to the forward model with a surrogate from the physics course, and attach a prediction interval.

Success is a surrogate whose predictions you can plot against held-out outputs, and an interval that covers those outputs at about the rate the interval claims. State the rate you aimed at before you look at the coverage.

Milestones

  1. Week 1. Freeze the forward model, the observation design, and the coverage rate you will claim.
  2. Week 2. Implement the baseline inversion and one plot of its error.
  3. Week 3. Fit the surrogate on simulations from the same forward model.
  4. Week 4. Compute coverage on held-out inputs. If the coverage misses the claim, say so and adjust the interval in a way the notes allow.
  5. Week 5. Write the report and rehearse the talk.

Deliverables

  • Code for the forward model, the baseline, the surrogate, and the coverage check.
  • A report of 6 to 10 pages for the group, 4 to 6 pages solo.
  • A talk of 15 minutes for the group, 10 minutes solo.

Evaluation

This rubric is a proposal. It is not the official assessment of the geosciences course or of the physics and uncertainty course.

Criterion Weight What earns the marks
Question, tied to the linked chapters 20 % The coverage rate is stated before the check, and the surrogate is the one the physics notes describe
Method and code 30 % The forward model and the surrogate rerun from a seed
Comparison 30 % Held-out coverage is reported next to the rate you claimed
Report and oral 20 % The report stays inside the page limit, and the talk fits its slot

The weights sum to 100 %.

Variants

  1. [Solo · ≈ 40 h] Surrogate and coverage. Keep a very small forward model. Drop a second inversion method.
  2. [Group of 3 or 4 · ≈ 40 h each] Baseline, surrogate, and coverage. Split those three pieces across the group.

Track RR

Courses

  • Fundamentals of reproducible research and free software, semester 1. Use the chapters on numerical determinism, environments, and writing a reproducible article.
  • Reproducible research project, semester 2. The official module asks for an IPOL-style article, source code, an online demo, and a review of another group.
  • Kernel methods, semester 2, is one ready-made numerical payload. A paper-study subject on any full course page is another. Pick one payload and stay with it.

Research question

Take one numerical experiment you can run on a laptop and ship it so a classmate rebuilds the figure with one command. Give the code and the data a licence and a citation. Fix the seed. Ask a classmate to review the package and to name one claim they could not reproduce.

This is a rehearsal of the semester 2 module, not a substitute for it. That page states the official weights. The reproducible project counts 80 %, and the intermediate practical work counts 20 %. This proposal does not change those weights. A local notebook may stand in for the online demo if you cannot publish on IPOL during these five weeks.

Success is a rebuild by someone who did not write the code, and a review of at most two pages that names one unreproduced claim or states that the figure matched.

Milestones

  1. Week 1. Choose the experiment, the licence, and the command you want a classmate to run.
  2. Week 2. Pin the environment and make the command rebuild one figure on your machine.
  3. Week 3. Write the short article around that figure, with the data citation.
  4. Week 4. Hand the package to a classmate. Record what failed.
  5. Week 5. Repair one failure, or document why you left it, and rehearse the talk.

Deliverables

  • The one-command package, including the environment pin and the licence.
  • A short article. 6 to 10 pages for the group, 4 to 6 pages solo.
  • A review of at most two pages, written by someone outside the author group.
  • A talk of 15 minutes for the group, 10 minutes solo.

Evaluation

This rubric is a proposal for the rehearsal. The official 80 % and 20 % weights stay on the module page.

Criterion Weight What earns the marks
Question, tied to the linked chapters 20 % The package has a licence, a data citation, and a fixed seed
Method and code 30 % A classmate rebuilds the figure with the documented command
Comparison 30 % The review names a claim and says whether the rebuild matched it
Report and oral 20 % The article stays inside the page limit, and the talk fits its slot

The weights sum to 100 %.

Variants

  1. [Solo · ≈ 40 h] One figure, one reviewer. You still need a classmate for the review. You write the package alone.
  2. [Group of 3 or 4 · ≈ 40 h each] Author group and exchange. The group writes one package. Each member also reviews another group’s package, so everyone is an author and a reviewer, as the semester 2 module asks.
