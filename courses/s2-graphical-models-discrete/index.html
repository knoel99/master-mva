0. Review: discrete labels, graphs and energies

A pixel, a word or a diagnosis takes one value in a finite set. The rest of the course minimizes a cost on those values. This chapter fixes the notation and one equivalence. The most probable labeling is a labeling of lowest energy.

Let G = (V, E) be an undirected graph. Each node s carries a random label X_s in a finite set \mathcal{L}.

A configuration x assigns one label to every node. Write N(s) for the neighbours of s.

Definition 0.1 — Pairwise energy

The energy of a configuration is a sum of unary costs and edge costs.

E(x) = \sum_{s \in V} \theta_s(x_s) + \sum_{\{s,t\} \in E} \theta_{st}(x_s, x_t).

The number \theta_s(k) is the cost of putting label k on node s. The number \theta_{st}(k, \ell) is the cost of that pair on an edge. Adding a constant to every configuration does not change the minimizer. You may shift one unary table so that its smallest entry is 0.

Definition 0.2 — Gibbs distribution

The Gibbs distribution of an energy E is

p(x) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp(-E(x)), \qquad Z = \sum_{x} \exp(-E(x)).

The sum that defines Z has |\mathcal{L}|^{|V|} terms. Ten labels on one hundred nodes give 10^{100} terms. You compute Z by listing states only on a tiny graph, or by the recursion of ch. 1 when the graph is a tree.

Proposition 0.3 — MAP is minimum energy

Any maximizer of p is a minimizer of E, and any minimizer of E is a maximizer of p.

Proof. The partition function Z is positive and does not depend on x.

The exponential is strictly increasing. So -E(x) \ge -E(y) if and only if E(x) \le E(y).

Therefore p(x) \ge p(y) if and only if E(x) \le E(y). The two arg sets are equal.

∎

Proposition 0.4 — Local Markov property

Under a pairwise Gibbs distribution, X_s is conditionally independent of the nodes outside \{s\} \cup N(s), given X_{N(s)}.

Proof. The conditional distribution of X_s is a ratio of two sums of \exp(-E).

Any unary term other than \theta_s, and any edge term that does not touch s, appears in the numerator and the denominator. Those factors cancel.

The remaining factors all touch s, so they depend on the configuration only through x_s and x_{N(s)}.

∎

For a strictly positive distribution the converse is the Hammersley-Clifford theorem. Lauritzen (1996) and Koller and Friedman (2009) prove it. These notes use only the direction proved above.

Example 0.5 — Two pixels

Take labels \{0, 1\}, unary costs \theta_1(0) = 0, \theta_1(1) = 0.4, \theta_2(0) = 0, \theta_2(1) = 1.2, and a cost 0.8 when the two labels differ.

  • Configuration (0, 0) has energy 0.
  • Configuration (0, 1) has energy 1.2 + 0.8 = 2.
  • Configuration (1, 0) has energy 0.4 + 0.8 = 1.2.
  • Configuration (1, 1) has energy 0.4 + 1.2 = 1.6.

The mode is (0, 0). Summing the four weights \exp(-E) gives Z = 1.638. The mode has probability 0.610. A low-energy labeling can still be far from certain.

The Potts pairwise cost charges a constant \lambda when two labels differ and charges 0 when they agree. It is the edge cost used in the introduction.

Example 0.6 — The eight-pixel line

The observations are 0.1, 0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 0.9, 0.7, 0.85, 0.15. The unary cost of a label is squared error. The code enumerates all 256 binary labelings.

import itertools
y = [0.1, 0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 0.9, 0.7, 0.85, 0.15]

def data(x):
    return sum((y[i] - x[i]) ** 2 for i in range(8))

def energy(x, lam):
    cuts = sum(x[i] != x[i + 1] for i in range(7))
    return data(x) + lam * cuts

def best(lam):
    return min(itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=8), key=lambda x: energy(x, lam))

x0, x1 = best(0), best(1)
print(round(data(x0), 3))
print(round(data(x1), 3))
print(x0)
print(x1)

The fence prints 0.275 and 1.575. The first labeling is (0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0). The second, for Potts cost 1, is (0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1). The spike at 0.8 is removed. The pixel at 0.15 is pulled onto the right-hand step. The data cost rises by 1.3, and the number of flips falls from 4 to 1.

MAP and sampling answer different questions. Here the mode has probability 0.610 already on two pixels, so other labelings still carry mass. Chapter 1 computes marginals. It does not only hunt the mode.

Key 0.1 — Gibbs form

p(x) = \frac{\exp(-E(x))}{Z}.

A mode of p is a minimizer of E.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A pairwise energy adds one table per node and one table per edge.
  • For a Gibbs distribution, maximum probability and minimum energy are the same problem.
  • The conditional distribution of one node depends on the configuration only through its neighbours.
  • Listing states to get Z is reserved for tiny graphs. A tree needs the recursion of ch. 1.

1. Belief propagation

On a tree you can sum configurations without listing them. Belief propagation pushes that sum along the edges. This chapter treats pairwise models. A larger factor is the same sum, taken over the scope of that factor.

Write \phi_s(k) = \exp(-\theta_s(k)) and \psi_{st}(k, \ell) = \exp(-\theta_{st}(k, \ell)). These factors are positive. Multiplying them and dividing by Z recovers p.

Definition 1.1 — Sum-product message

The message from a neighbour t into s is a function of the label of s.

m_{t \to s}(x_s) = \sum_{x_t} \psi_{st}(x_s, x_t)\, \phi_t(x_t) \prod_{u \in N(t)\setminus\{s\}} m_{u \to t}(x_t).

The belief at s is

b_s(x_s) \propto \phi_s(x_s) \prod_{t \in N(s)} m_{t \to s}(x_s).

On a chain, each product over neighbours contains at most one incoming message. You send messages from the left end to the right end, then from the right end back to the left end. Two sweeps finish the schedule.

Theorem 1.2 — Exact marginals on a tree

If G is a tree, then after one inward pass and one outward pass from any root, the normalized belief b_s equals the marginal of X_s under p.

Proof. Root the tree at an arbitrary node. A leaf t attached to s has an empty product in Definition 1.1. Its message is the exact sum of its own factor against the edge factor.

Suppose every child of t has already sent the exact sum of its subtree. The message from t to its parent multiplies those sums by \phi_t and by the edge factor, then sums out x_t. That is the exact sum of the subtree.

After the inward pass, the root belief multiplies the root factor by every subtree sum, so it is the root marginal. The outward pass repeats the same multiplication at every node, using the message that arrives from the parent. Each belief is therefore the corresponding marginal.

∎

The proof is the same dynamic program as the forward-backward algorithm on a chain. A loop breaks the induction, because a node can no longer be summed after its children. Chapter 4 shows a triangle where a different relaxation has a gap. Sum-product on that triangle is not covered by Theorem 1.2.

Example 1.3 — A chain of three binary nodes

Take factors \phi_1 = (1, 1/2), \phi_2 = (1, 1) and \phi_3 = (1, 1/4). The edge factor equals 1 when the two labels agree and equals 1/2 when they differ.

These factors are \exp(-\theta). Node 1 pays unary costs 0 and \log 2. Node 2 pays 0 and 0. Node 3 pays 0 and \log 4. An edge pays \log 2 on a disagreement.

  • The message m_{1 \to 2} equals (5/4, 1). For label 0 at node 2 the sum is 1 \cdot 1 + (1/2) \cdot (1/2) = 5/4.
  • The message m_{2 \to 3} equals (7/4, 13/8).
  • The message m_{3 \to 2} equals (9/8, 3/4), and m_{2 \to 1} equals (3/2, 21/16).
  • The normalized belief at node 1 is (16/23, 7/23). At node 2 it is (15/23, 8/23). At node 3 it is (56/69, 13/69).

The unnormalized belief at the middle node sums to 69/32, which is also Z. On a tree the messages return the partition function as well as the marginals.

Key 1.1 — What a message stores

A message m_{t \to s} is the sum of one subtree, not a probability. Normalize only when you read a marginal from a belief.

If you normalize too early, the product of beliefs is not the joint distribution. The joint on a tree is the product of the node and edge factors, divided by Z once.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Sum-product replaces a sum over a subtree by a message, one label at a time.
  • On a tree, one inward pass and one outward pass give exact marginals.
  • On a chain of three binary nodes the marginals are 16/23, 15/23 and 56/69.
  • A loop is outside Theorem 1.2. Later chapters bound that case instead of summing it.

2. Graph cuts for binary and multi-label energies

Binary labels turn some energies into cuts in a graph with two terminals. The cut is exact when every edge cost is submodular. Multi-label Potts energies reuse that fact one label at a time.

Call the labels 0 and 1. Identify label 0 with the source side of a cut and label 1 with the sink side.

Definition 2.1 — Binary submodularity

A pairwise table \theta_{st} is submodular when

\theta_{st}(0,0) + \theta_{st}(1,1) \le \theta_{st}(0,1) + \theta_{st}(1,0).

A Potts table with cost \lambda \ge 0 on a disagreement and cost 0 on an agreement meets the test, because 0 + 0 \le \lambda + \lambda. A table that rewards disagreement can fail it.

Theorem 2.2 — Graph representability

A binary pairwise energy equals the cost of an s-t cut with nonnegative capacities, up to a constant, if and only if every pairwise table is submodular.

Proof. Each directed edge of capacity c \ge 0 contributes c when its tail is on the source side and its head is on the sink side, and contributes 0 otherwise. That function of the two labels is submodular. A sum of submodular functions is submodular. A non-submodular pairwise table therefore cannot be a cut cost.

For the converse, unary costs are terminal edges. After shifting by a constant, put capacity \theta_s(1) on the edge from the source to node s.

Put capacity \theta_s(0) on the edge from that node to the sink. One of the two can be set to 0 by subtracting \min(\theta_s(0), \theta_s(1)) from both.

The cut then pays \theta_s(x_s) up to that constant.

A Potts term of cost \lambda is an undirected edge of capacity \lambda between its two nodes. In a directed implementation both directions receive capacity \lambda. A cut separates the nodes in only one direction, so it pays \lambda exactly when the labels differ.

Kolmogorov and Zabih (2004) give the capacities for a general submodular table. The constant that appears when a terminal weight would be negative does not change the minimizing cut.

∎

Representability means that every labeling, not only the best one, has cut cost equal to its energy up to one global constant. A minimum cut is then a minimum-energy labeling. Boykov and Kolmogorov (2004) compare implementations of the minimum cut. Any correct maximum-flow algorithm is enough for the theorem.

Proposition 2.3 — One Potts expansion does not raise the energy

Let the pairwise cost be \lambda \ge 0 times the indicator that two labels differ, now with more than two labels. An alpha-expansion lets every node keep its current label or switch to a fixed label \alpha. The binary energy of that move is submodular, and a minimum cut does not increase E.

Proof. Write y_s = 1 if node s switches to \alpha, and y_s = 0 if it keeps its current label c_s.

The pairwise cost of the pair (y_s, y_t) is \lambda times an indicator that the resulting labels disagree.

The sum of the keep-keep cost and the switch-switch cost equals \lambda when c_s \ne c_t, and equals 0 when c_s = c_t.

Switching both nodes to \alpha costs 0 on that edge. Each mixed move costs either 0 or \lambda, so the mixed sum is at least as large. The induced binary table is submodular, and Theorem 2.2 supplies a cut.

The all-keep move y = 0 is one binary configuration. Its energy is the energy of the current labeling. The minimizing move cannot be worse.

∎

Boykov, Veksler and Zabih (2001) iterate these moves over every label. Each iteration is one minimum cut. The energy is nonincreasing and the label set is finite, so the iteration stops. The labeling it returns is minimal among expansions. It need not be a global minimum when more than two labels are present.

Example 2.4 — One submodular edge

Take \theta_s(0) = 0.3, \theta_s(1) = 1, \theta_t(0) = 0.8, \theta_t(1) = 0.2, and Potts cost 1.2.

  • Labels (0, 0) have energy 0.3 + 0.8 = 1.1.
  • Labels (0, 1) have energy 0.3 + 0.2 + 1.2 = 1.7.
  • Labels (1, 0) have energy 1 + 0.8 + 1.2 = 3.
  • Labels (1, 1) have energy 1 + 0.2 = 1.2.

The minimum is 1.1 at labels (0, 0). The terminal capacities are 1 and 0.3 at the first node, and 0.2 and 0.8 at the second. Both directions between the nodes have capacity 1.2. The source-side cut for labels (0, 0) pays the two edges into the sink and pays 1.1.

Example 2.5 — Expansion on three nodes

Three nodes form a triangle with Potts cost 1. Unary costs for labels 0, 1, 2 are (0.2, 1.5, 1.4) at the first node, (1.3, 0.4, 1.2) at the second, and (1.1, 0.6, 0.3) at the third.

The constant labeling (0, 0, 0) has energy 2.6, all of it unary, because every pair agrees. One expansion toward label 1 can move every node. The labeling (1, 1, 1) has unary cost 1.5 + 0.4 + 0.6, which equals 2.5, and no Potts cost. That move is better, and it is the global minimum on this triangle. The third node prefers label 2 by its unary table, but taking label 2 while the others take label 1 pays two Potts edges and is worse.

Key 2.1 — The inequality to test

\theta(0,0) + \theta(1,1) \le \theta(0,1) + \theta(1,0).

If any edge fails this test, do not build a cut for that energy.

A binary table with \theta(0,0) = \theta(1,1) = 0 and \theta(0,1) = \theta(1,0) = -0.1 fails the test. The two nodes want to disagree. Theorem 2.2 says no nonnegative cut represents that table. Kolmogorov and Rother (2007) survey partial-optimality methods for energies that are not submodular. Those methods are outside the exact claim of this chapter.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Binary submodular pairwise energies are minimum cuts.
  • A Potts term is an undirected edge whose capacity is the disagreement cost.
  • One alpha-expansion is a binary submodular problem, and it cannot raise the energy.
  • The three-node move in Example 2.5 drops the energy from 2.6 to 2.5.

3. Tree-reweighted message passing

Belief propagation is exact on a tree and is not justified on a loop. A convex combination of trees still has a computable log partition function. Averaging those values upper-bounds the log partition function of the loopy model.

Definition 3.1 — Tree mixture

Let \rho be a probability distribution on the spanning trees of G. For each tree T in the support, let \theta^{T} be a parameter vector of a pairwise energy on that tree. The mixture represents \theta when

\theta = \sum_{T} \rho(T)\, \theta^{T}.

On a triangle there are three spanning trees, each omitting one edge. The uniform weight is \rho(T) = 1/3. If every original edge has disagreement cost 1, give cost 3/2 to each edge that a tree keeps, and cost 0 to the omitted edge. Each edge is kept by two of the three trees, so the average cost is (2/3) \cdot (3/2) = 1. The mixture represents the original parameters.

Proposition 3.2 — The log partition function is convex

Let E_\theta(x) be linear in the parameter vector \theta, and let Z(\theta) = \sum_x \exp(-E_\theta(x)). Then \log Z is a convex function of \theta.

Proof. Along any line \theta + t v, the second derivative of \log Z equals the variance, under the Gibbs distribution at \theta + t v, of the linear form E_v.

A variance is nonnegative. A function with nonnegative second derivative along every line is convex.

∎

Proposition 3.3 — Jensen bound for a tree mixture

If \theta = \sum_T \rho(T)\, \theta^{T}, then

\log Z(\theta) \le \sum_{T} \rho(T)\, \log Z(\theta^{T}).

Each tree term on the right is computed by the belief propagation of ch. 1.

Proof. Proposition 3.2 says that \log Z is convex. Jensen’s inequality applied to the finite average \sum_T \rho(T)\, \theta^{T} is the display above.

A spanning tree is a tree, so Theorem 1.2 computes \log Z(\theta^{T}) from the sum of the unnormalized beliefs.

∎

Example 3.4 — Uniform trees on a triangle

Take three binary nodes, unary costs 0, and disagreement cost 1 on every edge. Two configurations have energy 0, namely the two constant labelings. The other six have energy 2. Hence

Z = 2 + 6 \exp(-2). 

import itertools
import math

def logZ(scale):
    total = 0.0
    edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (0, 2)]
    for bits in itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=3):
        e = 0.0
        for k, (i, j) in enumerate(edges):
            e += scale[k] * (bits[i] != bits[j])
        total += math.exp(-e)
    return math.log(total)

full = logZ([1.0, 1.0, 1.0])
trees = []
for drop in range(3):
    scale = [1.5, 1.5, 1.5]
    scale[drop] = 0.0
    trees.append(logZ(scale))
bound = sum(trees) / 3.0
print(round(full, 3))
print(round(bound, 3))
print(round(bound - full, 3))

The fence prints 1.034, then 1.096, then 0.062. The bound sits above \log Z by 0.062 on this attractive triangle. The gap is small because the model wants labels to agree, and a tree already captures much of that preference.

The bound in Proposition 3.3 controls the sum that defines Z. Maximum a posteriori inference asks for the minimum of E instead. Those are different numbers.

Wainwright, Jaakkola and Willsky (2005) decompose a distribution into a convex combination of trees and bound the log probability of a maximum a posteriori labeling. The bound is tight when the trees share an optimal configuration, and that shared configuration is then optimal for the original model.

Kolmogorov (2006) works directly with the energy. His sequential tree-reweighted updates produce a lower bound on the minimum energy, and he proves that the bound does not decrease. The fixed points satisfy a weak tree-agreement condition. The proof is in that paper. Chapter 4 writes the linear program that this lower bound cannot exceed.

Key 3.1 — Which way the inequality points

\log Z(\theta) \le \sum_T \rho(T)\, \log Z(\theta^{T}).

This is an upper bound on the log partition function. It is not by itself a lower bound on the minimum energy.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • A tree mixture represents \theta when the average of the tree parameters equals \theta.
  • Convexity of \log Z turns that average into an upper bound.
  • On the attractive triangle the bound is 1.096 and \log Z is 1.034.
  • Kolmogorov (2006) gives a separate, nondecreasing lower bound on the minimum energy.

4. Dual decomposition and linear programming relaxations

The minimum of a pairwise energy is an integer linear program. One variable per node and label picks the label. One variable per edge and pair of labels picks the pair. Replacing the integer constraints by local consistency gives a linear program. The program is the relaxation studied by Wainwright, Jaakkola and Willsky (2005) and, under the name LP-S, by Kumar, Kolmogorov and Torr (2009).

Definition 4.1 — Local polytope

A collection of unary marginals \mu_s and pairwise marginals \mu_{st} lies in the local polytope when the following hold.

\sum_{k} \mu_s(k) = 1, \qquad \mu_s(k) \ge 0.

\sum_{\ell} \mu_{st}(k, \ell) = \mu_s(k), \qquad \mu_{st}(k, \ell) \ge 0.

The same agreement is required when the sum is taken over the first argument of \mu_{st}.

Every labeling produces a point in this polytope. Put mass 1 on the chosen labels and mass 0 elsewhere. The converse is false. A point of the local polytope need not be the marginal of any single labeling.

Definition 4.2 — LP relaxation

The linear programming relaxation minimizes the expected energy

\sum_{s,k} \theta_s(k)\, \mu_s(k) + \sum_{\{s,t\}} \sum_{k,\ell} \theta_{st}(k, \ell)\, \mu_{st}(k, \ell)

over the local polytope. Its value is at most the minimum of E, because every labeling is a feasible point.

Proposition 4.3 — Weak duality for edge slaves

Put one slave on each edge, and give each endpoint a copied label. Let multipliers on the copies of a node sum to 0 for every label. The sum of the slave minima is at most E(x) for every configuration x.

Proof. Fix a configuration x. Give each slave the two labels that x assigns to its endpoints.

The multipliers along those copies sum to 0 at every node, by the hypothesis on the multipliers. The sum of the slave costs of these copies is therefore exactly E(x).

Each slave minimum is at most the cost of this particular copy. Summing over slaves yields a value at most E(x).

∎

Komodakis, Paragios and Tziritas (2011) maximize this dual by a projected subgradient method. A slave on a tree is a minimum-energy problem of the kind solved in ch. 1 and ch. 2. The dual value is a lower bound. It cannot exceed the linear program of Definition 4.2 when the slaves are the edges of the graph.

Example 4.4 — A frustrated triangle

Three binary nodes, and energy equal to the number of edges whose endpoints agree. Any labeling uses only two labels on three nodes, so at least one edge agrees. The minimum energy is 1. The labeling (0, 0, 1) achieves it.

The following marginals lie in the local polytope.

  • Each unary marginal equals 1/2.
  • Each pairwise marginal puts mass 0 on (0, 0) and on (1, 1).
  • Each pairwise marginal puts mass 1/2 on (0, 1) and on (1, 0).

Agreement of an edge has probability 0, so the expected energy is 0. The linear program is feasible at value 0. The integer minimum is 1. The gap is 1 on three nodes.

At zero multipliers, each edge slave pays 0 by choosing two different labels. Proposition 4.3 says this dual value is a lower bound. It equals 0, the same number as the linear program. No update of the multipliers can push the bound up to 1, because the dual of this edge decomposition cannot exceed the linear program.

Kumar, Kolmogorov and Torr (2009) compare this linear program with a quadratic relaxation and a second-order cone relaxation. They prove that the linear program is at least as tight as those two, and strictly tighter on some energies. The triangle above is the kind of gap a tighter constraint has to cut off. Adding constraints is the subject of ch. 8.

Key 4.1 — What the relaxation guarantees

The linear program returns a number less than or equal to the minimum energy. Equality is a certificate that the labeling you recovered is optimal. A gap is not a coding error.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • The local polytope keeps nonnegative marginals that agree on single nodes.
  • Minimizing the expected energy there lower-bounds the minimum of E.
  • Edge slaves with multipliers that sum to zero give the same style of lower bound.
  • On the frustrated triangle the bound is 0 and the minimum energy is 1.

5. Causality and interventions

The energies above describe how labels co-occur. A causal question asks what would change if you set a label, rather than watched it. This chapter uses a directed acyclic graph and one intervention formula. Chapter 6 returns to the observational factorization and to learning it from counts.

Definition 5.1 — Structural assignment

A structural causal model gives each variable X_j a function of its parents and of a noise variable. The noises are independent of each other. The graph is a directed acyclic graph, with an arrow from each parent to X_j.

The observational distribution is the distribution of the variables when every assignment runs as written. Conditioning on X_j = x restricts that distribution to the outcomes where X_j happened to equal x.

Definition 5.2 — Intervention

The intervention \mathrm{do}(X_j = x) replaces the assignment of X_j by the constant x. Arrows into X_j are deleted. Every other assignment is left as it was.

Write p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) for the probability of Y = y after that replacement. It is not the same object as p(y \mid x).

Theorem 5.3 — Adjustment on a confounder

Suppose the graph consists of the arrows Z \to X, Z \to Y and X \to Y, and of no other arrows. Then

p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \sum_{z} p(y \mid x, z)\, p(z).

Proof. Under the intervention, the assignment of X no longer reads Z. The marginal of Z is unchanged, because the assignment of Z was not edited.

The assignment of Y still reads X and Z. Its conditional distribution given X and Z is therefore the observational conditional p(y \mid x, z).

The law of total probability, applied to the intervened model, sums that conditional against p(z).

∎

This identity is one case of the back-door adjustment formula. Pearl (2009) states the general criterion. The proof above covers the confounder drawn in the theorem, which is the graph used in the example and in Exercise 8.

Example 5.4 — A numerical confounder

Let P(Z = 1) = 1/2. Let P(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) = 0.8 and P(X = 1 \mid Z = 0) = 0.2.

The conditional probability of Y = 1 is 0.9 when (X, Z) = (1, 1), and 0.6 when (X, Z) = (0, 1). It is 0.4 when (X, Z) = (1, 0), and 0.1 when (X, Z) = (0, 0).

  • Then P(X = 1) = 0.5 \cdot 0.8 + 0.5 \cdot 0.2 = 0.5.
  • And P(Y = 1, X = 1) = 0.9 \cdot 0.4 + 0.4 \cdot 0.1 = 0.4, so P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = 0.80.
  • Similarly, P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = 0.20. The observational gap is 0.60.
  • Theorem 5.3 at X = 1 equals 0.5 \cdot 0.9 + 0.5 \cdot 0.4, which is 0.65.
  • The same theorem at X = 0 equals 0.35. The causal gap is 0.30.

The observational gap is twice the causal gap. The extra association is Z, which pushes both X and Y up. Conditioning on X keeps that path. The intervention deletes it.

Key 5.1 — The sum to compute

p(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \sum_{z} p(y \mid x, z)\, p(z)

on the confounder graph of Theorem 5.3. The sum uses the marginal of Z, not the conditional of Z given X.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • An intervention replaces one structural assignment by a constant and deletes the arrows into that variable.
  • On the confounder graph, the back-door sum averages the observational conditional of Y against p(z).
  • In Example 5.4 the observational gap is 0.60 and the causal gap is 0.30.
  • The formula needs the graph. It does not by itself say how to learn the arrows. That is ch. 6 and ch. 8.

6. Bayesian networks

A Bayesian network factorizes a discrete distribution along a directed acyclic graph. With complete observations, each conditional table is a ratio of counts. Choosing the arrows is a different problem, and the counts do not always orient an edge.

Definition 6.1 — Bayesian network

A distribution factorizes according to a directed acyclic graph when

p(x) = \prod_{i} p(x_i \mid x_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}).

The parents \mathrm{pa}(i) are the nodes with an arrow into i. If i has no parents, the factor is the marginal of X_i.

Proposition 6.2 — A chain and a collider

On the chain X \to Z \to Y, every factorization satisfies X \perp Y \mid Z. On the collider X \to Z \leftarrow Y, independence of X and Y in the prior can fail after conditioning on Z.

Proof. On the chain the joint is p(x)\, p(z \mid x)\, p(y \mid z). Divide by p(z). The identity p(x \mid z)\, p(z) = p(x)\, p(z \mid x) leaves p(x \mid z)\, p(y \mid z). The product form is the conditional independence.

On the collider, let X and Y be independent fair bits, and let Z be the Boolean or of those two bits.

Then P(Z = 1) = 3/4. The conditional probability P(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) equals 2/3.

Given Z = 1 and Y = 1, the conditional probability of X = 1 is 1/2. Given Z = 1 and Y = 0, it equals 1. Conditioning on the or makes the two bits dependent.

∎

The general rule that reads every independence off the arrows is d-separation. Koller and Friedman (2009) and Pearl (1988) treat it. The two graphs in Proposition 6.2 are the patterns used in ch. 5. A chain is a path that conditioning blocks. A collider is a path that conditioning opens.

Proposition 6.3 — Maximum likelihood by counts

Assume n complete observations and a fixed directed acyclic graph. A maximum likelihood table is

\hat p(x_i \mid \pi) = \frac{N(x_i, \pi)}{N(\pi)}

whenever the parent configuration \pi appears at least once. Here N denotes a count in the sample.

Proof. The log likelihood is a sum over nodes. For a fixed parent configuration, that node contributes \sum_k N(k, \pi)\, \log p(k \mid \pi), and the probabilities of one row sum to 1.

The unique maximizer of \sum_k n_k \log p_k on the simplex is p_k = n_k / n when n = \sum_k n_k > 0. The same identity applies to each row.

∎

Rows that never appear are not determined by the likelihood. A prior count, or a later regularizer, has to fill them. The formula above is only the complete-data maximum likelihood estimator.

Definition 6.4 — BIC

For a graph G with d(G) free parameters and n observations,

\mathrm{BIC}(G) = \ell(G) - \frac{d(G)}{2} \log n,

where \ell(G) is the maximized log likelihood. For a binary node with q parent configurations, that node contributes q free parameters.

Example 6.5 — Four complete pairs

The sample of (A, B) is (0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1), (1, 1). So n = 4.

  • The empty graph has d = 2. Its maximum likelihood fit uses P(A = 1) = 1/2 and P(B = 1) = 3/4. The log likelihood is -5.022.
  • The graph A \to B has d = 3. The fit uses P(A = 1) = 1/2, P(B = 1 \mid A = 0) = 1/2 and P(B = 1 \mid A = 1) = 1. The log likelihood is -4.159.
  • Then \mathrm{BIC} equals -6.408 for the empty graph and -6.238 for A \to B. The edge wins by 0.170.
  • The reversed graph B \to A has the same number of free parameters and the same maximum likelihood, because both graphs can represent any joint of two binary variables. The two directions have the same BIC. The counts do not orient the edge.

Chickering (2002) proves that a greedy search over Markov equivalence classes is consistent when the score is decomposable, score-equivalent and consistent, and the distribution is faithful to some directed acyclic graph. BIC has those score properties for multinomial tables. The proof of consistency is in that paper. Example 6.5 is only an illustration. Four rows are too few for an asymptotic claim, and the tie between the two directions is the point.

Key 6.1 — The count ratio

\hat p(k \mid \pi) = \frac{N(k, \pi)}{N(\pi)}.

Use it on rows that the sample actually contains.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • A Bayesian network is a product of conditional tables along a directed acyclic graph.
  • A chain gives a conditional independence. A collider can destroy an independence.
  • Complete-data maximum likelihood is a ratio of counts.
  • On the four-row sample, BIC prefers an edge to the empty graph and does not choose a direction.

7. Deep learning in graphical models

A convolutional network can emit the unary costs. The pairwise costs can stay a Potts table, or a small table with its own parameters. Inference is then the discrete problem of ch. 2 to ch. 4, and the training loop has to differentiate through an approximate minimizer or through approximate marginals.

Definition 7.1 — Neural unary costs

Let o denote the observation, for example an image. A network produces a vector f_s(o) at each node. The unary energy of label k is the negative logit

\theta_s(k) = - f_s(o)_k.

The conditional distribution of the labels given o is the Gibbs distribution of the full energy, unary plus pairwise.

The partition function of that conditional distribution depends on the network weights, because the unary costs do. A gradient of \log Z is an expectation under the conditional distribution. On a grid you replace that expectation by an approximation. Mean field is the approximation developed here.

Definition 7.2 — Fully factorized trial distribution

Mean field uses a trial distribution q(x) = \prod_s q_s(x_s). The coordinates q_s are chosen to minimize \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p), where p is the Gibbs conditional distribution of the labels.

Proposition 7.3 — One coordinate update

Freeze every factor of q except q_s. For a Potts pairwise cost \lambda times a disagreement, the minimizing q_s is the softmax of the expected energy

\varepsilon_s(k) = \theta_s(k) + \sum_{t \in N(s)} \lambda \,(1 - q_t(k)).

The update does not increase the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

Proof. With p \propto \exp(-E), the divergence \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) equals -H(q) + \mathbb{E}_q[E] + \log Z. The term \log Z does not depend on q.

For a product distribution the entropy H(q) is a sum of the entropies of the factors. With every factor but q_s held fixed, the objective in that factor is the sum below, up to a constant.

\sum_k q_s(k)\, \log q_s(k) + \sum_k q_s(k)\, \varepsilon_s(k).

The minimizer of that function on the simplex is q_s(k) \propto \exp(-\varepsilon_s(k)). Any other q_s gives a larger or equal objective, so the divergence does not increase.

For a Potts term, the expected cost of assigning label k at s, with the neighbour t drawn from q_t, equals \lambda times 1 - q_t(k). Summing neighbours gives the display.

∎

Zheng, Jayasumana, Romera-Paredes, Vineet, Su, Du, Huang and Torr (2015) repeat this update a fixed number of times and differentiate through the repeats. The pairwise weights in their network follow the fully connected model of Krahenbuhl and Koltun (2011), with a Gaussian kernel on position and color. Chen, Papandreou, Kokkinos, Murphy and Yuille (2018) use the same split on a segmentation network. A deep net proposes unaries. A dense conditional random field charges the pairwise term.

A fixed number of updates is a composition of softmax maps. It is a different function from the fixed point of the same maps. Both can be useful. They do not return the same marginals on the example below.

Example 7.4 — Two nodes, one pass and the exact marginal

Take labels \{0, 1\}, Potts cost \lambda = 1, unary energies \theta_s = (0, 0.5) and \theta_t = (1, 0). Start both factors at (1/2, 1/2).

import math
import numpy

lam = 1.0
th_s = numpy.array([0.0, 0.5])
th_t = numpy.array([1.0, 0.0])

def update(th, q_other):
    eps = numpy.array([th[k] + lam * (1.0 - q_other[k]) for k in (0, 1)])
    q = numpy.exp(-eps)
    q = q / q.sum()
    return q

qt = numpy.array([0.5, 0.5])
qs = update(th_s, qt)
qt = update(th_t, qs)
qs2 = update(th_s, qt)
print(round(float(qs[0]), 3))
print(round(float(qs2[0]), 3))

Es = {(0, 0): 1.0, (0, 1): 1.0, (1, 0): 2.5, (1, 1): 0.5}
Z = sum(math.exp(-e) for e in Es.values())
p0 = sum(math.exp(-Es[(0, t)]) for t in (0, 1)) / Z
print(round(p0, 3))

The fence prints 0.622, then 0.535, then 0.517. The first number is q_s(0) after updating node s from the uniform neighbour.

The second number is q_s(0) after one update of node t and a second update of node s. The third number is the exact marginal of label 0 at that node. The first pass overshoots. The second pass moves toward the marginal and does not reach it.

Key 7.1 — The mean-field softmax

q_s(k) \propto \exp(-\varepsilon_s(k)).

The vector \varepsilon_s is the unary energy plus the expected Potts cost under the current neighbour factors.

If the pairwise cost \lambda is held fixed while the network is trained, the gradient updates only the unary logits. That is a modeling choice, not a consequence of Proposition 7.3. Jointly training \lambda requires the same unrolled updates, with \lambda left in the computational graph.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • A network may supply unary costs. The conditional distribution of the labels is still a Gibbs distribution.
  • One mean-field coordinate update is a softmax of the expected energy, and it does not increase the divergence.
  • On the two-node Potts model the first update prints 0.622, against an exact marginal of 0.517.
  • A fixed number of updates is differentiable. It is not the same map as the fixed point.

8. Research directions and open problems

The eight sessions produce exact algorithms on trees and on submodular binary energies, and bounds on loopy graphs. The gaps that remain are research problems. The papers below exist. The open questions are stated only where the paper itself leaves them open, or where ch. 2 to ch. 7 already show a concrete obstruction.

When the linear program is integral

Theorem 2.2 says that a binary submodular pairwise energy is a minimum cut, so the linear program of ch. 4 is tight for that class. The certificate is the cut. Example 4.4 shows a gap of 1 on three binary nodes as soon as the energy rewards agreement on a triangle that cannot be satisfied.

Sontag, Meltzer, Globerson, Jaakkola and Collins (2008) add cluster constraints that cut off some fractional vertices of the local polytope. Which clusters to add on a large vision grid is not settled by a single rule in that paper. Kumar, Kolmogorov and Torr (2009) prove that several convex relaxations are dominated by LP-S, and then build tighter cone constraints from cycles and cliques. How large a cycle constraint can be before the solver, rather than the relaxation gap, becomes the limit, is a practical open question on the grids of ch. 2.

Higher-order costs

Potts models charge pairs. Region consistency charges a whole set of nodes when they fail to share one label. Kohli, Ladicky and Torr (2009) reduce a robust version of that cost to pairwise graph-cut moves. The reduction covers that robust consistency term. It does not cover an arbitrary table on five labels of a five-node clique. Representing a general higher-order table by a pairwise energy, with a guarantee on the minimizer, is open outside the families those reductions name.

Hidden confounding and learned variables

Chapter 6 already shows that two directions of a single edge can share a BIC score. From observations alone, a directed acyclic graph is identified at best up to a Markov equivalence class, and only under faithfulness assumptions of the kind Chickering (2002) uses. Hidden confounders enlarge the set of graphs that can produce the same observational distribution.

Spirtes, Glymour and Scheines (2000) and Peters, Janzing and Schölkopf (2017) treat discovery from observations, including some hidden-variable models. Schölkopf, Locatello, Bauer, Ke, Kalchbrenner, Goyal and Bengio (2021) list causal representation learning as open. In that problem the variables themselves are learned from a high-dimensional observation, and the graph is learned at the same time. Nothing in ch. 5 identifies those variables.

Derivatives of a discrete minimizer

Proposition 7.3 differentiates easily because a softmax is smooth. A minimum cut is not a softmax. Mensch and Blondel (2018) differentiate dynamic programming on sequences. A cut on a general vision grid is not that recursion. Domke (2013) differentiates approximate marginal inference for exponential families, which covers message-passing approximations of the kind in ch. 1 and ch. 3. Extending either argument to alpha-expansion, with a stated bound on the gradient bias, is open.

A related obstruction is the partition function of a deep energy on a large discrete grid. Definition 0.2 makes \log Z the normalizer. Chapter 3 upper-bounds it by trees. Using that bound inside a training loss, and knowing the bias of the resulting gradient, is not settled by the Jensen inequality alone.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Binary submodular energies are the class where the cut certifies a tight linear program. The frustrated triangle is a small case with a gap.
  • Robust region consistency reduces to cuts for some costs, not for an arbitrary clique table.
  • Observational scores do not orient every edge, and hidden confounders make the set of explanations larger.
  • Softmax updates differentiate. A minimum cut on a general grid does not fall under the sequence algorithms that are already differentiated.

Exercises

The ten exercises follow the session plan. A star marks a direct use of one chapter. Two stars mark a short derivation or a program. Three stars mark a question that stays open after the numerical check.

  1. ★ Two-pixel energies. Using the costs of Example 0.5, list the four energies, name the mode, and give Z and the probability of the mode to three decimals.

    Solution. The energies are 0, 2, 1.2 and 1.6. The mode is (0, 0). Then Z = 1.638 and the mode has probability 0.610.

  2. ★ A Gibbs estimate of one marginal. Three binary variables pay unary costs 0.2, -0.5 and 0.4 for label 1, and pay 0 for label 0. Neighbouring variables pay an extra 0.7 when they differ. The sampler below prints a Monte Carlo estimate of P(X_0 = 1) and then the exact value.

import itertools
import math
import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
unary = numpy.array([0.2, -0.5, 0.4])
x = rng.integers(0, 2, size=3)
burn, steps = 500, 4000
hits = 0
for t in range(burn + steps):
    s = int(rng.integers(0, 3))
    acc = numpy.zeros(2)
    for v in (0, 1):
        yv = x.copy()
        yv[s] = v
        e = float(unary @ yv)
        e += 0.7 * (yv[0] != yv[1]) + 0.7 * (yv[1] != yv[2])
        acc[v] = numpy.exp(-e)
    p1 = acc[1] / acc.sum()
    x[s] = int(rng.random() < p1)
    if t >= burn and x[0] == 1:
        hits += 1
print(round(hits / steps, 4))
Z = 0.0
mass = 0.0
for bits in itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=3):
    yv = numpy.array(bits, dtype=float)
    e = float(unary @ yv)
    e += 0.7 * (yv[0] != yv[1]) + 0.7 * (yv[1] != yv[2])
    w = math.exp(-e)
    Z += w
    if bits[0] == 1:
        mass += w
print(round(mass / Z, 4))

Solution. The fence prints 0.5135 and then 0.4806. The Monte Carlo estimate uses 4000 draws after 500 burn-in steps. The exact marginal is the second print.

  4. ★★ A tree-reweighted upper bound. Three binary nodes form a triangle. Unary coefficients and disagreement costs are drawn with the seed below. Each spanning tree drops one edge and multiplies the kept costs by 3/2. The prints are \log Z, the average of the three tree log partitions, and the gap.

import itertools
import math
import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
a = rng.normal(size=3)
b = rng.random(size=3)

def energy(x, edge_scale):
    e = float(a @ x)
    for k, (i, j) in enumerate([(0, 1), (1, 2), (0, 2)]):
        e += float(edge_scale[k]) * (x[i] != x[j])
    return e

def logZ(edge_scale):
    total = 0.0
    for bits in itertools.product([0.0, 1.0], repeat=3):
        x = numpy.array(bits)
        total += math.exp(-energy(x, edge_scale))
    return math.log(total)

logZ_full = logZ(b)
bounds = []
for drop in range(3):
    scale = b * 1.5
    scale[drop] = 0.0
    bounds.append(logZ(scale))
bound = sum(bounds) / 3.0
print(round(logZ_full, 4))
print(round(bound, 4))
print(round(bound - logZ_full, 4))

Solution. The fence prints 1.128, then 1.2079, then 0.0799. Proposition 3.3 says the third number is nonnegative. Here the gap is 0.0799.

    Solution. Three nodes and two labels force at least one agreeing edge, so the energy is at least 1, and (0, 0, 1) achieves 1. The marginals of Example 4.4 are feasible and have expected energy 0. Each edge slave at zero multipliers can disagree and pay 0, so the dual value is 0.

  2. ★★ Back-door arithmetic. Using the conditional probabilities of Example 5.4, compute P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) and P(Y = 1 \mid \mathrm{do}(X = 1)). Say which distribution of Z the second sum uses.

  3. ★★★ BIC does not orient an edge. On the sample of Example 6.5, compute both BIC scores to three decimals. Then show that B \to A matches the score of A \to B, and say what this blocks for a causal claim of the kind in ch. 5.

  4. ★★★ A fixed number of mean-field passes. From the prints in Example 7.4, compare one pass, two passes and the exact marginal at node s. Explain why unrolling five passes, as in Zheng et al. (2015), is a different function from running the updates until the factors stop moving.

The fixed point is defined by a consistency condition, and reaching it can take a data-dependent number of iterations. Those two procedures assign different numbers to q_s on this example already at the second pass.

Written exam (proposal)

This exam is a study proposal for three hours, with one handwritten sheet of notes and no computer. It is not part of the official assessment. The official assessment remains the project, the report and the defense. Three questions, scored out of 20.

  1. Conceptual question (6 points). Define a pairwise energy and the Gibbs distribution. State the local Markov property. State the hypothesis of Theorem 1.2, and what the beliefs equal under that hypothesis.

  2. Computation (8 points). (a) For Example 0.5, give the four energies and the mode. (b) Test submodularity of a Potts cost 1.2. (c) For Example 4.4, give one integer energy and the local-polytope value.

    Expected. (a) Energies 0, 2, 1.2 and 1.6, with mode (0, 0). (b) The test 0 + 0 \le 1.2 + 1.2 holds. (c) The integer energy is 1, for example at labels (0, 0, 1), and the local-polytope value is 0.

  3. Short proof (6 points). Prove Proposition 4.3. Explain in two sentences why the dual value on Example 4.4 cannot rise from 0 to the integer minimum.
