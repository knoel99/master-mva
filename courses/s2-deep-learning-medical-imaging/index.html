0. Review: deep learning and statistics for medical images

Every chapter trains a network by minimising a loss, on images that are often volumes, and then measures its performance on a finite test set. This chapter reviews the three facts behind these steps: what a loss estimates, what a convolution costs in 3D, and how precise a test-set estimate is.

0.1 Losses are negative log-likelihoods

A classifier with parameters \theta outputs a probability vector q_\theta(x) over K classes. Training minimises the empirical risk over pairs (x_i, y_i).

Definition 0.1 — Cross-entropy loss For a label y \in \{1, \dots, K\} and a predicted probability vector q, \ell(q, y) = -\log q_y .

With two classes and q = q_1, it reads -y \log q - (1 - y) \log(1 - q), the binary cross-entropy. Minimising its average is maximum likelihood for a categorical model of y given x.

Proposition 0.2 — The cross-entropy is minimised by the posterior Among all functions q of x, the expected loss \mathbb{E}[\ell(q(X), Y)] is minimised by q^\star(x) = \big(P(Y = k \mid X = x)\big)_{k = 1, \dots, K}.

Proof. Condition on X = x and write p_k = P(Y = k \mid X = x). The conditional risk is -\sum_k p_k \log q_k.

It equals the entropy of p plus the Kullback–Leibler divergence \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q), which is non-negative and vanishes only at q = p. Minimising pointwise in x gives q^\star. ∎

Two consequences recur in this course. A well-trained classifier outputs probabilities, which can be corrected when the class frequencies change (ch. 2.3) and must be checked for calibration (ch. 8.3). The squared loss is likewise minimised by the conditional mean, the basis of denoising (ch. 6).

0.2 Convolutions in two and three dimensions

Medical images are often volumes: a brain MRI has about 256^3 voxels. A 3D convolutional layer slides a kernel along three axes instead of two.

Definition 0.3 — Convolutional layer A layer with C_{\text{in}} input channels, C_{\text{out}} output channels and kernel size k in dimension d computes, for each output channel c, z_c = b_c + \sum_{c'=1}^{C_{\text{in}}} w_{c, c'} \star x_{c'}, with kernels w_{c,c'} of size k^d. It has k^d\, C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}} + C_{\text{out}} parameters.

Along each axis, an input of length n with padding p and stride s gives an output of length \Big\lfloor \frac{n + 2p - k}{s} \Big\rfloor + 1 .

The layer is equivariant to translations: shifting the input shifts the output. Stacking L layers of size 3 and stride 1 gives a receptive field of 1 + 2L voxels per axis; pooling and strides make it grow faster.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
torch.set_num_threads(1)

conv2d = nn.Conv2d(32, 64, kernel_size=3, padding=1)    # 32 -> 64 channels, 3 x 3
conv3d = nn.Conv3d(32, 64, kernel_size=3, padding=1)    # same, 3 x 3 x 3
count = lambda m: sum(p.numel() for p in m.parameters())
print("parameters  2D:", count(conv2d), "  3D:", count(conv3d))

# the 2D layer on a 128 x 128 slice (batch of 1): shape is kept by padding=1
y = conv2d(torch.zeros(1, 32, 128, 128))
print("2D output", tuple(y.shape), f"{y.numel() * 4 / 2**20:.0f} MiB in float32")
# the same layer in 3D on a 128^3 volume: 128 times more voxels
n3 = 64 * 128 ** 3
print("3D output (1, 64, 128, 128, 128)", f"{n3 * 4 / 2**20:.0f} MiB in float32")

# receptive field of L stacked 3x3 convolutions with stride 1: 1 + 2L
for L in (5, 10, 20):
    print(f"{L} layers -> receptive field {1 + 2 * L} voxels per axis")

The 3D layer has three times more parameters (55 360 against 18 496), but its activations take 512 MiB against 4 MiB for one slice. Memory, not parameters, is why 3D networks are trained on patches and small batches (ch. 4.3).

0.3 Generalisation and the test set

Networks are trained with stochastic gradient methods (SGD with momentum, Adam), regularised by weight decay, data augmentation and early stopping. Hyperparameters are chosen on a validation set. The test set is used once, at the end, to estimate the risk of the final model.

Theorem 0.4 — Hoeffding’s bound for a test error Let f be fixed before seeing the test set, and let \hat R be its error rate on n independent test cases drawn from the population, whose true error rate is R. For every t > 0, P\big(|\hat R - R| \geq t\big) \leq 2 e^{-2 n t^2}.

Proof. \hat R is the mean of n independent variables with values in [0, 1] and mean R. Hoeffding’s inequality bounds each tail by e^{-2nt^2}; a union bound over the two tails gives the factor 2. ∎

With n = 100 test patients, a 95 % guarantee needs 2 e^{-200 t^2} = 0.05, that is t = \sqrt{\log(40)/200} \approx 0.136 .

An accuracy measured on 100 patients is therefore only known to within about 14 points by this bound, and about 8 points by the normal approximation of ch. 8.2 when it is near 80 %. Medical test sets are often that small.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Cross-entropy training estimates the posterior probabilities; the squared loss estimates the conditional mean.
  • A 3D convolution has k^3 weights per channel pair and costs memory proportional to the volume: 3D networks work on patches.
  • The receptive field of a stack of 3 \times 3 convolutions grows by 2 per layer.
  • A test set of n cases gives an error rate known to within about 1/\sqrt{n}.

1. Introduction to medical imaging

A medical image is a measurement, not a photograph: each modality measures a different physical quantity, on a 3D grid with its own spacing, units and noise. This chapter explains how the main modalities form their images, how a scan is stored and preprocessed, and which tasks the rest of the course addresses.

1.1 What makes medical images different

Four features separate medical images from the natural images of computer vision.

  • Dimension: CT and MRI are volumes, sometimes with a time axis (cine cardiac MRI) or several contrasts per patient; whole-slide microscopy images have billions of pixels.
  • Physics: the intensity has a physical meaning in CT (attenuation) and PET (tracer concentration), and an arbitrary scale in MRI.
  • Data: data sets have hundreds or thousands of patients, not millions; labels come from experts, who disagree, and pathologies are rare.
  • Stakes: predictions concern patients, so validation, interpretation and reproducibility (ch. 8) are part of the method.

1.2 How images are formed

X-ray radiography and CT. An X-ray beam crossing tissue of linear attenuation coefficient \mu loses intensity at a rate proportional to itself.

Proposition 1.1 — Beer–Lambert law If dI/ds = -\mu(s)\, I along a ray, the detected intensity is I = I_0 \exp\Big(-\int_{\text{ray}} \mu(s)\, ds\Big).

Proof. Divide by I and integrate: \log I - \log I_0 = -\int \mu\, ds. ∎

A radiograph shows \log(I_0 / I), the line integral of \mu along each ray, projected on a plane. Computed tomography (CT) measures these line integrals along many directions and inverts them to recover \mu on a 3D grid (ch. 6.2).

Definition 1.2 — Hounsfield units CT intensities are reported as \mathrm{HU}(\mu) = 1000\, \frac{\mu - \mu_{\text{water}}}{\mu_{\text{water}} - \mu_{\text{air}}}, so that air is -1000 HU and water 0 HU. Fat is around -100 HU, soft tissues between 20 and 80 HU, and dense bone above 700 HU.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Hydrogen nuclei, aligned by a strong magnetic field, are excited by radio-frequency pulses and relax with tissue-dependent time constants T_1 and T_2. Magnetic field gradients encode position in the frequency and phase of the signal, so that the scanner samples the Fourier transform of the image: s(k) = \int \rho(x)\, e^{-2 i \pi \langle k, x \rangle}\, dx .

The space of k is called k-space. The weighting \rho depends on the sequence: T_1-weighted images show anatomy, T_2-weighted and FLAIR images show oedema and lesions, diffusion images show the motion of water. MRI intensities have no absolute unit and drift with a smooth multiplicative bias field.

Other modalities. Positron emission tomography (PET) counts photon pairs emitted by a radioactive tracer: its data are Poisson counts, and its images are noisy and of low resolution. Ultrasound images the echoes of sound waves, in real time, with speckle noise. Digital pathology scans stained tissue slides into RGB whole-slide images; ophthalmology and dermatology use photographs.

1.3 Volumes, voxels and world coordinates

A scan is stored as an array together with the geometry that places it in the patient. Formats such as DICOM (the clinical standard, one file per slice with a header) and NIfTI (common in research) both store this geometry.

Definition 1.3 — Voxel-to-world affine A volume is an array indexed by voxel coordinates (i, j, k) together with a 4 \times 4 affine matrix A that gives the position in millimetres of each voxel centre: \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = A \begin{pmatrix} i \\ j \\ k \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} R\, \mathrm{diag}(s) & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

Here s is the voxel spacing, R a rotation (the orientation of the axes) and t the position of the first voxel. NIfTI uses the RAS convention (axes towards Right, Anterior, Superior); DICOM uses LPS.

Example 1.4 — An anisotropic CT volume An axial CT has spacing 0.8 \times 0.8 \times 5 mm. A segmented lesion of 1000 voxels has volume 1000 \times 0.8 \times 0.8 \times 5 = 3200 mm³, that is 3.2 \text{ mL}.

A 3 \times 3 \times 3 kernel covers 2.4 \times 2.4 \times 15 mm: it mixes very different scales along the three axes. Resampling to isotropic voxels, or choosing anisotropic kernels, removes this mismatch.

The code below builds a sphere of radius 30 mm on such a grid, resamples it to 2 mm isotropic voxels, measures its volume and applies a CT soft-tissue window.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import zoom

# voxel -> world (mm) affine of a volume with spacing 0.8 x 0.8 x 5 mm (axial CT)
spacing = np.array([0.8, 0.8, 5.0])
A = np.diag(np.append(spacing, 1.0))
A[:3, 3] = [-200.0, -200.0, -100.0]                 # world position of voxel (0, 0, 0)
ijk = np.array([250, 250, 20, 1.0])
print("voxel (250, 250, 20) is at", A @ ijk, "mm")

# a synthetic volume: a sphere of radius 30 mm, 40 HU (soft tissue) in air (-1000 HU)
shape = (500, 500, 40)
grid = np.indices(shape).reshape(3, -1).T * spacing + A[:3, 3]
hu = np.where(np.linalg.norm(grid, axis=1) < 30, 40.0, -1000.0).reshape(shape)

# resample to isotropic 2 mm voxels (linear interpolation)
iso = zoom(hu, spacing / 2.0, order=1)
print("shape", hu.shape, "->", iso.shape)
vol_mm3 = (iso > -480).sum() * 2.0 ** 3
print(f"sphere volume: {vol_mm3 / 1e3:.1f} cm3 (exact {4 / 3 * np.pi * 30 ** 3 / 1e3:.1f} cm3)")

# a soft-tissue window [level 40, width 400] maps HU to [0, 1]
level, width = 40.0, 400.0
win = np.clip((iso - (level - width / 2)) / width, 0, 1)
print("window values: air", win.min(), " tissue", win.max())

The measured volume is 113.6 cm³ against 113.1 cm³ exactly: the error comes from the 5 mm slices, not from the method.

1.4 Preprocessing

Most pipelines apply the same steps before any learning.

  1. Reorientation and resampling to a common orientation and spacing, with linear interpolation for images and nearest-neighbour interpolation for label maps.
  2. Intensity normalisation: a fixed window in HU for CT; for MRI, a bias-field correction (the N4 algorithm of Tustison et al., 2010), then a z-score inside the body or brain mask, or a clipping between percentiles.
  3. Cropping and masking, for instance skull stripping in brain MRI.
  4. Spatial normalisation to a template by registration (ch. 5), when the analysis compares anatomies across subjects.

Data augmentation then generates plausible variants during training: flips (if the anatomy allows), small rotations and scalings, elastic deformations, intensity and contrast changes, simulated noise and bias fields. Libraries such as TorchIO and MONAI implement these operations for 3D volumes.

2. Classification

Classification assigns one label to an image or to a patient: diseased or healthy, a disease subtype, a risk group. This chapter adapts convolutional classifiers to medical data, then studies the two statistical issues that dominate in practice: the class frequencies and the choice of a metric.

2.1 Architectures for 2D and 3D images

For 2D images (radiographs, fundus photographs, dermoscopy, pathology patches), the standard choice is a 2D residual network or one of its successors, often pretrained on ImageNet. For volumes there are three options.

  • 3D networks take the whole volume, or a large crop, as input. They see the 3D context but are limited by memory (ch. 0.2) and need more data.
  • 2D networks on slices classify each slice, then aggregate the slice scores by averaging or by a small attention module. They reuse 2D pretrained weights.
  • 2.5D networks feed a few neighbouring slices, or three orthogonal slices, as input channels.

Images over time. Cine cardiac MRI and follow-up scans add a time axis. The frames can be stacked as input channels, convolved over space and time together, or encoded one by one and then combined by a recurrent or attention module across time steps. Several contrasts of the same patient (T1, T2, FLAIR) are stacked as channels once they are registered (ch. 5).

Transfer learning. Initialising from ImageNet weights then fine-tuning is the default for 2D images. Raghu et al. (2019) found, on large retinal and chest X-ray tasks, that pretraining brought little accuracy over well-chosen small networks trained from scratch, but sped up convergence. Its benefit is largest with small data sets.

2.2 Sensitivity, specificity and the ROC curve

A binary classifier outputs a score S and decides “positive” when S > t. The clinical vocabulary names the error rates.

Definition 2.1 — Clinical metrics With TP, FP, TN, FN the counts of true and false positives and negatives:

  • sensitivity (recall, true positive rate) = \mathrm{TP} / (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN});
  • specificity = \mathrm{TN} / (\mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP}), and the false positive rate is 1 - specificity;
  • positive predictive value (precision) = \mathrm{TP} / (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}).

The ROC curve plots sensitivity against the false positive rate as t varies; the AUC is the area under it.

Theorem 2.2 — The AUC is a probability of correct ranking Let S^+ and S^- be the scores of an independent positive and negative case, with continuous distributions. Then \mathrm{AUC} = P\big(S^+ > S^-\big).

Proof. Write \mathrm{TPR}(t) = P(S^+ > t) and \mathrm{FPR}(t) = P(S^- > t), and let f^- be the density of S^-.

The ROC curve is parametrised by t, running from +\infty to -\infty, and d\,\mathrm{FPR} = -f^-(t)\, dt. Hence \mathrm{AUC} = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} P(S^+ > t)\, f^-(t)\, dt = P(S^+ > S^-), by conditioning on S^- = t and using independence. ∎

With ties, the AUC counts each tied pair as one half: this is the Mann–Whitney statistic divided by the number of pairs. The AUC does not depend on the threshold or on the prevalence, which makes it a good summary of ranking quality, but not of the decisions taken at one threshold.

2.3 Prevalence and class imbalance

Diseases are rare in screening populations and over-represented in research data sets. The predictive value depends on the prevalence \pi of the disease, by Bayes’ rule.

Example 2.3 — A good test with a poor predictive value A test with sensitivity 0.9 and specificity 0.9 is used where the prevalence is 1 %. Among 1000 people, 9 of the 10 diseased are detected and 99 of the 990 healthy are false alarms. The positive predictive value is \frac{9}{9 + 99} \approx 0.083 .

At a prevalence of 20 %, the same test has a predictive value of 180 / (180 + 80) \approx 0.69.

A classifier trained with cross-entropy estimates the posterior of its training distribution (Proposition 0.2). When the class frequencies differ at deployment while the images of each class do not change (a prior shift), the probabilities can be corrected.

Proposition 2.4 — Correcting probabilities for a change of prevalence Let q(x) be the posterior probability of the positive class under training prevalence \pi_0, and assume that the class-conditional densities p(x \mid y) are the same at deployment, where the prevalence is \pi. The deployment posterior is p(x) = \frac{\frac{\pi}{\pi_0}\, q(x)}{\frac{\pi}{\pi_0}\, q(x) + \frac{1 - \pi}{1 - \pi_0}\, (1 - q(x))}.

Proof. By Bayes’ rule, the posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio times the prior odds, under both distributions. The likelihood ratio p(x \mid 1) / p(x \mid 0) is common to both, so the posterior odds are multiplied by the ratio of prior odds. Solving for the probability gives the formula. ∎

A network trained on balanced data (\pi_0 = 1/2) that outputs q = 0.9 means, at \pi = 1\%, a probability of \frac{0.01 \times 0.9}{0.01 \times 0.9 + 0.99 \times 0.1} \approx 0.083, the number of Example 2.3. Weighting the loss of class k by w_k is equivalent to training under a prevalence proportional to w_k \pi_k, so the same correction undoes class weights. Weighted losses and oversampling of the rare class change the effective prior. The focal loss of ch. 3.3 is not a prior change, but it also distorts the outputs, which must then be recalibrated (ch. 8.3) before being read as probabilities.

2.4 Weak labels and multiple-instance learning

A whole-slide pathology image has about 10^{10} pixels and a single diagnosis. It is cut into N tiles, and only the label of the whole bag of tiles is known.

Definition 2.5 — Multiple-instance learning A bag X = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\} with unknown instance labels y_n \in \{0, 1\} has label Y = \max_n y_n: it is positive when at least one instance is. A model scores each instance with embeddings h_n = \phi(x_n) and pools them with a function that does not depend on the order of the instances.

Ilse, Tomczak and Welling (2018) pool with attention weights computed by a small network with parameters w and V: z = \sum_{n=1}^N a_n h_n, \qquad a_n = \frac{\exp\big(w^\top \tanh(V h_n)\big)}{\sum_{m} \exp\big(w^\top \tanh(V h_m)\big)} .

The pooled vector z is invariant to permutations of the tiles, and the weights a_n show which tiles drove the decision. Campanella et al. (2019) trained weakly supervised multiple-instance models (max-pooling over tiles, then a recurrent aggregator) on more than 40 000 slides, with diagnoses as the only labels.

2.5 The radiomics baseline and a worked example

Before deep learning, and still today in many clinical studies, classification used radiomics (Lambin et al., 2012): hand-crafted features of a segmented region (volume, shape, intensity statistics, co-occurrence texture), followed by a penalised logistic regression or a random forest. On small data sets this baseline is hard to beat, and every deep model should be compared with it.

The code trains a small CNN to detect a faint blob (a “lesion”) in a textured background, at a prevalence of 30 %, with a weighted loss. It compares the CNN with a hand-crafted feature, the maximum of the smoothed image, and checks Theorem 2.2.

import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)

def make(n, prevalence=0.3, size=24):
    """Smooth random 'tissue' texture; positives carry a faint blob."""
    x = gaussian_filter(rng.standard_normal((n, size, size)), sigma=(0, 1.5, 1.5)) * 3
    y = (rng.random(n) < prevalence).astype(np.float32)
    ii, jj = np.mgrid[:size, :size]
    for k in np.flatnonzero(y):
        ci, cj = rng.integers(5, size - 5, 2)
        x[k] += 1.5 * np.exp(-((ii - ci) ** 2 + (jj - cj) ** 2) / (2 * 2.0 ** 2))
    x += 0.3 * rng.standard_normal(x.shape)
    return torch.tensor(x[:, None], dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(y)

xtr, ytr = make(1200)
xte, yte = make(600)

net = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Conv2d(1, 8, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Conv2d(8, 16, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Conv2d(16, 16, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.AdaptiveMaxPool2d(1), nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(16, 1))
pos_weight = (1 - ytr.mean()) / ytr.mean()           # re-weights the rare class
loss_fn = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss(pos_weight=pos_weight)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for epoch in range(6):
    perm = torch.randperm(len(xtr))
    for b in range(0, len(xtr), 64):
        idx = perm[b:b + 64]
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = loss_fn(net(xtr[idx]).squeeze(1), ytr[idx])
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    s = net(xte).squeeze(1).numpy()
y = yte.numpy()
# AUC as the Mann-Whitney probability P(score of a positive > score of a negative)
pos, neg = s[y == 1], s[y == 0]
mw = (pos[:, None] > neg[None, :]).mean() + 0.5 * (pos[:, None] == neg[None, :]).mean()
print(f"CNN test AUC: {roc_auc_score(y, s):.3f} (Mann-Whitney: {mw:.3f})")
# baseline 'radiomic' feature: maximum of the image after a smoothing filter
feat = np.array([gaussian_filter(im[0], 2).max() for im in xte.numpy()])
print(f"hand-crafted feature AUC: {roc_auc_score(y, feat):.3f}")

The CNN reaches an AUC of about 0.76 and the hand-crafted feature about 0.60; the Mann–Whitney count agrees with the library to three decimals. The blob is barely visible, and the CNN has learned a better matched filter than the smoothing kernel chosen by hand.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Volumes are classified by 3D networks, by 2D networks on slices with aggregation, or by 2.5D inputs; ImageNet pretraining mainly helps small data sets.
  • The AUC is the probability of ranking a positive above a negative; it ignores the threshold and the prevalence.
  • The predictive value collapses at low prevalence; probabilities trained under one prevalence can be corrected for another.
  • Multiple-instance learning trains from bag labels with permutation-invariant pooling.
  • Radiomics with a penalised linear model is the baseline to beat.

3. Detection

Detection answers “where are the lesions, and how many?”: lung nodules in CT, polyps in colonoscopy, microbleeds in brain MRI, cells in microscopy, anatomical landmarks. The output is a set of boxes or points with confidence scores, and the evaluation must count both missed lesions and false alarms.

3.1 Boxes and overlap

A 2D box is b = (x_0, y_0, x_1, y_1); a 3D box adds a third axis. A predicted box is matched to a ground-truth box when they overlap enough.

Definition 3.1 — Intersection over union For two boxes or regions A and B, \mathrm{IoU}(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} \in [0, 1].

A detection is a true positive if its IoU with an unmatched ground-truth box exceeds a threshold, often 0.5. For small round lesions, a centre-distance criterion is common instead: the detection hits a nodule if its centre lies inside the nodule’s radius.

IoU is very sensitive to localisation for small objects: shifting a 4 \times 4 box by one pixel along one axis gives an IoU of 12 / 20 = 0.6.

3.2 Region proposals and one-stage detectors

Modern detectors place many reference boxes, called anchors, at every position of a feature map, at several scales and aspect ratios. For each anchor, the network predicts an objectness score and a correction of the box.

Definition 3.2 — Box regression targets For an anchor with centre (x_a, y_a), width w_a and height h_a, and a target box (x, y, w, h), the network regresses t_x = \frac{x - x_a}{w_a}, \quad t_y = \frac{y - y_a}{h_a}, \quad t_w = \log \frac{w}{w_a}, \quad t_h = \log \frac{h}{h_a}, usually with a smooth-L^1 loss. The targets are invariant to translation and scale.

  • Two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN, Ren et al., 2015): a region proposal network scores the anchors; the best few hundred proposals are cropped from the feature map and passed to a second head that classifies them and refines the boxes.
  • One-stage detectors (YOLO, Redmon et al., 2016; RetinaNet, Lin et al., 2017) classify and regress all anchors (all grid cells, for the first YOLO) in one pass. They are faster, but face an extreme imbalance: tens of thousands of background anchors per lesion.
  • Medical variants add 3D anchors, and Retina U-Net (Jaeger et al., 2020) adds a segmentation decoder whose voxel-level supervision helps the detector when lesions are few.

3.3 The focal loss

The background anchors are mostly easy: the network quickly gives them a probability close to 1 of being background. Their sum still dominates the cross-entropy and its gradient.

Definition 3.3 — Focal loss (Lin et al., 2017) With p_t the predicted probability of the true class and a focusing parameter \gamma \geq 0, \mathrm{FL}(p_t) = -(1 - p_t)^\gamma \log p_t .

For \gamma = 0 it is the cross-entropy; a class weight \alpha_t is often added in front.

Proposition 3.4 — Easy examples are down-weighted The ratio of the focal loss to the cross-entropy is \frac{\mathrm{FL}(p_t)}{\mathrm{CE}(p_t)} = (1 - p_t)^\gamma , which tends to 0 as p_t \to 1 and is close to 1 for misclassified examples (p_t small).

Proof. Divide the two definitions; (1 - p_t)^\gamma is decreasing in p_t, equal to 1 at p_t = 0 and to 0 at p_t = 1. ∎

With \gamma = 2, an easy background anchor at p_t = 0.99 is down-weighted by 10^{-4}, a hard one at p_t = 0.5 by only 0.25. A thousand easy anchors then weigh less than one hard anchor.

3.4 Non-maximum suppression

A detector fires on many overlapping anchors around each lesion. Non-maximum suppression keeps one box per object.

Algorithm 3.5 — Greedy non-maximum suppression

  1. Sort the detections by decreasing score.
  2. Keep the highest-scoring box, and remove every remaining box whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold (0.5 for instance).
  3. Repeat on the remaining boxes until none is left.

Its cost is O(n^2) IoU computations in the worst case. Two adjacent lesions with overlapping boxes can be merged by mistake, which matters for clustered lesions.

3.5 Candidates, false positive reduction and landmarks

The classical detection pipeline has two stages. A sensitive candidate generator proposes locations, for instance with Hessian-based blob filters or intensity thresholds. A classifier then rejects the false candidates. Setio et al. (2016) kept this structure for lung nodules in CT and replaced the second stage by a multi-view CNN, which classifies 2D patches cut through each candidate along several planes.

Anatomical landmarks (points on the bones of a hand radiograph, cephalometric points of the skull, vertebra centres in spine CT) are single points. They are detected by heatmap regression: the network predicts, for landmark \ell, a map close to the Gaussian G_\ell(x) = \exp\big(-\|x - \ell\|^2 / (2\sigma^2)\big), and the prediction is the location of its maximum (Payer et al., 2019).

Proposition 3.6 — A differentiable argmax For a heatmap H on a grid \Omega and a temperature \beta > 0, the soft-argmax \hat \ell_\beta = \sum_{x \in \Omega} x\, \frac{e^{\beta H(x)}}{\sum_{y \in \Omega} e^{\beta H(y)}} is differentiable in H, and converges to the location of the maximum when this maximum is unique and \beta \to \infty.

Proof. The weights form a softmax, a smooth function of H. As \beta \to \infty, the weight of the unique maximiser x^\star is \Big(1 + \sum_{y \neq x^\star} e^{-\beta (H(x^\star) - H(y))}\Big)^{-1} \to 1, since every exponent is negative; the other weights tend to 0. ∎

The soft-argmax lets the network be trained directly on the coordinate error, in millimetres.

3.6 Evaluation: FROC analysis

A scan contains a variable number of lesions, and a detector can raise any number of false alarms; specificity is undefined. Detection is evaluated per lesion and per scan.

Definition 3.7 — Free-response ROC curve For each score threshold, let the sensitivity be the fraction of all ground-truth lesions that are hit, and count the average number of false positives per scan. The FROC curve plots the sensitivity against the false positives per scan.

The LUNA16 challenge (Setio et al., 2017) summarises it by the competition performance metric: the mean sensitivity at 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4 and 8 false positives per scan.

The code computes IoU and non-maximum suppression on four boxes, then an FROC curve on simulated detections: 90 % of the nodules receive a candidate, and each scan has about 20 false candidates.

import numpy as np

def iou(a, b):
    """Boxes as (x0, y0, x1, y1); a: (n, 4), b: (m, 4) -> (n, m) IoU matrix."""
    x0 = np.maximum(a[:, None, 0], b[None, :, 0]); y0 = np.maximum(a[:, None, 1], b[None, :, 1])
    x1 = np.minimum(a[:, None, 2], b[None, :, 2]); y1 = np.minimum(a[:, None, 3], b[None, :, 3])
    inter = np.clip(x1 - x0, 0, None) * np.clip(y1 - y0, 0, None)
    area = lambda r: (r[:, 2] - r[:, 0]) * (r[:, 3] - r[:, 1])
    return inter / (area(a)[:, None] + area(b)[None, :] - inter)

def nms(boxes, scores, thr=0.5):
    order, keep = np.argsort(-scores), []
    while order.size:
        i = order[0]
        keep.append(i)
        order = order[1:][iou(boxes[[i]], boxes[order[1:]])[0] < thr]
    return np.array(keep)

boxes = np.array([[10, 10, 30, 30], [12, 11, 31, 32], [50, 50, 70, 72], [11, 9, 29, 31.0]])
scores = np.array([0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6])
print("IoU(box0, box1) =", round(iou(boxes[[0]], boxes[[1]])[0, 0], 3))
print("kept after NMS:", nms(boxes, scores))

# FROC on synthetic scans: each scan has a few nodules; the detector outputs scored candidates
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_scans, hits, all_scores, n_nodules = 100, [], [], 0
for s in range(n_scans):
    k = rng.integers(1, 4); n_nodules += k
    tp = rng.random(k) < 0.9                          # 90 % of nodules get a candidate
    all_scores += list(rng.normal(1.5, 1.0, tp.sum())); hits += [1] * tp.sum()
    nf = rng.poisson(20)                              # false candidates in this scan
    all_scores += list(rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, nf)); hits += [0] * nf
order = np.argsort(-np.array(all_scores)); hits = np.array(hits)[order]
sens = np.cumsum(hits) / n_nodules                    # sensitivity at each threshold
fp_per_scan = np.cumsum(1 - hits) / n_scans           # average false positives per scan
cpm = [sens[fp_per_scan <= r].max() for r in (1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4, 8)]
print("sensitivity at 1/8 ... 8 FP/scan:", np.round(cpm, 3))
print(f"CPM (mean of the seven) = {np.mean(cpm):.3f}")

The IoU of the first two boxes is 342 / 457 \approx 0.748, and NMS keeps boxes 0 and 2. The FROC sensitivity rises from 0.13 to 0.79 between 1/8 and 8 false positives per scan, for a CPM of 0.43: with 20 false candidates per scan, false positive reduction is the real problem.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Detections are matched to lesions by IoU or by centre distance; IoU is harsh for small objects.
  • Anchor-based detectors regress scale-invariant box corrections; two-stage detectors filter proposals, one-stage detectors use the focal loss against the background imbalance.
  • Non-maximum suppression keeps one box per object.
  • Landmarks are detected by heatmap regression; the soft-argmax makes the coordinate differentiable.
  • Detection is evaluated by FROC curves: sensitivity per lesion against false positives per scan.

4. Segmentation

Segmentation labels every voxel: which voxels belong to the left ventricle, to the hippocampus, to the tumour? It gives volumes, shapes and boundaries, from which clinicians compute measurements such as the ejection fraction of the heart or the volume of a tumour. It is the task where deep learning has changed practice the most.

4.1 Classical segmentation

  • Thresholding labels the voxels whose intensity lies in an interval; Otsu’s method chooses the threshold that maximises the between-class variance of the histogram. It works for bone in CT, rarely in MRI.
  • Region growing starts from a seed and adds neighbouring voxels of similar intensity; it leaks through weak boundaries.
  • Energy-based methods (active contours, level sets, graph cuts) minimise an energy that balances fidelity to the image and smoothness of the boundary. Graph cuts find the global minimum of such binary energies when the pairwise terms are submodular, for instance when they penalise label changes between neighbours.
  • Atlas-based segmentation registers one or several labelled images (atlases) to the new image and transfers their labels; multi-atlas methods fuse the transferred labels by voting. It uses the registration of ch. 5 and was the state of the art for brain structures before deep learning.

4.2 Fully convolutional networks and U-Net

A fully convolutional network has no dense layer, so it maps an image of any size to a map of class scores of the same size. U-Net (Ronneberger, Fischer and Brox, 2015) became the reference architecture for biomedical segmentation.

Definition 4.1 — U-Net A U-Net has

  • an encoder of convolution blocks separated by downsampling, which halves the resolution and doubles the number of channels at each level;
  • a decoder of blocks separated by upsampling (transposed convolutions or interpolation);
  • skip connections that concatenate, at each resolution, the encoder features to the decoder features.

A final 1 \times 1 convolution gives one score per class and per voxel, turned into probabilities by a softmax.

The encoder provides context: after four downsamplings, a voxel’s features depend on a large region. The skip connections bring back the fine localisation that downsampling lost. Without them, boundaries are blurred.

4.3 Three-dimensional networks and patch-based training

The 3D U-Net (Çiçek et al., 2016) replaces every 2D operation by its 3D counterpart, and V-Net (Milletari, Navab and Ahmadi, 2016) adds residual blocks and the Dice loss below. A full volume rarely fits in GPU memory (ch. 0.2), so training and inference work on patches.

  • Training: sample patches of, say, 128^3 voxels, with a fixed fraction centred on foreground voxels, so that rare structures are seen.
  • Inference: slide the patch over the volume with overlap, and average the overlapping predictions, often with weights that decrease towards the patch border, where context is missing.

4.4 Losses and metrics

For a binary segmentation, let P be the predicted set of voxels and G the ground truth.

Definition 4.2 — Dice coefficient and soft Dice loss The Dice coefficient is \mathrm{Dice}(P, G) = \frac{2\,|P \cap G|}{|P| + |G|} .

With predicted probabilities p_i and labels g_i \in \{0, 1\}, the soft Dice loss is L_{\text{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{2 \sum_i p_i g_i + \varepsilon}{\sum_i p_i + \sum_i g_i + \varepsilon}, where \varepsilon is a small constant that handles empty masks.

Proposition 4.3 — Dice, F1 and Jaccard In terms of true positive, false positive and false negative voxels, \mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{2\,\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN}} = \frac{2 J}{1 + J}, where J = \mathrm{IoU}(P, G). The Dice coefficient is the F1 score of the voxel classification, and it does not depend on the true negatives.

Proof. |P \cap G| = \mathrm{TP}, |P| = \mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} and |G| = \mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN} give the first form. The union has U = \mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN} voxels, so J = \mathrm{TP} / U.

The denominator of the first form is \mathrm{TP} + U; dividing it and the numerator by U gives 2J / (J + 1). ∎

Because the true negatives are absent, the Dice loss is not swamped by the background, which can fill 99.9 % of a brain MRI with a small lesion. The cross-entropy, a mean over all voxels, is dominated by that background. It gives smoother gradients, however, and the sum of the two losses is a robust default.

The overlap measures miss the boundary: a thin false-positive island far away barely changes the Dice coefficient but can matter clinically.

Definition 4.4 — Hausdorff distance For the boundaries \partial P and \partial G, d_H(P, G) = \max\Big(\sup_{a \in \partial P} d(a, \partial G),\ \sup_{b \in \partial G} d(b, \partial P)\Big).

The 95th-percentile version (HD95) replaces each supremum by a 95th percentile, to be robust to a few outlier voxels. Distances are measured in millimetres, using the voxel spacing.

Example 4.5 — Dice depends on the size of the structure A one-voxel error all around the boundary of a cube of side 40 voxels (prediction of side 42) gives \mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2 \times 40^3}{40^3 + 42^3} \approx 0.927 .

The same error on a lesion of side 4 (prediction of side 6) gives 2 \times 64 / (64 + 216) \approx 0.457. Dice values are comparable only between structures of similar size.

4.5 nnU-Net and applications

Isensee et al. (2021) showed with nnU-Net that a carefully configured U-Net outperformed most specialised architectures on 23 public data sets. nnU-Net reads a fingerprint of the data set (spacings, image sizes, intensity statistics) and sets the preprocessing, patch size, network depth and batch size by fixed rules. It then trains 2D, 3D and cascaded variants by five-fold cross-validation and selects or ensembles them empirically.

  • Brain tumours: the BraTS challenge (Menze et al., 2015) segments, from four MRI contrasts, the enhancing tumour, the tumour core and the whole tumour, three nested regions.
  • Cardiac MRI: the ACDC challenge (Bernard et al., 2018) segments the left and right ventricular cavities and the myocardium on cine MRI. From the left-ventricle volumes at end-diastole (EDV) and end-systole (ESV) follows the ejection fraction (\mathrm{EDV} - \mathrm{ESV}) / \mathrm{EDV}: with 150 mL and 60 mL, it is 60 %.

The code trains a two-level U-Net on noisy ellipses with an intensity shading, with the sum of cross-entropy and soft Dice, and compares it with Gaussian smoothing followed by a threshold.

import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def make(n, size=32):
    """Noisy images of one ellipse (the 'organ'), with its binary mask."""
    ii, jj = np.mgrid[:size, :size]
    x, m = np.zeros((n, 1, size, size)), np.zeros((n, 1, size, size))
    for k in range(n):
        ci, cj = rng.uniform(10, 22, 2); a, b = rng.uniform(3, 8, 2)
        m[k, 0] = ((ii - ci) / a) ** 2 + ((jj - cj) / b) ** 2 < 1
        x[k, 0] = 0.6 * m[k, 0] + 0.2 * np.sin(jj / 3.0 + rng.uniform(0, 6))  # contrast + shading
    x += 0.35 * rng.standard_normal(x.shape)
    return torch.tensor(x, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(m, dtype=torch.float32)

def block(cin, cout):
    return nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(cin, cout, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(),
                         nn.Conv2d(cout, cout, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU())

class TinyUNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc1, self.enc2 = block(1, 8), block(8, 16)
        self.up = nn.ConvTranspose2d(16, 8, 2, stride=2)
        self.dec1 = block(16, 8)                       # 8 (skip) + 8 (upsampled) channels
        self.out = nn.Conv2d(8, 1, 1)
    def forward(self, x):
        e1 = self.enc1(x)
        e2 = self.enc2(F.max_pool2d(e1, 2))
        d1 = self.dec1(torch.cat([e1, self.up(e2)], dim=1))   # skip connection
        return self.out(d1)

def soft_dice_loss(logits, m, eps=1.0):
    p = torch.sigmoid(logits)
    inter = (p * m).sum(dim=(1, 2, 3))
    return 1 - ((2 * inter + eps) / (p.sum(dim=(1, 2, 3)) + m.sum(dim=(1, 2, 3)) + eps)).mean()

xtr, mtr = make(400)
xte, mte = make(100)
net = TinyUNet()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for it in range(200):
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(xtr), (16,))
    logits = net(xtr[idx])
    loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, mtr[idx]) + soft_dice_loss(logits, mtr[idx])
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    pred = (net(xte) > 0).float()
dice = 2 * (pred * mte).sum((1, 2, 3)) / (pred.sum((1, 2, 3)) + mte.sum((1, 2, 3)))
# classical baseline: Gaussian smoothing, then a global threshold
thr = torch.tensor(gaussian_filter(xte.numpy(), sigma=(0, 0, 1.5, 1.5)) > 0.3).float()
dice_thr = 2 * (thr * mte).sum((1, 2, 3)) / (thr.sum((1, 2, 3)) + mte.sum((1, 2, 3)))
print(f"U-Net Dice: mean {dice.mean():.3f}, worst case {dice.min():.3f}")
print(f"smoothing + threshold Dice: mean {dice_thr.mean():.3f}")

After 200 iterations the U-Net reaches a mean Dice of about 0.89, against 0.79 for the smoothed threshold, whose parameters were even tuned on the test images. The worst case, near 0.58, is a small ellipse, as Example 4.5 predicts: report the distribution of per-case scores, not only the mean.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Classical segmentation uses thresholds, region growing, energies and atlases; atlas methods rely on registration.
  • U-Net combines a contextual encoder, a decoder and skip connections that restore localisation; 3D versions train on patches.
  • Dice is the F1 score of voxels and ignores true negatives; Dice loss and cross-entropy are often summed.
  • Report overlap and boundary metrics (Dice, HD95) in millimetres, per case, aware that Dice penalises small structures.
  • nnU-Net configures a U-Net from the data fingerprint and is the baseline to beat.

5. Registration

Registration finds the spatial transformation that aligns two images: two scans of the same patient taken months apart, an MRI and a CT, or a patient and an atlas. It is needed to compare images voxel by voxel, to transfer labels and to measure change.

5.1 The registration problem

Definition 5.1 — Registration as optimisation Given a fixed image F and a moving image M on a domain \Omega, registration seeks a transformation \varphi : \Omega \to \Omega that minimises E(\varphi) = \mathcal{D}\big(F, M \circ \varphi\big) + \lambda\, \mathcal{R}(\varphi), where \mathcal{D} is a dissimilarity between images and \mathcal{R} a regulariser of the transformation.

The warped image M \circ \varphi is evaluated at non-integer positions, by linear or B-spline interpolation. Writing \varphi(x) = x + u(x) with a displacement field u, a typical regulariser is the smoothness term \int \|\nabla u\|^2.

5.2 Transformation models

  • Rigid: a rotation and a translation, 6 parameters in 3D; enough between two scans of the same head.
  • Affine: a linear map and a translation, 12 parameters; it absorbs global differences of size and shear between subjects.
  • Free-form deformations (Rueckert et al., 1999): a displacement interpolated by cubic B-splines from a coarse grid of control points.
  • Dense displacements: one vector per voxel, regularised by \mathcal{R}.

A transformation is physically plausible if it does not tear or fold tissue: it should be invertible, with positive local volume change.

Definition 5.2 — Jacobian determinant and folding For \varphi(x) = x + u(x), the Jacobian determinant J_\varphi(x) = \det\big(I + \nabla u(x)\big) is the local volume change. Where J_\varphi(x) < 0 the orientation is reversed. Where J_\varphi changes sign, space folds onto itself and \varphi is not injective near the fold; such voxels, with J_\varphi(x) \leq 0, are counted as folded.

Diffeomorphisms (smooth bijections with smooth inverse) are obtained by integrating a velocity field. With a stationary velocity field v, the transformation is the time-one flow of the equation \frac{d}{dt} \phi_t = v \circ \phi_t, with \phi_0 = \mathrm{id}, written \exp(v) (Arsigny et al., 2006; Ashburner, 2007).

Theorem 5.3 — Scaling and squaring Let v be a smooth velocity field on \mathbb{R}^d, bounded with bounded derivatives, and let (\phi_t) be its flow. Then \phi_1 is a diffeomorphism with inverse \phi_{-1}, and for every integer N, \phi_1 = \underbrace{\phi_{2^{-N}} \circ \dots \circ \phi_{2^{-N}}}_{2^N \text{ times}}, \qquad \phi_{2^{-N}} = \mathrm{id} + 2^{-N} v + O(2^{-2N}).

Proof. The equation is autonomous and has unique solutions (Cauchy–Lipschitz). Hence t \mapsto \phi_{s+t}(x) and t \mapsto \phi_t(\phi_s(x)) solve the same equation with the same initial value, and coincide: \phi_{s+t} = \phi_t \circ \phi_s.

With s = -t, this gives \phi_t \circ \phi_{-t} = \mathrm{id}, so each \phi_t is a bijection; smoothness of v makes \phi_t and its inverse smooth.

Applying the group property N times gives \phi_1 = \phi_{1/2} \circ \phi_{1/2}, then \phi_{1/2} = \phi_{1/4} \circ \phi_{1/4}, and so on. Finally a Taylor expansion of the flow at t = 0 gives the first-order approximation. ∎

In practice \phi_{2^{-N}} is replaced by \mathrm{id} + 2^{-N} v, then squared N times by composing it with itself (about 7 compositions). Each composition is one interpolation, so the whole computation is differentiable.

5.3 Similarity measures

  • Sum of squared differences, \int (F - M \circ \varphi)^2, for images of the same modality with the same intensities.
  • Normalised cross-correlation, often computed in local windows, for the same modality with different contrast or bias field.
  • Mutual information (Viola and Wells, 1997; Maes et al., 1997), for different modalities such as MRI and CT: \mathrm{MI}(F, M) = \sum_{a, b} p(a, b) \log \frac{p(a, b)}{p(a)\, p(b)}, where p(a, b) is the joint histogram of the intensities of F and M \circ \varphi at the same voxels.

Proposition 5.4 — Invariances of the similarity measures Normalised cross-correlation is unchanged when M is replaced by \alpha M + \beta with \alpha > 0. Mutual information of quantised intensities is unchanged when M is replaced by g(M) for any bijection g of the intensity levels.

Proof. NCC centres both images and divides by their standard deviations: \beta disappears with the centring, \alpha with the normalisation.

For MI, the joint histogram of (F, g(M)) is that of (F, M) with its columns relabelled by g. Each term of the sum is unchanged, since p(a, g(b)) = p(a, b) and the marginal p(g(b)) = p(b). ∎

Mutual information therefore only asks that intensities correspond consistently, not that they be equal: bone is bright in CT and dark in a T_1-weighted MRI.

5.4 Classical optimisation

Classical methods minimise E for each pair of images, by gradient descent over the parameters, from coarse to fine resolution to avoid local minima. Demons algorithms (Thirion, 1998; Vercauteren et al., 2009) alternate a force step and a Gaussian smoothing of the field; SyN (Avants et al., 2008) optimises symmetric diffeomorphisms. These methods are accurate but take minutes to hours per pair of 3D images.

5.5 Learning registration

A network g_\theta takes the pair (F, M) and outputs a displacement field u = g_\theta(F, M). The warped image is computed by a spatial transformer (Jaderberg et al., 2015), a differentiable interpolation layer.

Proposition 5.5 — Interpolation is differentiable in the displacement In 2D, linear interpolation evaluates M at a non-integer point p as M(p) = \sum_{q \in \mathbb{Z}^2} M(q) \prod_{d=1}^{2} \max\big(0, 1 - |p_d - q_d|\big).

It is continuous and piecewise linear in p, hence in u, with gradient given almost everywhere by finite differences of M between the four neighbouring grid points.

Proof. Only the four grid points with |p_d - q_d| < 1 contribute. On each cell of the grid, the weights are products of affine functions of p_1 and p_2, so their derivatives are differences of the neighbouring values of M. ∎

VoxelMorph (Balakrishnan et al., 2019) trains g_\theta without ground-truth deformations, on pairs of images, by minimising the energy of Definition 5.1: \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(F, M)}\Big[\mathcal{D}\big(F, M \circ (\mathrm{id} + g_\theta(F, M))\big) + \lambda \|\nabla g_\theta(F, M)\|^2\Big].

The optimisation is amortised: once trained, the network registers a new pair in one forward pass, in about a second on a GPU. When segmentations are available for training, a Dice term between warped and fixed labels improves anatomical accuracy. Dalca et al. (2019) make the output a stationary velocity field integrated by scaling and squaring layers (Theorem 5.3), which gives diffeomorphisms.

Supervised alternatives regress known deformations, either synthetic ones applied to real images or ones computed by a classical method; they inherit the errors of the method they imitate.

Evaluation. A good image match is easy and meaningless: an unregularised field can make any image look like any other. Registration is evaluated on what it should preserve: the Dice overlap of anatomical labels transferred by the transformation, the target registration error between corresponding landmarks in millimetres, and the fraction of voxels with J_\varphi \leq 0.

The code registers an image to an affinely transformed copy of itself by gradient descent through grid_sample, then compares a direct displacement with its scaling-and-squaring version.

import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 64
img = gaussian_filter((rng.random((n, n)) > 0.5).astype(float), 3)      # a blobby 'anatomy'
fixed = torch.tensor(img, dtype=torch.float32)[None, None]

def warp(im, theta):                     # theta: 2 x 3 affine in normalised [-1, 1] coordinates
    grid = F.affine_grid(theta[None], im.shape, align_corners=False)
    return F.grid_sample(im, grid, align_corners=False, padding_mode="border")

a = np.deg2rad(10)
true = torch.tensor([[1.05 * np.cos(a), -np.sin(a), 0.10], [np.sin(a), 0.95 * np.cos(a), -0.05]],
                    dtype=torch.float32)
moving = warp(fixed, true)               # the moving image is a warped copy of the fixed one

# 1) affine registration: find theta such that moving o T_theta ~ fixed  (SSD + gradient descent)
theta = torch.tensor([[1.0, 0, 0], [0, 1.0, 0]], requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([theta], lr=0.01)
for it in range(300):
    loss = ((warp(moving, theta) - fixed) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
A = torch.cat([theta.detach(), torch.tensor([[0, 0, 1.0]])])
B = torch.cat([true, torch.tensor([[0, 0, 1.0]])])
print(f"final SSD {loss.item():.2e}; composed transform (should be ~identity):")
print(np.round((B @ A).numpy()[:2], 3))

# 2) folding: Jacobian determinant of x -> x + u(x) for a large random displacement u
def jac_det(u):                           # u: (2, n, n) displacement in pixels, u[a] along axis a
    d = lambda f, axis: np.gradient(f, axis=axis)
    return (1 + d(u[0], 0)) * (1 + d(u[1], 1)) - d(u[0], 1) * d(u[1], 0)

v = np.stack([gaussian_filter(rng.standard_normal((n, n)), 4) for _ in range(2)])
v *= 8 / np.abs(v).max()                  # smooth field, up to 8 pixels
print(f"u = v directly:      {100 * (jac_det(v) <= 0).mean():.1f} % folded pixels")

# 3) scaling and squaring: phi = exp(v) = (id + v / 2^N) composed 2^N times
def compose(u, w):                        # displacement of (id + u) o (id + w)
    ii, jj = np.mgrid[:n, :n].astype(float)
    coords = torch.tensor(np.stack([jj + w[1], ii + w[0]]) / (n - 1) * 2 - 1, dtype=torch.float32)
    grid = coords.permute(1, 2, 0)[None]
    u_at = F.grid_sample(torch.tensor(u, dtype=torch.float32)[None], grid,
                         align_corners=True, padding_mode="border")[0].numpy()
    return w + u_at

N = 7
u = v / 2 ** N
for _ in range(N):
    u = compose(u, u)
print(f"u = exp(v) - id:     {100 * (jac_det(u) <= 0).mean():.1f} % folded pixels,"
      f" min det {jac_det(u).min():.2f}")

The recovered transform composed with the true one is the identity to within 0.01. Used directly as a displacement, the smooth field folds 3.7 % of the pixels; its exponential folds none, with a minimal Jacobian determinant of 0.15.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Registration minimises a dissimilarity between the fixed image and the warped moving image plus a regulariser.
  • Rigid, affine, B-spline and dense models trade flexibility for robustness; the Jacobian determinant detects folding.
  • Exponentials of stationary velocity fields, computed by scaling and squaring, are diffeomorphisms.
  • SSD, NCC and mutual information suit increasingly different intensity relations.
  • VoxelMorph amortises the optimisation in a network trained without ground-truth deformations; evaluate with label overlap, landmarks and folding, never image similarity alone.

6. Denoising and reconstruction

Scanners do not measure images: CT measures projections and MRI measures Fourier coefficients, both corrupted by noise. Reducing the radiation dose or the acquisition time degrades these measurements further. This chapter writes the forward models, recalls the classical reconstructions, and shows how networks denoise and reconstruct while staying consistent with the data.

6.1 Forward models

All reconstruction problems of this chapter have the form y = \mathcal{A} x + n, where x is the unknown image, \mathcal{A} the known acquisition operator and n the noise.

Definition 6.1 — Radon transform For an image f on \mathbb{R}^2, a direction \theta = (\cos \alpha, \sin \alpha) and \theta^\perp orthogonal to it, the projection at angle \alpha and offset s is p_\alpha(s) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f\big(s\, \theta + t\, \theta^\perp\big)\, dt .

By Proposition 1.1, a CT scanner measures p_\alpha(s) for the attenuation f = \mu, on a finite set of angles and offsets.

In MRI, \mathcal{A} = M \mathcal{F}: the Fourier transform followed by a sampling mask M that keeps only some k-space locations. Acquiring one line in R shortens the scan by a factor R, the acceleration.

6.2 Classical reconstruction

Theorem 6.2 — Fourier slice theorem The one-dimensional Fourier transform of a projection is a line of the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the image: \hat p_\alpha(\omega) = \hat f(\omega\, \theta) .

Proof. By definition, \hat p_\alpha(\omega) = \int\!\!\int f(s\theta + t\theta^\perp)\, e^{-2i\pi \omega s}\, dt\, ds .

The change of variables x = s\theta + t\theta^\perp is a rotation, with Jacobian 1, and s = \langle x, \theta \rangle. The integral becomes \int f(x)\, e^{-2i\pi \langle \omega\theta, x \rangle}\, dx = \hat f(\omega\theta).

∎

Writing the inverse 2D Fourier transform in polar coordinates gives filtered backprojection: f(x) = \int_0^\pi \int_{\mathbb{R}} \hat p_\alpha(\omega)\, |\omega|\, e^{2i\pi \omega \langle x, \theta \rangle}\, d\omega\, d\alpha .

Each projection is filtered by the ramp |\omega|, which amplifies high-frequency noise, then smeared back along its rays. With few angles or a low dose, the image shows streaks and noise.

For undersampled MRI, the zero-filled reconstruction \mathcal{F}^{-1}(y) shows aliasing. Compressed sensing (Lustig, Donoho and Pauly, 2007) samples k-space randomly, so that aliasing looks like noise, and solves \hat x = \arg\min_x \tfrac12 \|M \mathcal{F} x - y\|^2 + \lambda \|\Psi x\|_1, with \Psi a wavelet or finite-difference transform in which images are sparse.

6.3 Noise models

  • CT: photon counts are Poisson; at low dose, the noise of the log-transformed data grows where few photons arrive, and it becomes correlated after reconstruction.
  • PET: Poisson counts, at much lower numbers.
  • MRI: the complex k-space data have white complex Gaussian noise; the displayed magnitude image does not.

Proposition 6.3 — Rician bias of magnitude MRI If the complex value at a voxel is a + n_1 + i n_2 with n_1, n_2 independent \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), its magnitude |a + n_1 + i n_2| follows a Rice distribution. Where the signal vanishes (a = 0), its mean is \mathbb{E}\,|n_1 + i n_2| = \sigma \sqrt{\pi / 2} \approx 1.25\, \sigma .

Proof. For a = 0, the magnitude R has the Rayleigh density (r / \sigma^2)\, e^{-r^2 / 2\sigma^2} on r > 0. Its mean is \mathbb{E} R = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \int_0^\infty r^2 e^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\, dr = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\, \sigma^3}{2} = \sigma \sqrt{\pi/2}, because the integral over the whole line is the second moment of a centred Gaussian, \sigma^2, times its normalising constant \sqrt{2\pi}\, \sigma. ∎

The background of a magnitude image is therefore not zero, and low-signal regions are biased upwards; a denoiser trained with Gaussian noise on magnitude images learns the wrong model (Gudbjartsson and Patz, 1995).

6.4 Learned denoising

A network trained on pairs (noisy, clean) with the squared loss estimates the conditional mean of the clean image (Proposition 0.2 and its analogue for the squared loss). For low-dose CT, Chen et al. (2017) trained a residual encoder–decoder on pairs of simulated low-dose and normal-dose images; Jin et al. (2017) post-processed sparse-view filtered backprojections with a U-Net.

Clean targets are rare in medicine: no patient is scanned twice at full dose to make a training set. Noise2Noise removes the need for them.

Theorem 6.4 — Noise2Noise (Lehtinen et al., 2018) Let Y = X + N and Y' = X + N' be two noisy copies of the same image, with \mathbb{E}[N' \mid X, Y] = 0. For every function f, \mathbb{E}\|f(Y) - Y'\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\|f(Y) - X\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|N'\|^2 .

The two training objectives have the same minimisers.

Proof. Expand \|f(Y) - X - N'\|^2. The cross term is -2\, \mathbb{E}\langle f(Y) - X, N' \rangle. Conditioning on (X, Y), which determines f(Y) - X, it equals -2\, \mathbb{E}\big\langle f(Y) - X,\ \mathbb{E}[N' \mid X, Y] \big\rangle = 0 .

The last term does not depend on f. ∎

Two independent acquisitions, or two halves of the raw data, are enough. Noise2Self (Batson and Royer, 2019) goes further: it trains on single noisy images by predicting each pixel from the others, which is valid when the noise is independent between pixels.

6.5 Unrolled reconstruction networks

The most reliable learned reconstructions alternate a network and the physics. Starting from the zero-filled image, each iteration applies a learned denoiser D_\theta, then a data-consistency step that brings the image back towards the measurements.

Proposition 6.5 — Data consistency for Cartesian MRI With \mathcal{F} the unitary Fourier transform, M a binary mask, y the measured k-space (zero off the mask) and z = D_\theta(x), the problem \min_x \lambda \|M \mathcal{F} x - y\|^2 + \|x - z\|^2 has the solution x = \mathcal{F}^{-1} k, with k_j = \begin{cases} \big((\mathcal{F} z)_j + \lambda\, y_j\big) / (1 + \lambda) & \text{if } M_j = 1, \\ (\mathcal{F} z)_j & \text{if } M_j = 0. \end{cases}

Proof. \mathcal{F} is unitary, so \|x - z\|^2 = \|k - \mathcal{F} z\|^2 with k = \mathcal{F} x. The objective is then a sum of independent terms, one per frequency j. Each is a quadratic in k_j, minimised at the weighted average given. ∎

As \lambda \to \infty, the measured coefficients are simply put back. Cascades of such blocks were trained end to end by Schlemper et al. (2018); the variational network of Hammernik et al. (2018) and MoDL (Aggarwal, Mani and Jacob, 2019) unroll gradient or conjugate-gradient steps in the same way. The fastMRI data set (Zbontar et al., 2018) provides raw k-space for training and benchmarking.

Learned reconstructions can be unstable to tiny perturbations of the data, and can hallucinate or erase small structures (Antun et al., 2020). Radiologists’ reading of the reconstructed images, not only PSNR, is the final test.

The code simulates a fourfold-accelerated Cartesian acquisition of a head phantom, then alternates a median-filter denoiser and the exact data-consistency step, a hand-made version of an unrolled network. It also checks Proposition 6.3.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import median_filter

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 128
yy, xx = (np.mgrid[:n, :n] - n / 2) / (n / 2)
def ellipse(cx, cy, a, b):
    return ((xx - cx) / a) ** 2 + ((yy - cy) / b) ** 2 < 1
# a simple head phantom: skull, brain, ventricles, a small lesion
x = 1.0 * ellipse(0, 0, 0.72, 0.92) - 0.7 * ellipse(0, 0, 0.66, 0.86)
x += 0.2 * ellipse(-0.18, -0.1, 0.1, 0.3) + 0.2 * ellipse(0.18, -0.1, 0.1, 0.3)
x += 0.3 * ellipse(0.3, 0.45, 0.06, 0.06)

F = lambda u: np.fft.fft2(u, norm="ortho")              # unitary DFT: k-space
Fi = lambda k: np.fft.ifft2(k, norm="ortho")
# Cartesian undersampling: keep the 9 central phase-encoding lines + about 20 % of the others
rows = rng.random(n) < 0.2
rows[np.abs(np.fft.fftfreq(n) * n) < 5] = True
M = np.repeat(rows[:, None], n, axis=1)
sigma = 0.01
y = M * (F(x) + sigma * (rng.standard_normal((n, n)) + 1j * rng.standard_normal((n, n))))
psnr = lambda u: 10 * np.log10(x.max() ** 2 / np.mean((np.abs(u) - x) ** 2))
print(f"acceleration R = {n / rows.sum():.1f}")

z = Fi(y)                                                 # zero-filled reconstruction
print(f"zero-filled PSNR: {psnr(z):.2f} dB")

# alternate a denoiser D and an exact data-consistency step (Proposition 6.5, lambda -> infinity)
u = z
for k in range(50):
    d = median_filter(u.real, size=3) + 1j * median_filter(u.imag, size=3)   # D
    u = Fi(M * y + (1 - M) * F(d))                                           # DC
print(f"denoise + data consistency, 50 iterations: {psnr(u):.2f} dB")

# Rician bias: magnitude of pure complex Gaussian noise (zero signal)
noise = sigma * (rng.standard_normal(10**6) + 1j * rng.standard_normal(10**6))
print(f"mean magnitude of noise: {np.abs(noise).mean():.5f}, "
      f"sigma*sqrt(pi/2) = {sigma * np.sqrt(np.pi / 2):.5f}")

The zero-filled image reaches 16.6 dB; fifty iterations of median filtering and data consistency bring it to 21.7 dB, with no training at all. A learned denoiser in place of the median filter, trained end to end through the iterations, is exactly a cascade network.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • CT measures the Radon transform, MRI an undersampled Fourier transform; both are inverse problems y = \mathcal{A}x + n.
  • The Fourier slice theorem gives filtered backprojection; compressed sensing reconstructs undersampled MRI with a sparsity prior.
  • Noise is Poisson in CT and PET, complex Gaussian in raw MRI and Rician in magnitude MRI.
  • Noise2Noise trains denoisers without clean images; Noise2Self needs a single noisy image.
  • Unrolled networks alternate a learned denoiser and an exact data-consistency step, and must be checked for hallucinations.

7. Generative models: autoencoders and GANs

Generative models learn the distribution of images. In medical imaging they serve to detect anomalies as departures from normal anatomy, to translate one modality into another (MRI to CT), and to synthesise training data. This chapter derives the objectives of autoencoders, variational autoencoders and GANs, and shows what can go wrong.

7.1 Autoencoders and PCA

Definition 7.1 — Autoencoder An autoencoder is a pair of maps, an encoder E : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k and a decoder D : \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^d with k < d, trained to minimise the reconstruction error \sum_i \|x_i - D(E(x_i))\|^2. The bottleneck forces the code E(x) to keep only the main factors of variation.

Theorem 7.2 — The optimal linear autoencoder is PCA Let the data matrix X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} be centred, and let U_k hold its k leading left singular vectors. Among linear encoders and decoders, the reconstruction error is minimised by D(E(x)) = U_k U_k^\top x , the orthogonal projection onto the principal subspace.

Proof. For linear maps, D \circ E is a matrix W of rank at most k, so WX has rank at most k.

By the Eckart–Young theorem, the closest matrix of rank at most k to X in Frobenius norm is its truncated SVD, which equals U_k U_k^\top X. This value is reached with E = U_k^\top and D = U_k. ∎

Non-linear convolutional autoencoders generalise PCA to curved families of images. Their reconstructions are blurred, because the squared loss averages what the code does not determine (Proposition 0.2).

7.2 Variational autoencoders

A variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling, 2014) is a latent-variable model p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz, with p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I), trained together with an encoder q_\phi(z \mid x) that approximates the posterior.

Proposition 7.3 — Evidence lower bound For every x, \log p_\theta(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x)\, \|\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{ELBO}} + \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x)\, \|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big).

The ELBO is therefore a lower bound of the log-likelihood, tight when the encoder is the exact posterior.

Proof. For every z, Bayes’ rule gives \log p_\theta(x) = \log p_\theta(x, z) - \log p_\theta(z \mid x).

Add and subtract \log q_\phi(z \mid x), and take the expectation over z \sim q_\phi(\cdot \mid x).

The terms regroup into the expected log-likelihood, minus the KL divergence to the prior, plus the KL divergence to the true posterior. The last one is non-negative. ∎

With a Gaussian encoder \mathcal{N}(\mu, \mathrm{diag}(s^2)), the KL term is \frac12 \sum_j (\mu_j^2 + s_j^2 - \log s_j^2 - 1). The expectation is estimated by sampling z = \mu + s \odot \varepsilon with \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), the reparameterisation that lets gradients flow to \mu and s.

7.3 Anomaly detection

Annotating every possible pathology is impossible, but healthy scans are plentiful. A generative model trained on healthy images only reconstructs healthy anatomy well; a lesion is badly reconstructed, so the residual |x - D(E(x))| highlights it. Schlegl et al. (2017) did this with a GAN on retinal OCT images, and Baur et al. (2021) compared many autoencoder variants on brain MRI.

The code uses the optimal linear autoencoder of Theorem 7.2, fitted on 2000 synthetic healthy “brain slices”, to detect faint lesions in 100 of 200 test slices.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 32
yy, xx = (np.mgrid[:n, :n] - n / 2) / (n / 2)

def healthy(m):
    """'Brain slices': an ellipse whose size, ventricle width and contrast vary between subjects."""
    out = np.empty((m, n, n))
    for k in range(m):
        a, b, w, c = rng.uniform(0.7, 0.9), rng.uniform(0.8, 0.95), rng.uniform(0.1, 0.25), rng.uniform(0.8, 1.2)
        brain = (xx / a) ** 2 + (yy / b) ** 2 < 1
        vent = (xx / w) ** 2 + (yy / 0.4) ** 2 < 1
        out[k] = gaussian_filter(c * brain - 0.5 * c * vent, 1.0)
    return out + 0.15 * rng.standard_normal(out.shape)

train = healthy(2000).reshape(2000, -1)
pca = PCA(n_components=20, random_state=0).fit(train) # the optimal linear autoencoder (Thm 7.2)

test = healthy(200)
lesion = np.zeros_like(test, dtype=bool)
for k in range(100):                                  # half of the test subjects get a lesion
    ci, cj = rng.uniform(-0.4, 0.4, 2)
    lesion[k] = ((xx - cj) ** 2 + (yy - ci) ** 2) < 0.12 ** 2
test = test + 0.25 * lesion
flat = test.reshape(200, -1)
recon = pca.inverse_transform(pca.transform(flat))
resid = gaussian_filter(np.abs(flat - recon).reshape(200, n, n), (0, 1, 1))   # anomaly map

print(f"pixel-level AUC of the residual map: {roc_auc_score(lesion.ravel(), resid.ravel()):.3f}")
is_patho = np.arange(200) < 100
print(f"subject-level AUC of max residual:   {roc_auc_score(is_patho, resid.max(axis=(1, 2))):.3f}")
print(f"explained variance of 20 components: {pca.explained_variance_ratio_.sum():.3f}")

The residual map separates lesion pixels well (AUC about 0.94), but the maximum of the map separates patients less well (about 0.77): noise produces high residuals in healthy slices too. Real anatomy varies far more than this toy model, which is the central difficulty of the approach.

7.4 Generative adversarial networks

A GAN (Goodfellow et al., 2014) trains a generator G, which maps noise z to images, against a discriminator D, which tells real images from generated ones: \min_G \max_D\ V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}} \log D(x) + \mathbb{E}_{z} \log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big).

Proposition 7.4 — Optimal discriminator For a fixed generator with image density p_g, the optimal discriminator is D^\star(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}, and then V(D^\star, G) = -\log 4 + 2\, \mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g), which is minimal exactly when p_g = p_{\text{data}}.

Proof. Pointwise, a \log D + b \log(1 - D) with a, b > 0 is maximised at D = a / (a + b). Substituting, and writing m = (p_{\text{data}} + p_g)/2, the value becomes \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, m) + \mathrm{KL}(p_g \,\|\, m) - \log 4 = 2\, \mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g) - \log 4 .

∎

In practice the generator maximises \log D(G(z)) instead, which gives stronger gradients early in training. GAN samples are sharp, but training is unstable and can collapse onto a few modes.

7.5 Image-to-image translation

  • Paired data: pix2pix (Isola et al., 2017) conditions both networks on the source image s, and adds to the adversarial loss the term \lambda\, \mathbb{E}\|G(s) - t\|_1 over pairs (s, t) of source and target images.
  • Unpaired data: CycleGAN (Zhu et al., 2017) trains two generators G : S \to T and F : T \to S with two discriminators, and adds a cycle-consistency term \mathbb{E}\|F(G(s)) - s\|_1 + \mathbb{E}\|G(F(t)) - t\|_1 .

Wolterink et al. (2017) synthesised CT from brain MRI with unpaired data, useful for radiotherapy planning from MRI alone. Frid-Adar et al. (2018) augmented a liver-lesion classifier with GAN-generated lesions.

The danger is specific to medicine. Cohen, Luck and Honari (2018) showed that distribution-matching losses can hallucinate or remove tumours when the training distributions of the two domains differ: the output looks realistic for the target domain, whatever the input says. A translated image must never be read as a measurement.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • The optimal linear autoencoder projects onto the principal subspace; non-linear autoencoders generalise it and blur what they cannot encode.
  • VAEs maximise the ELBO, a lower bound of the log-likelihood, with the reparameterisation trick.
  • Models of healthy anatomy detect anomalies through reconstruction residuals; normal variability limits them.
  • The optimal discriminator turns the GAN game into the minimisation of the Jensen–Shannon divergence.
  • pix2pix and CycleGAN translate modalities, and can hallucinate or erase pathology.

8. Validation, interpretation and reproducibility

A medical imaging model is only as good as the evidence that it works on new patients. This chapter shows how evaluations go wrong (leakage, small test sets, unpaired comparisons), how to explain a prediction without fooling oneself, and how to make an experiment reproducible.

8.1 Splitting the data without leakage

Data leakage is any path by which information about the test set reaches training. The most common forms in medical imaging are:

  • slices, patches or augmented copies of the same subject in both training and test sets;
  • several visits of the same patient split across sets in a longitudinal study;
  • preprocessing, feature selection or normalisation statistics computed on all the data before splitting;
  • hyperparameters or the number of epochs chosen on the test set.

Wen et al. (2020) reviewed convolutional networks for Alzheimer’s disease classification from MRI and found that a substantial share of the published studies may have suffered from leakage, often through slice-level splits. They showed that such splits inflate accuracy, and released an open-source framework for reproducible evaluation.

Splits must be made at the level of the subject: every image of a patient goes to the same set. Hyperparameters are chosen by cross-validation inside the training set (nested cross-validation), and the test set is used once. An external test set, from other hospitals and scanners, measures the robustness to changes of scanner, protocol and population (the distribution shifts surveyed in ch. 9.4).

In the code below, each subject contributes 10 slices with a strong individual “fingerprint” and a weak disease effect. A one-nearest-neighbour classifier recognises the subject, not the disease.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, GroupKFold, cross_val_predict
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, accuracy_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_subj, per_subj, d = 100, 10, 50
label = rng.integers(0, 2, n_subj)                       # one diagnosis per subject
finger = rng.standard_normal((n_subj, d)) * 2.0          # subject-specific anatomy
signal = np.zeros(d); signal[:5] = 0.4                   # weak disease effect on 5 features
X = np.concatenate([finger[s] + label[s] * signal + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((per_subj, d))
                    for s in range(n_subj)])
y = np.repeat(label, per_subj)
groups = np.repeat(np.arange(n_subj), per_subj)          # subject identifier of each slice

clf = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=1)
splits = {"slice-level split (leaky)": KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0).split(X),
          "subject-level split": GroupKFold(5).split(X, y, groups)}
for name, cv in splits.items():
    pred = cross_val_predict(clf, X, y, cv=list(cv))
    print(f"{name:28s} accuracy = {accuracy_score(y, pred):.3f}")

# bootstrap confidence interval of a test AUC, resampling subjects (n = 100)
score = X[:: per_subj, :5].sum(axis=1)                   # one slice per subject, a simple score
auc = roc_auc_score(label, score)
boot = []
for b in range(2000):
    i = rng.integers(0, n_subj, n_subj)
    if label[i].min() != label[i].max():
        boot.append(roc_auc_score(label[i], score[i]))
lo, hi = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"AUC = {auc:.3f}, 95 % bootstrap interval [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")

The leaky split gives an accuracy of 1.000, the subject-level split 0.527: chance level for this classifier. The second part of the code is used in ch. 8.2.

8.2 The uncertainty of a performance estimate

Proposition 8.1 — Standard error of an accuracy If a fixed classifier has true accuracy p and is tested on n independent cases, the measured accuracy \hat p has mean p and standard deviation \sqrt{p(1 - p)/n} .

For large n, \hat p \pm 1.96 \sqrt{\hat p (1 - \hat p)/n} is an approximate 95 % confidence interval.

Proof. n \hat p is a sum of n independent Bernoulli variables of parameter p: its variance is n p (1 - p). Divide by n^2; the interval follows from the central limit theorem. ∎

With n = 100 and \hat p = 0.8, the interval is 0.8 \pm 0.078; with n = 1000, 0.8 \pm 0.025. Varoquaux (2018) showed that, with the sample sizes common in neuroimaging, cross-validation error bars are of the order of 10 points, larger than many published improvements.

For metrics without a simple formula (AUC, Dice, sensitivity at a fixed specificity), the bootstrap resamples the test subjects with replacement many times, recomputes the metric, and takes the 2.5 % and 97.5 % percentiles. In the code above, an AUC of 0.71 on 100 subjects has the interval [0.60, 0.81].

8.3 Comparing models, choosing metrics, calibration

Two models evaluated on the same test cases must be compared with paired tests, which remove the variability shared by both.

Proposition 8.2 — McNemar’s test Let b be the number of test cases that model A classifies correctly and model B wrongly, and c the reverse. Under the hypothesis that both models have the same accuracy, conditionally on b + c, b follows a binomial law of parameters b + c and \tfrac12, and for large counts \frac{(b - c)^2}{b + c} \approx \chi^2_1 .

Proof. The cases where both models agree carry no information on their difference. Under the hypothesis, each discordant case is equally likely to favour A or B, independently. The normal approximation of the binomial gives (b - c)/\sqrt{b + c} \approx \mathcal{N}(0, 1). ∎

For segmentation, the per-case Dice coefficients of two methods are compared by a paired Wilcoxon test or a paired bootstrap. When many methods are compared, the tests must be corrected for multiplicity. Maier-Hein et al. (2018) showed that challenge rankings can change with the metric, the aggregation and the test cases, and the Metrics Reloaded recommendations (Maier-Hein et al., 2024) guide the choice of a metric from the clinical question.

Probabilities matter when a clinician reads them. A model is calibrated if, among the cases given probability 0.8, 80 % are positive. The expected calibration error groups predictions into B bins of confidence and averages the gap: \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{|I_b|}{n}\, \big|\mathrm{acc}(I_b) - \mathrm{conf}(I_b)\big| .

Modern networks are often over-confident; temperature scaling divides the logits by a scalar fitted on validation data, which improves calibration without changing the ranking (Guo et al., 2017).

8.4 Interpretation

An explanation method assigns to each input voxel a relevance for a given prediction f_c(x) of class c.

  • Gradient saliency (Simonyan et al., 2014) uses |\partial f_c / \partial x|.
  • Occlusion masks regions and records the drop of the score.
  • Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) weights the feature maps A^k of the last convolutional layer by their averaged gradients.

Definition 8.3 — Grad-CAM With Z the number of positions of the feature map, \alpha_k^c = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial f_c}{\partial A^k_{ij}}, \qquad L^c = \mathrm{ReLU}\Big(\sum_k \alpha_k^c A^k\Big), upsampled to the image size.

Proposition 8.4 — Completeness of integrated gradients For a baseline x' (a black image, say) and a differentiable f, the integrated gradients (Sundararajan, Taly and Yan, 2017) \mathrm{IG}_i(x) = (x_i - x'_i) \int_0^1 \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\big(x' + t (x - x')\big)\, dt satisfy \sum_i \mathrm{IG}_i(x) = f(x) - f(x').

Proof. Let \gamma(t) = x' + t(x - x'). By the chain rule, \frac{d}{dt} f(\gamma(t)) = \sum_i (x_i - x'_i)\, \partial_i f(\gamma(t)).

Integrating from 0 to 1, the left side gives f(x) - f(x') by the fundamental theorem of calculus, and the right side gives \sum_i \mathrm{IG}_i(x). ∎

Explanations must themselves be validated. Adebayo et al. (2018) proposed sanity checks: randomise the weights of the trained network, or train it on random labels, and verify that the explanation changes. Some popular methods produce almost the same maps, which then reflect edges of the image rather than the model.

Explanations are most useful for detecting shortcut learning. Zech et al. (2018) found that pneumonia detectors could identify the hospital of a radiograph, and that their performance was often lower on radiographs from other hospitals; DeGrave, Janizek and Lee (2021) found that COVID-19 detectors relied on shortcuts such as laterality markers and features near the image borders, rather than on the lungs. Performance must also be reported by subgroup (sex, age, site): Larrazabal et al. (2020) showed that gender imbalance in training data degrades chest X-ray diagnosis for the under-represented group.

8.5 Reproducibility

A result is reproducible if another team can obtain it again from the same data and code. In practice:

  • fix and report the random seeds, and run several seeds, since GPU convolutions are not always deterministic;
  • version the code, the data splits (as lists of subject identifiers) and the preprocessing pipeline, and record library versions;
  • report the data set, inclusion criteria, labels and their source, following a checklist such as CLAIM (Mongan, Moy and Kahn, 2020);
  • release the code and, when allowed, the trained weights; use public data for at least one experiment.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Split by subject; never let slices, visits, preprocessing statistics or hyperparameter choices cross the train/test boundary.
  • With 100 test cases an accuracy is known to about \pm 8 points; use confidence intervals, bootstrap over subjects and paired tests.
  • Choose metrics from the clinical question; check calibration, and fix it with temperature scaling.
  • Grad-CAM and integrated gradients explain predictions; sanity checks and subgroup analyses reveal shortcuts.
  • Reproducibility requires seeds, versioned splits and code, and complete reporting.

9. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It surveys active research topics that extend the methods of ch. 2–8, with entry points into the literature; the MICCAI, IPMI, ISBI and MIDL conferences named in the official references are where most of this work appears.

9.1 Foundation models

Large models pretrained on broad data and adapted to many tasks are reaching medical imaging. The Segment Anything Model (Kirillov et al., 2023) segments an object from a prompt (a point or a box); work by Ma and coauthors (2024) fine-tuned it on more than a million medical image–mask pairs across many modalities to obtain MedSAM. Moor et al. (2023) describe the broader programme of “generalist” medical models that combine images, text and other data.

Open questions: do such models beat a task-specific nnU-Net when a few hundred annotated cases exist? How should they be validated, given that their pretraining data are rarely documented well enough to exclude overlap with test sets?

9.2 Learning with fewer labels

Self-supervised pretraining learns representations from unlabelled scans. Models Genesis (Zhou et al., 2021) learns to restore deliberately corrupted 3D CT patches; Azizi et al. (2021) showed that contrastive pretraining on unlabelled medical images improved dermatology and chest X-ray classifiers. Radiology reports are a free source of weak labels: Tiu et al. (2022) trained an image–text model on chest radiographs and their reports that detects pathologies without any explicit annotation.

The survey of Cheplygina, de Bruijne and Pluim (2019) organises semi-supervised, multiple-instance and transfer learning in medical imaging. Annotation noise and disagreement between experts remain poorly handled by losses that assume a single true label.

9.3 Privacy and federated learning

Medical data rarely leave the hospital. In federated learning, each site trains on its own data and only model updates are aggregated; Rieke et al. (2020) and Kaissis et al. (2020) review the approach and its privacy limits. Model updates can still leak information about the training data, which motivates secure aggregation and differential privacy, at a cost in accuracy that is not yet well understood for 3D segmentation.

9.4 Distribution shift, causality and fairness

Models degrade when the scanner, protocol, hospital or population changes. Castro, Walker and Glocker (2020) argue that the causal structure of the data-generating process (does the image cause the label, or the reverse?) determines which shifts a model can survive. Oakden-Rayner et al. (2020) showed that average performance can hide clinically important subgroups where models fail (“hidden stratification”).

Gichoya et al. (2022) found that deep networks can predict patients’ self-reported race from medical images, even when clinicians cannot, which raises the question of how such information is used in diagnostic models. Measuring and correcting such effects is an open problem.

9.5 Uncertainty quantification

A clinician needs to know when not to trust a prediction. Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017) and Monte Carlo dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016) estimate predictive uncertainty empirically. Conformal prediction gives distribution-free guarantees (Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023).

Proposition 9.1 — Split conformal prediction Let s(x, y) be a non-conformity score (for instance 1 - \hat p_y(x)), computed on n calibration cases exchangeable with the test case.

Let \hat q be the \lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha) \rceil-th smallest calibration score, and \hat q = +\infty if this index exceeds n. The set C(x) = \{y : s(x, y) \leq \hat q\} satisfies P\big(Y_{\text{test}} \in C(X_{\text{test}})\big) \geq 1 - \alpha .

Proof. By exchangeability, the rank of the test score among the n + 1 scores is uniform on \{1, \dots, n + 1\} (ties broken at random). The test label is in C when its score is at most \hat q. This happens whenever its rank is at most m = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil, an event of probability m / (n + 1) \geq 1 - \alpha. ∎

The guarantee is marginal, averaged over patients, and it fails under the distribution shifts of ch. 9.4: conditional and shift-robust versions are an active topic.

9.6 Diffusion models and new architectures

Diffusion models, which generate images by learning to reverse a gradual noising process, now dominate image synthesis. Chung and Ye (2022) used score-based diffusion models as priors for accelerated MRI reconstruction, combining them with the data-consistency step of ch. 6.5. Wolleb et al. (2022) used them for anomaly detection, and Pinaya et al. (2022) generated synthetic 3D brain MRI with latent diffusion models. They inherit the hallucination risks of ch. 7.5.

Transformers have been combined with U-Nets (TransUNet, Chen et al., 2021; UNETR, Hatamizadeh et al., 2022). Isensee et al. (2024) argued, after re-evaluating many recent architectures under rigorous validation, that well-configured convolutional U-Nets remain competitive: claims of progress depend on the strength of the baseline (ch. 8).

9.7 Open problems

  • Validation that predicts clinical benefit: prospective studies and the comparison with clinicians on the decisions that matter, not only on benchmark metrics.
  • Robustness to new scanners and hospitals without retraining, and the detection of inputs outside the training distribution.
  • Learning from heterogeneous labels: different annotation protocols, partial labels, disagreement between experts.
  • Reconstruction guarantees: when can a learned reconstruction be trusted not to invent or erase a lesion?
  • Longitudinal and multimodal models that combine images over time with clinical data, as clinicians do.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Foundation models such as MedSAM and self-supervised pretraining reduce the need for labels, but their validation is harder.
  • Federated learning keeps data in hospitals; privacy guarantees have a cost.
  • Distribution shift, hidden stratification and fairness are where models fail in practice.
  • Conformal prediction gives marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability.
  • Diffusion models and transformers are powerful, but strong baselines and rigorous validation decide what is progress.

    1. Let p_{\text{data}} be uniform on [0, 1] and p_g uniform on [2, 3]. Give D^\star, the Jensen–Shannon divergence and V(D^\star, G).

    What happens when p_g moves to [1.5, 2.5], and why is it a problem for training?

    Solution: (a) By the formula of ch. 7.2, \tfrac12 (1 + 0.25 - \log 0.25 - 1) \approx 0.818.

    1. By Proposition 7.4, D^\star = 1 on [0, 1] and 0 on [2, 3]. The supports are disjoint, so \mathrm{JS} = \log 2 and V = -\log 4 + 2 \log 2 = 0.

    Moving p_g to [1.5, 2.5] leaves the supports disjoint: the divergence stays \log 2, so it gives no signal about how close the generator is. This motivates other divergences, such as the Wasserstein distance.

  12. ★★ Confidence intervals and a paired test, numerically. A model is correct on 80 of 100 test patients. (a) Compute the normal-approximation interval of Proposition 8.1, a percentile-bootstrap interval, and the Hoeffding radius of Theorem 0.4. (b) A second model is correct on 85; on the discordant cases, the first is right 3 times and the second 8 times. Is the difference significant at 5 %?

    Hints and expected result: (a) The normal interval is [0.722, 0.878], the bootstrap gives about [0.72, 0.88], and Hoeffding’s radius is 0.136.

    1. McNemar’s statistic (Proposition 8.2) is 25 / 11 \approx 2.27, with a p-value of 0.13; the exact binomial test gives 0.23. Five points of accuracy on 100 patients are not significant.

  13. ★★ Integrated gradients for a logistic model. Let f(x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b), with \sigma the logistic function, and the baseline x' = 0.

    1. Compute \mathrm{IG}_i(x) in closed form, for w^\top x \neq 0, and check Proposition 8.4. (b) Compare with the gradient saliency when |w^\top x| is large. (c) Propose a sanity check, in the sense of Adebayo et al. (2018), that the attribution x_i alone (the input itself) would fail.

    Solution: (a) Along the path, \partial_i f = w_i\, \sigma'(t\, w^\top x + b). Integrating in t, \mathrm{IG}_i(x) = x_i w_i\, \frac{\sigma(w^\top x + b) - \sigma(b)}{w^\top x}. Summing over i gives \sigma(w^\top x + b) - \sigma(b) = f(x) - f(0).

    1. The gradient w_i\, \sigma'(w^\top x + b) vanishes when the logistic saturates, even though the features drove the decision; IG does not.

    2. Randomise w: IG changes with w, whereas the input-only map does not, so it explains the image, not the model.

  14. ★★★ Leakage with a convolutional network. Modify the code of ch. 2.5 so that the data come from 150 “subjects” of 8 slices each: every subject has its own background texture, shared by all its slices, and its own label. Train the CNN with a slice-level split and with a subject-level split, and compare the test AUCs. How does the gap change with the strength of the subject texture and of the lesion?

    Hints and expected result: draw one texture per subject and add small slice-specific noise. Split with GroupShuffleSplit for the subject level.

    The slice-level split gives a higher AUC, because the network can recognise the subject’s texture seen in training. The gap grows with the subject-specific variability and shrinks when the lesion signal is strong (ch. 8.1).

  15. ★★★ Designing a multi-centre validation study. A team wants to show that a hippocampus segmentation network trained on one public data set works in three hospitals. Design the study: data and splits, reference segmentations, metrics, confidence intervals, subgroups, comparison with a classical method, and what result would count as success.

    Hints and expected result: train and tune only on the public data, with subject-level cross-validation (ch. 8.1). Hold out the three hospitals entirely as external test sets, with reference segmentations by two raters to measure inter-rater variability.

    Report Dice and HD95 in millimetres per hospital with bootstrap intervals (ch. 4.4, 8.2), and compare with a multi-atlas method by paired tests (ch. 8.3). Analyse age, disease stage and scanner as subgroups (ch. 9.4).

    Success: a per-hospital performance within inter-rater variability, with intervals reported, and a pre-registered analysis plan.

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 2 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic device; four exercises, 20 points in total.

  1. Question (5 points). Course question. Define data leakage, give three forms of it in medical imaging, and describe a protocol that avoids them. A model has an accuracy of 0.85 on 200 independent test patients: give an approximate 95 % confidence interval.

    Expected: ch. 8.1 (subject-level splits, nested cross-validation, preprocessing inside the training folds, external test set). By Proposition 8.1, the standard error is \sqrt{0.85 \times 0.15 / 200} \approx 0.025, hence 0.85 \pm 0.049.

  2. Question (5 points). Computation. A tumour segmentation has 800 true positive, 200 false positive and 100 false negative voxels, with voxels of 1 \times 1 \times 2 mm. Compute the Dice coefficient, the Jaccard index, the predicted and true volumes, and check the relation between Dice and Jaccard. Explain why a Dice of 0.84 means less for a small lesion than for the liver.

    Expected: Dice = 1600 / 1900 \approx 0.842, Jaccard = 800 / 1100 \approx 0.727, and 2J/(1 + J) \approx 0.842 (Proposition 4.3). The volumes are 2.0 mL predicted and 1.8 mL true. Example 4.5: boundary errors weigh more on small structures.

  3. Question (5 points). Short proof. State and prove the Noise2Noise theorem. Then explain why training on two noisy magnitude MRI images of the same slice gives a biased denoiser at low signal-to-noise ratio.

    Expected: Theorem 6.4 and its proof. For magnitude images, the noise of the target is not centred given the clean image (Proposition 6.3): the hypothesis fails, and the network learns the mean of the Rician magnitude, which is above the true signal.

  4. Question (5 points). Registration. Write the VoxelMorph training loss and explain each term. Why is a low image dissimilarity not evidence of a good registration? Compute the Jacobian determinant of \varphi(x, y) = (x + a \sin x,\ y) and say for which a it folds; how does scaling and squaring avoid folding?

    Expected: the loss of ch. 5.5 (dissimilarity of the warped image, smoothness of the field). An unregularised field can match intensities while tearing anatomy; evaluation uses labels, landmarks and folding. The determinant is 1 + a \cos x: folding for |a| > 1. Theorem 5.3: exponentials of smooth velocity fields are diffeomorphisms.
