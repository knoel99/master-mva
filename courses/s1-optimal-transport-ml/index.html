0. Review: couplings, convex functions, and push-forwards

Optimal transport moves the mass of one probability onto the mass of another and charges a cost for the motion. The later chapters need three M1 tools: couplings, push-forwards, and convex conjugates. This review fixes the words and checks them on a 2 by 2 matrix.

Every measure in the in-class chapters has total mass 1. An integral against a discrete measure is a finite sum. The line is enough for the running example. The Gaussian formula in ch. 4 needs matrices on a Euclidean space.

Definition 0.1 — Coupling

Let \mu and \nu be probabilities. A coupling is a probability on the product space whose first marginal is \mu and whose second marginal is \nu.

The set of all couplings is written \Pi(\mu,\nu). On a finite set a coupling is a nonnegative matrix. Its row sums are the masses of \mu. Its column sums are the masses of \nu.

If the two mass vectors have different totals, that set is empty. Every example below has total mass 1 on each side. The matrix test is the one the linear program in ch. 1 will use as its constraint.

Definition 0.2 — Push-forward

Let T be a measurable map. The push-forward T_{\sharp}\mu assigns to a set B the mass \mu gives to the preimage of B.

The map x \mapsto (x, T(x)) pushes \mu forward to a coupling supported on the graph of T. That coupling is called deterministic. Mass does not split along the way.

Proposition 0.3 — The product is a coupling

The product measure \mu \otimes \nu belongs to \Pi(\mu,\nu). On a finite set the product is the rank-one matrix whose entries are a_i b_j.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 0.3

Let f depend only on the first coordinate. Integrating f against the product and applying Fubini leaves \int f\,d\mu, because the integral in the second variable equals 1.

The same argument with the coordinates swapped shows that the second marginal is \nu. The total mass is 1, so the product is a coupling.

On a finite set the same arithmetic is (ab^{\top})\mathbf{1} = a, since b sums to 1, and the column sums equal b. The entries are nonnegative.

∎

The product coupling is always feasible and usually wasteful. It moves mass independently of any geometry. Every optimality proof below has to beat it.

Definition 0.4 — Convex conjugate

For a function \varphi from \mathbb{R}^d to the extended reals, the conjugate at y is the supremum of \langle x, y \rangle - \varphi(x) over x.

Proposition 0.5 — The conjugate is convex

The conjugate \varphi^{\ast} is a convex function of y, whether or not \varphi itself is convex.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 0.5

Fix x. The map y \mapsto \langle x, y \rangle - \varphi(x) is affine in y. The conjugate is the pointwise supremum of these affine functions as x varies.

A supremum of affine functions is convex. This is the pattern ch. 2 reuses when a cost constraint is rearranged into a convex potential.

∎

Example 0.6 — Two feasible matrices

Take masses a = (0.4, 0.6) and b = (0.5, 0.5). The matrix with entries 0.4, 0, 0.1, 0.5 has row sums 0.4 and 0.6 and column sums 0.5 and 0.5.

It is a coupling. The product matrix has entries 0.2, 0.2, 0.3, 0.3. It is a second coupling, and the two matrices are not equal. ch. 1 scores both by a squared cost.

Key — chapter 0

A finite coupling is a nonnegative matrix with the prescribed row sums and the prescribed column sums. The product matrix always qualifies. A map produces only the couplings that sit on its graph.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A coupling keeps both marginals. If the totals differ, no coupling exists.
  • The product measure is the coupling that ignores geometry. It is feasible and usually not optimal.
  • A map induces one coupling, supported on its graph. The converse is false, because mass can split.
  • The convex conjugate is a supremum of affine functions, so it is convex. ch. 2 builds Brenier’s potential the same way.

1. Monge maps and the Kantorovich linear program

Monge asked for a map that moves a pile of mass onto another pile at minimal cost. The map is a rigid way to move mass. Kantorovich allowed the mass at one point to split. On a finite set the relaxed problem is a linear program, and that program is the computational form used in the rest of the notes.

The cost in the running example is squared distance on the line. In several dimensions the same theory uses the squared Euclidean norm. Multiplying the cost by a positive constant does not change the minimizing plan. It only rescales the optimal value.

Definition 1.1 — Monge problem

The Monge problem seeks a map T with T_{\sharp}\mu = \nu that minimizes the integral of c(x, T(x)) against \mu.

If no such map exists, the problem is infeasible. Existence of a map is a property of the pair (\mu, \nu), not only of the cost.

Definition 1.2 — Kantorovich problem

The Kantorovich problem seeks a coupling \pi \in \Pi(\mu,\nu) that minimizes the integral of c(x,y) against \pi.

On a finite set this is the linear program that minimizes \langle C, P \rangle over nonnegative matrices with the right row sums and column sums.

The optimal value for the squared distance is written W_2^2. Its square root is the 2-Wasserstein distance. For the distance |x-y| the optimal value is already W_1, with no extra root. Both names appear in ch. 5.

Theorem 1.3 — Kantorovich relaxes Monge

If a map T pushes \mu forward to \nu, the deterministic coupling built from T is feasible for Kantorovich and has the same cost as T.

The Kantorovich infimum is therefore at most the Monge infimum. Whenever a Monge minimizer exists, it is a Kantorovich minimizer as well.

Proof. Proof of Theorem 1.3

Let \pi be the push-forward of \mu by x \mapsto (x, T(x)). The first marginal is \mu because the first coordinate is the identity.

The second marginal is T_{\sharp}\mu, which equals \nu by hypothesis. Thus \pi is a coupling.

On its support the second coordinate equals T of the first, so the integral of c against \pi equals the Monge cost of T.

Kantorovich minimizes over a larger set. Its infimum cannot exceed a cost that a feasible coupling already attains.

∎

Proposition 1.4 — Vertices have few positive entries

Assume a \in \mathbb{R}^n and b \in \mathbb{R}^m are probability vectors. A vertex of the transport polytope has at most n+m-1 positive entries.

When every mass equals 1/n, every vertex is a permutation matrix divided by n.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 1.4

The n row-sum constraints and the m column-sum constraints are dependent. Adding the row sums reproduces the total mass, and adding the column sums reproduces the same number.

At most n+m-1 of the equalities are independent. A vertex is a feasible matrix whose positive entries correspond to independent columns of that constraint system, so it has at most n+m-1 positive entries.

If every mass equals 1/n, the polytope is a scaling of the doubly stochastic matrices. The Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem says those vertices are permutation matrices, so the transport vertices are the same matrices divided by n. The Hungarian algorithm finds an optimal permutation in O(n^3) operations. ch. 5 is where large problems switch to Sinkhorn scaling.

∎

The bound n+m-1 is what makes a 2 by 2 plan easy to draw by hand. Here n+m-1 = 3, so an optimal vertex has at most three positive entries. Example 1.5 hits that count exactly.

Example 1.5 — Squared cost on four atoms

Source atoms sit at 0 and 1 with masses 0.4 and 0.6. Target atoms sit at 0.2 and 1.5 with masses 0.5 and 0.5. The cost is squared distance.

C = \begin{pmatrix} 0.04 & 2.25 \\ 0.64 & 0.25 \end{pmatrix}.

Every coupling has the shape below for a single parameter p \in [0, 0.4]. The four marginal equations leave one degree of freedom, in line with Proposition 1.4.

P(p) = \begin{pmatrix} p & 0.4-p \\ 0.5-p & 0.1+p \end{pmatrix}.

The cost \langle C, P(p) \rangle is linear in p. Its slope equals 0.04 + 0.25 - 2.25 - 0.64 = -2.6. The slope is negative, so the minimum on [0, 0.4] sits at p = 0.4.

The optimal entries are 0.4, 0, 0.1, and 0.5. The cost is 0.04 \cdot 0.4 + 0.64 \cdot 0.1 + 0.25 \cdot 0.5 = 0.205. This matrix is the coupling of Example 0.6.

The product coupling of Example 0.6 has entries 0.2, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.3. Its cost is the sum below.

0.2\cdot 0.04 + 0.2\cdot 2.25 + 0.3\cdot 0.64 + 0.3\cdot 0.25 = 0.725.

Independence pays 0.725 where the optimal plan pays 0.205. The optimal plan refuses the long jump from 0 to 1.5.

Example 1.6 — A target that no map can hit

Let \nu be a point mass at 0, and let \mu be the average of point masses at 0 and at 1. A map defined \nu-almost everywhere sends the single atom to one place.

Its push-forward is a single point mass. It cannot equal \mu. The Monge problem from \nu to \mu is empty.

Kantorovich still has a coupling. Send mass 1/2 from 0 to 0 and mass 1/2 from 0 to 1. For the ground cost |x-y| that coupling costs 1/2.

Every coupling has this cost, because half the mass must travel a distance 1.

Key — chapter 1

On a finite set, compute W_2^2 by minimizing the linear cost \langle C, P \rangle over couplings. A vertex has at most n+m-1 positive entries. The Monge problem is the same minimization restricted to maps, and that restriction can be empty.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Monge minimizes over maps. Kantorovich minimizes over couplings. Theorem 1.3 says the second value is at most the first.
  • The finite problem is a linear program. Proposition 1.4 limits the number of positive entries at a vertex.
  • In Example 1.5 the optimal squared cost is 0.205, against 0.725 for the product coupling.
  • Example 1.6 is a pair with couplings and with no transport map. Splitting mass is the whole point of the relaxation.

2. Duality and Brenier’s theorem

A feasible coupling gives an upper bound on the transport cost. A dual potential gives a lower bound. When the two bounds meet, both are optimal. On a finite set this is linear programming duality. For the quadratic cost in several dimensions, Brenier’s theorem says the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function.

Definition 2.1 — c-transform

Given a cost c and a function \varphi, the c-transform at y is the infimum of c(x,y) - \varphi(x) over x.

The pair (\varphi, \varphi^{c}) satisfies \varphi(x) + \varphi^{c}(y) \le c(x,y) for every (x,y). It is feasible for the dual problem below.

Theorem 2.2 — Discrete Kantorovich duality

Let C be a real n by m cost, and let a and b be probability vectors. The minimal coupling cost equals the maximum of \varphi \cdot a + \psi \cdot b over vectors that satisfy the inequality below for every pair of indices.

\varphi_i + \psi_j \le C_{ij}.

At a joint optimum, a positive entry of P forces equality in that constraint.

Proof. Proof of Theorem 2.2

The transport polytope is nonempty by Proposition 0.3, and it is closed and bounded. A linear function on a nonempty compact polytope attains its minimum.

Attach a multiplier \varphi_i to each row sum and a multiplier \psi_j to each column sum. If \varphi_i + \psi_j > C_{ij} for some entry, increasing that entry of P drives the Lagrangian to -\infty.

A finite dual bound therefore requires \varphi_i + \psi_j \le C_{ij} for every entry. When the inequality holds, the Lagrangian is at least \varphi \cdot a + \psi \cdot b, with equality for plans supported where equality holds in the constraint.

Every dual-feasible pair is a lower bound on the primal cost. A feasible bounded linear program has strong duality, so the dual maximum equals the primal minimum. Complementary slackness is the support statement in the theorem.

∎

Example 2.4 — A sharp certificate

Use the cost and the masses of Example 1.5. Set \varphi = (0, 0.6) and \psi = (0.04, -0.35).

On the support of the optimal plan the constraints are equalities. The route from 0 to 0.2 gives 0 + 0.04 = 0.04. The route from 1 to 0.2 gives 0.6 + 0.04 = 0.64. The route from 1 to 1.5 gives 0.6 - 0.35 = 0.25.

The unused route from 0 to 1.5 has slack 2.25 - (0 - 0.35) = 2.6. That slack is the absolute value of the slope in Example 1.5. It is the reduced cost of the missing edge.

The dual objective is the sum below. It matches the primal cost, so both sides are optimal.

0.6\cdot 0.6 + 0.04\cdot 0.5 + (-0.35)\cdot 0.5 = 0.205.

Brenier’s theorem is the continuous counterpart of this certificate for the quadratic cost. The convex potential plays the role of \varphi, and its gradient plays the role of the support of P.

Theorem 2.3 — Brenier

Let \mu and \nu be probabilities on \mathbb{R}^d with finite second moments, and assume \mu is absolutely continuous.

For the cost \|x-y\|^2 there is a convex function u such that \nabla u pushes \mu forward to \nu.

The map \nabla u is the unique optimal Monge map, up to a \mu-negligible set.

The factor 1/2 in the cost does not change the minimizer. It makes the gradient appear with coefficient 1.

Proof. Proof of the convexity step in Theorem 2.3

Work with the cost \|x-y\|^2 / 2. A dual constraint \varphi(x) + \psi(y) \le \|x-y\|^2 / 2 rearranges as follows. Expand the square and move \varphi(x) to the other side.

The resulting inequality says that u(x) = \|x\|^2 / 2 - \varphi(x) lies above an affine function of x whose slope is y. The slope is y and the intercept depends on \psi(y) and on \|y\|^2.

Therefore u is a supremum of affine functions, hence convex, by the same reason as Proposition 0.5. Where u is differentiable and the constraint is an equality, the slope matches, so y = \nabla u(x).

Absolute continuity of \mu lets one invoke Alexandrov’s theorem. A convex function is differentiable \mu-almost everywhere. Brenier (1991) proves that the resulting map is optimal and unique. These notes stop at the convexity step, which is the step Exercise 5 asks for.

∎

Key — chapter 2

Dual feasibility is the inequality \varphi_i + \psi_j \le C_{ij}. The dual objective is the dot product of the potentials with the masses. Equality of primal and dual values certifies optimality. For a quadratic cost the potential is convex and the map is its gradient.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The c-transform produces a feasible dual pair by construction.
  • Theorem 2.2 is linear programming duality for couplings, plus complementary slackness on the support.
  • Example 2.4 matches the primal cost 0.205 with an explicit dual vector. The unused edge has reduced cost 2.6.
  • Theorem 2.3 states Brenier’s map. The proof given here shows why the potential is convex. Uniqueness is cited from Brenier (1991).

3. Entropic regularization and the Sinkhorn iteration

The linear program of ch. 1 can have several optimal vertices, and a vertex is a sparse matrix. Entropic regularization replaces the linear objective by a strictly convex one. The minimizer is unique, it has the Gibbs form u_i K_{ij} v_j, and the multipliers are found by alternating rescaling.

Class labs code the same rescaling in PyTorch. The script below is NumPy. Peyré and Cuturi (2019) switch to logarithms of the scalings when \varepsilon is small enough to underflow.

Definition 3.1 — Entropic problem

Fix \varepsilon > 0 and a reference matrix Q with entries a_i b_j. Among couplings, minimize the linear cost plus \varepsilon times the Kullback-Leibler divergence of P from Q.

The divergence is the sum of P_{ij} \log(P_{ij}/Q_{ij}), with the convention 0 \log 0 = 0.

Entropy and KL agree on plans

On the transport polytope the marginals are fixed. There the divergence from the product ab^{\top} and the sum of P_{ij} \log P_{ij} differ by the constant \sum_i a_i \log a_i + \sum_j b_j \log b_j.

Cuturi (2013) minimizes the entropy form. Definition 3.1 minimizes the divergence form. The minimizing plans agree. The optimal values differ by \varepsilon times that constant.

The map t \mapsto t \log t is strictly convex, so the objective has at most one minimizer on the polytope. The polytope is compact and the objective is continuous up to the boundary, so a minimizer exists.

Proposition 3.2 — Gibbs form

The unique minimizer has the shape P_{ij} = u_i K_{ij} v_j for positive vectors u and v, with K_{ij} = \exp(-C_{ij}/\varepsilon).

Equivalently, for some dual multipliers, the plan is the product of the marginals and a Gibbs factor.

P_{ij} = a_i b_j \exp((\varphi_i + \psi_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon).

Proof. Proof of Proposition 3.2

Stationarity in the entry P_{ij}, with row and column multipliers, sets the derivative of the objective to zero. Differentiating t \log t produces a logarithm plus 1.

The extra \varepsilon coming from that 1 is absorbed into the multipliers. Solving for P_{ij} yields the exponential formula in the proposition.

Set u_i = a_i \exp(\varphi_i/\varepsilon) and v_j = b_j \exp(\psi_j/\varepsilon), and set K_{ij} = \exp(-C_{ij}/\varepsilon). The same plan is u_i K_{ij} v_j.

∎

Proposition 3.3 — One scaling restores a marginal

Let K have positive entries and let v be a positive vector. The choice u_i = a_i / (Kv)_i makes the matrix P_{ij} = u_i K_{ij} v_j have row sums a.

The symmetric update for v, with u held fixed, restores the column sums.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 3.3

The i-th row sum is u_i times (Kv)_i. Substituting u_i = a_i / (Kv)_i makes that product equal a_i.

Positivity of K and of v keeps the denominator positive, so the division is legal. The column update is the same argument applied to K^{\top}.

∎

Alternating the two updates is Sinkhorn’s algorithm. Peyré and Cuturi (2019) prove convergence when K is positive. The proof above is only the marginal identity used in the code.

Proposition 3.4 — The regularized value sits above the linear cost

The Kullback-Leibler divergence from the product is nonnegative. For every coupling, the entropic objective is at least the linear cost, hence at least the unregularized minimum.

The linear cost of the entropic plan is a different number, and it is also at least the unregularized minimum.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 3.4

Nonnegativity of the divergence is the standard information inequality, applied to the finite probability P and the product Q. Adding a nonnegative term cannot decrease the objective.

The minimum of a larger function is at least the minimum of the smaller one. That is the comparison of optimal values.

The entropic plan is itself a coupling, so its linear cost is at least the unregularized minimum. Example 3.5 shows a case where the inequality on the linear cost is strict.

∎

Feydy, Séjourné, Vialard, Amari, Trouvé and Peyré (2019) subtract the two self-costs, half each, so the Sinkhorn divergence vanishes when the two laws agree. Genevay, Peyré and Cuturi (2018) train generators with that debiased cost.

Example 3.5 — One step on a symmetric kernel

Take the cost with zeros on the diagonal and ones off the diagonal, equal masses 1/2, and \varepsilon = 1. The unregularized optimum is the diagonal plan, and its cost is 0. The kernel and the first scaling are computed by the script.

import numpy

C = numpy.array([[0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 0.0]])
a = numpy.array([0.5, 0.5])
K = numpy.exp(-C)
u = numpy.ones(2)
v = numpy.ones(2)
u = a / (K @ v)
v = a / (K.T @ u)
P = u[:, None] * K * v[None, :]
print(f"{float(numpy.sum(P * C)):.6f}")

The script prints 0.268941. By symmetry one step already meets both marginals. The off-diagonal entries of K equal e^{-1}, about 0.367879. The updated scaling is about 0.365529, and each off-diagonal mass of P is about 0.134471. Twice that mass is the printed cost, since those entries carry cost 1.

The blurred plan pays 0.268941 in transport cost. The unregularized plan pays 0. Proposition 3.4 said the inequality could be strict. This is the picture. Exercise 7 recomputes the same step by hand.

Key — chapter 3

Build the kernel with entries \exp(-C_{ij}/\varepsilon). Rescale the positive vectors until both margins match. The plan is that scaled kernel. Its linear cost is at least the unregularized cost, and the gap closes as the regularization goes to 0.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Entropic regularization makes the transport problem strictly convex. The minimizer is unique and positive.
  • On the polytope, KL against the product and Shannon entropy differ by a constant. Cuturi’s plan and Definition 3.1 share a minimizer.
  • Proposition 3.3 is the line of code in the script. The printed cost on the symmetric example is 0.268941, against an unregularized cost of 0.
  • The Sinkhorn divergence of Feydy and coauthors subtracts the self-costs so that a law has distance 0 from itself.

4. Dynamic formulation, gradient flows, and the Bures metric

The dynamic problem asks for a path of laws and a velocity that pushes the path. Benamou and Brenier showed that the cheapest kinetic action equals W_2^2. Gradient flows of entropy in that metric become the heat equation.

Gaussians are the family in which the distance is a closed formula. The official description calls that formula the Bures metric on Gaussian distributions. Dowson and Landau (1982) derived the Fréchet form used below.

Definition 4.1 — Dynamic formulation

A pair (\mu_t, v_t) solves the continuity equation when the time derivative of \mu_t equals minus the divergence of \mu_t v_t.

The Benamou-Brenier problem minimizes the action \int_0^1 \int \|v_t\|^2 \, d\mu_t \, dt over such pairs with endpoints \mu_0 and \mu_1.

The action charges squared speed. The dynamic problem for W_1 penalizes the unsquared speed instead. Definition 4.1 does not apply to W_1 unchanged.

Theorem 4.2 — The action equals the squared distance

For probabilities with finite second moments, the Benamou-Brenier infimum equals W_2^2(\mu_0, \mu_1).

An optimal static map produces a feasible path whose action equals the static cost. The reverse inequality is the analytic content of the theorem.

Proof. Proof of the easy inequality in Theorem 4.2

Let T be an optimal map for the squared cost, which exists under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.3. Define T_t(x) = (1-t) x + t\, T(x).

The Lagrangian velocity of the particle that starts at x is T(x) - x, and it does not depend on t. The action of this path equals the integral of \|T(x) - x\|^2 against \mu_0.

That integral is the Monge cost of T, which equals W_2^2. The dynamic infimum is therefore at most W_2^2. Benamou and Brenier (2000) and Santambrogio (2015) prove the matching lower bound.

∎

The path T_t is the displacement interpolation. On the line between two Gaussians it stays Gaussian. Exercise 9 computes the mean, the variance, and the action for a concrete pair. The static value is Example 4.5.

Definition 4.3 — Jordan-Kinderlehrer-Otto step

Given a functional F and a step \tau > 0, the next measure minimizes F(\nu) plus W_2^2(\mu, \nu) / (2\tau) over \nu.

The scheme is a proximal step for F in the 2-Wasserstein metric.

Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto (1998) prove that the entropy scheme converges to the heat equation as \tau goes to 0, and to the Fokker-Planck equation when a potential is present. ch. 5 uses that fact for diffusion models.

Proposition 4.4 — Gaussians on the line and in general

Let \sigma_1 and \sigma_2 be positive standard deviations. The squared 2-Wasserstein distance between N(m_1, \sigma_1^2) and N(m_2, \sigma_2^2) equals (m_1-m_2)^2 + (\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2.

For nondegenerate Gaussians in several dimensions, add the squared gap of the means to the trace below. The matrix S is the square root of \Sigma_1^{1/2} \Sigma_2 \Sigma_1^{1/2}.

\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_1) + \mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_2) - 2\, \mathrm{Tr}(S).

Proof. Proof of the one-dimensional formula

The increasing map T(x) = m_2 + (\sigma_2/\sigma_1)(x-m_1) pushes the first Gaussian to the second. It is the derivative of a convex quadratic, so Theorem 2.3 says it is optimal.

Write T(X) - X as (m_2-m_1) + (\sigma_2/\sigma_1 - 1)(X-m_1). The cross term vanishes because X-m_1 has mean 0.

The remaining second moment is (m_2-m_1)^2 + (\sigma_2-\sigma_1)^2. In one dimension the trace formula reduces to the same quantity, because of the identity below. Dowson and Landau (1982) prove the matrix case.

\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\sigma_1\sigma_2 = (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2.

∎

The official name for this geometry is the Bures metric. The formula above is W_2^2.

Example 4.5 — A distance equal to 2

Take N(0,1) and N(1,4). The variances are 1 and 4, so the standard deviations are 1 and 2.

Proposition 4.4 gives (0-1)^2 + (1-2)^2 = 2. Swapping the two laws leaves the value unchanged. Exercise 9 recovers the same 2 as the Benamou-Brenier action of the displacement interpolation, whose law at time t is Gaussian with mean t and variance (1+t)^2.

Key — chapter 4

On the line, W_2^2 between Gaussians is the squared gap of the means plus the squared gap of the standard deviations. In the dynamic picture that number is a kinetic action. The JKO step is the proximal map of a functional for this distance.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • The continuity equation is the constraint. The objective is kinetic energy. Theorem 4.2 identifies the optimal action with W_2^2.
  • The easy inequality is the displacement interpolation of an optimal map. The reverse inequality is cited from Benamou and Brenier (2000).
  • Definition 4.3 is one proximal step. For the entropy it converges to the heat equation.
  • Example 4.5 evaluates the Gaussian formula at 2. The standard deviations, not the variances, are subtracted.

5. Semi-discrete maps and generative models

One measure in a semi-discrete problem is a density and the other is a finite sum of atoms. The unknown is a weight vector. The models below are then read with ch. 2 and ch. 4. This chapter identifies the optimal-transport object. It does not train a network.

Definition 5.1 — Laguerre cell

Let the target be a finite sum of atoms, with mass b_j at location y_j, and let \psi be a weight vector. The Laguerre cell of site j is the set of points x that win the comparison below against every other site k.

\|x-y_j\|^2 - \psi_j \le \|x-y_k\|^2 - \psi_k.

Boundaries may be assigned to either adjacent site. For a source with a density, boundaries have mass 0.

Proposition 5.2 — A cell map with the right masses is optimal

Suppose \mu gives mass b_j to the cell of site j. The map that sends that cell to y_j is optimal for the squared cost.

The weight vector \psi is a dual potential on the target atoms.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 5.2

Define \varphi(x) as the minimum over k of \|x-y_k\|^2 - \psi_k. On the cell of site j this minimum equals \|x-y_j\|^2 - \psi_j, so \varphi(x) + \psi_j equals the squared distance.

For every other site the same minimum is at most \|x-y_k\|^2 - \psi_k. Adding \psi_k shows that \varphi(x) + \psi_k is at most the squared distance. The pair (\varphi, \psi) is dual feasible for Theorem 2.2 in its continuous form.

Integrate \varphi against \mu and \psi against the target. On each cell the sum \varphi + \psi_j equals the squared distance to y_j, so the dual objective equals the Monge cost of the cell map. The two bounds meet, and the map is optimal.

∎

Mérigot (2011) computes the weights by a damped Newton method. These notes prove the certificate in Proposition 5.2. The group project asks for that Newton loop on the unit square.

Proposition 5.3 — Two distinct point masses

If a and b are distinct real numbers, then W_1(\delta_a, \delta_b) = |a-b|.

The Jensen-Shannon divergence of the same pair equals \log 2. It does not depend on the locations beyond the fact that they differ.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 5.3

Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality says that W_1 is the supremum of f(a) - f(b) over 1-Lipschitz functions f. The gap f(a) - f(b) cannot exceed |a-b|.

The function f(x) = \mathrm{sign}(a-b)\, x is 1-Lipschitz and attains the gap |a-b|. The supremum equals the distance.

Let m be the average of the two point masses. Each point mass has density 2 with respect to m at its own atom. Each Kullback-Leibler divergence equals \log 2. The Jensen-Shannon divergence is the average of those two numbers, so it equals \log 2.

∎

Example 5.4 — A move that only transport sees

Proposition 5.3 with atoms at 0 and 1 gives W_1 = 1 and Jensen-Shannon divergence \log 2. Move the first atom from 0 to 0.1, still distinct from 1.

The divergence is still \log 2. The Wasserstein distance falls from 1 to 0.9. A training signal built from the divergence is constant along this move. A training signal built from W_1 has slope -1 while the atom approaches 1 from the left.

Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou (2017) minimize an estimate of W_1. The critic is the Kantorovich-Rubinstein potential, and they clip its weights to keep a Lipschitz bound. Goodfellow and coauthors (2014) used an objective whose population limit is the Jensen-Shannon divergence. Example 5.4 is why that score is a weak location signal.

Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020) learn a noise predictor for a Gaussian-smoothed data law. The Fokker-Planck equation of that process is the Wasserstein gradient flow of the relative entropy, as in Definition 4.3 and Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto (1998). Song and coauthors (2021) sample by reversing the associated stochastic differential equation. The sampler is not a Monge map of the clean data.

Chizat and Bach (2018) write a two-layer network as particles. In the many-particle limit, gradient descent follows the Wasserstein gradient flow of the mean-field risk and, under their homogeneity assumptions, converges to a global minimizer. This is the announcement’s topic of training by gradient flows. It is a mean-field limit.

Sander, Ablin, Blondel and Peyré (2022) replace row-softmax attention by the Sinkhorn scaling of Proposition 3.3, so the matrix is doubly stochastic. Geshkovski, Letrouit, Polyanskiy and Rigollet (2023) prove that tokens, treated as particles in a self-attention ODE, form clusters.

Key — chapter 5

A semi-discrete map sends each Laguerre cell to one atom. It is optimal when the cell masses equal the target weights. For generative models, W_1 between two point masses is the distance between them, while the Jensen-Shannon divergence stays at \log 2 as soon as the points differ.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Definition 5.1 cuts space into cells biased by weights. Proposition 5.2 turns the right masses into an optimal map.
  • Proposition 5.3 and Example 5.4 separate W_1 from the Jensen-Shannon divergence on point masses.
  • A Wasserstein GAN estimates the Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual. A diffusion model follows a Fokker-Planck gradient flow. Attention can be doubly stochastic by Sinkhorn.
  • Chizat and Bach (2018) identify mean-field training of a two-layer net with a Wasserstein gradient flow of the risk.

6. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is self-study, about 4 h, after the 18 h of class. It points at papers that extend ch. 1 through ch. 5. Each pointer names authors and a year. The notes are a map of questions, not a second course.

Peyré and Cuturi (2019) survey the statistical side of the theory. In high dimension the expected cost of replacing a law by its empirical measure decays like a negative power of the sample size, of order n^{-1/d}. That rate is the obstruction behind every estimator in this list. Choosing a better algorithm does not remove it.

Feydy, Séjourné, Vialard, Amari, Trouvé and Peyré (2019) define the Sinkhorn divergence so that it vanishes on the diagonal. The bias of the raw entropic cost is then gone. How \varepsilon should depend on the sample size, if the target really is W_2 rather than a blurred surrogate, is still a design choice. The one-dimensional test in the first solo project is a place to watch the gap.

Chizat, Peyré, Schmitzer and Vialard (2018) extend both the dynamic problem and the Kantorovich problem to measures whose total masses need not agree. The continuity equation gains a source term. The models of ch. 5 often compare laws that were normalized by hand. Unbalanced transport is the version that does not force that normalization.

A Brenier potential is convex. Amos, Xu and Kolter (2017) represent convex functions by input-convex networks. The representation is available for the potential in Theorem 2.3. Turning it into a map estimator with a high-dimensional guarantee that beats the empirical rate above is open. The citation is for the parameterization, not for an optimal-transport theorem.

Weight clipping, as used by Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou (2017), enforces a Lipschitz bound only through the size of the weights. Whether the trained critic is close to a Kantorovich potential, or only to some 1-Lipschitz function with a loose constant, is not settled by the population identity in Proposition 5.3. The identity says what the best critic can return. It does not say what gradient descent on a finite sample returns.

Geshkovski, Letrouit, Polyanskiy and Rigollet (2023) prove clustering for a class of self-attention dynamics. Sander, Ablin, Blondel and Peyré (2022) make the attention matrix doubly stochastic. Which interaction kernels force clustering, beyond the kernels already analyzed, is open. Token dynamics on a trained transformer are a further step. The ODE analysis does not by itself describe training.

Questions to carry into the papers

  1. How should \varepsilon scale with the sample size and the dimension when the quantity one wants is W_2, not the entropic surrogate of ch. 3?
  2. What rate, if any, is provable for a Brenier map whose convex potential is an input-convex network?
  3. After weight clipping, which distance to a true Kantorovich potential can be certified from the critic’s Lipschitz constant?
  4. Which attention kernels, beyond those in Geshkovski, Letrouit, Polyanskiy and Rigollet (2023), force the particles to form clusters?

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Empirical optimal transport in high dimension is limited by a rate of order n^{-1/d}.
  • Unbalanced formulations drop the equal-mass constraint. Debiased Sinkhorn costs fix \varepsilon only after a statistical choice.
  • Convex networks can represent Brenier potentials. Clipped critics and attention ODEs still leave open estimation and clustering questions.

Exercises

Levels rise from a direct application, through a short proof, to a problem with a longer argument. Exercises 2 and 6 are computational. The printed numbers in their solutions come from the fences below.

  1. ★ Two couplings with the same margins. Masses are (0.4, 0.6) and (0.5, 0.5). Show that the matrix with entries 0.4, 0, 0.1, 0.5 is a coupling, and that the product matrix is a different coupling.

    Solution. The first matrix has row sums 0.4 and 0.6 and column sums 0.5 and 0.5. The product matrix has entries 0.2, 0.2, 0.3, 0.3, with the same row sums and the same column sums. Both are nonnegative. They differ in every entry that was zero in the first matrix, so they are different couplings. This is Example 0.6.

  2. ★ Monotone matching in one dimension. Draw two samples of size 4 with the seeded generator below, sort each sample, and compare the squared cost of the sorted pairing with the squared cost of the independent coupling.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
x = numpy.sort(rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, size=4))
y = numpy.sort(rng.normal(1.5, 1.0, size=4))
monotone = float(numpy.mean((x - y) ** 2))
product = float(numpy.mean((x[:, None] - y[None, :]) ** 2))
print(f"{monotone:.6f}")
print(f"{product:.6f}")
*Solution.* The fence prints 3.589471 and then 3.929063. The first number is the squared cost of the sorted pairing, with equal weights. The second is the cost of the product coupling. The sorted cost is smaller.

Uncrossing is the reason a convex cost on the line prefers the sorted pairing. If $x_1 < x_2$ and the matched images are in the opposite order, swapping those images lowers the sum of squared gaps by twice the product of the two gaps. Exercise 5 and Theorem 2.3 put the same fact in the language of convex potentials.

  1. ★ The 2 by 2 squared cost. Using the atoms and the cost matrix of Example 1.5, minimize the linear cost over couplings and report the plan and the value.

    Solution. Plans are indexed by p \in [0, 0.4] as in Example 1.5. The slope in p equals -2.6, so the minimum is at p = 0.4. The entries are 0.4, 0, 0.1, and 0.5. The cost is 0.205. The product coupling costs 0.725 on the same matrix.

  2. ★ A target with no map. Let \nu be a point mass at 0 and let \mu be the average of point masses at 0 and at 1. Show that no map pushes \nu forward to \mu. Give a coupling and its cost for the ground cost |x-y|.

    Solution. A map sends the single atom of \nu to one location, so the push-forward is a single point mass. It cannot equal \mu. Send mass 1/2 from 0 to 0 and mass 1/2 from 0 to 1. The cost is 1/2. Every coupling has this cost, because the atom at 1 in the target must be fed from 0. This is Example 1.6.

  3. ★★ From a cost constraint to a convex potential. Assume \varphi(x) + \psi(y) \le \|x-y\|^2 / 2 for every x and y. Show that u(x) = \|x\|^2 / 2 - \varphi(x) is convex. State what this step is doing inside Theorem 2.3.

    Solution. Expand the square. The constraint says that u(x) lies above an affine function of x whose slope is y, for every y. A supremum of affine functions is convex, by Proposition 0.5.

Inside Theorem 2.3 this is why a quadratic cost produces a convex potential. Where u is differentiable and equality holds, the slope equals y, so the target point is the gradient of u at x.

  1. ★★ A seeded perturbation of the 2 by 2 plan. Add a normal perturbation of scale 0.01 to the cost of Example 1.5, using the seed below. Minimize the perturbed linear cost over the same segment of couplings.
import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
cost = numpy.array([[0.04, 2.25], [0.64, 0.25]], dtype=float)
cost = cost + rng.normal(scale=0.01, size=cost.shape)
best_cost = None
best_p = None
for p in numpy.linspace(0.0, 0.4, 40001):
    plan = numpy.array([[p, 0.4 - p], [0.5 - p, 0.1 + p]])
    value = float(numpy.sum(plan * cost))
    if best_cost is None or value < best_cost:
        best_cost = value
        best_p = float(p)
print(f"{best_cost:.6f}")
print(f"{best_p:.4f}")
*Solution.* The fence prints 0.206668 and then 0.4000. The minimizer stays at the same endpoint $p = 0.4$ as in the unperturbed problem, whose cost was 0.205. The noise of scale 0.01 moves the value and does not move the vertex. The row sums remain 0.4 and 0.6, and the column sums remain 0.5 and 0.5, because every matrix on the segment is a coupling.

  1. ★★ One Sinkhorn step by hand. For the cost of Example 3.5, with \varepsilon = 1 and equal masses 1/2, start from scalings equal to 1 and perform the update of Proposition 3.3. Compare the transport cost with the printed value.

    Solution. The off-diagonal entries of the kernel equal e^{-1}, about 0.367879. Each row sum of K against the initial vector of ones is about 1.367879. Dividing the mass 0.5 by that sum produces a scaling of about 0.365529. The off-diagonal mass is about 0.134471, and twice that mass is about 0.268941. The script in ch. 3 prints 0.268941. The unregularized cost is 0, so the inequality of Proposition 3.4 is strict.

  2. ★★ Why the entropic value sits above the linear minimum. Prove Proposition 3.4. On Example 3.5, say which of the two numbers 0 and 0.268941 is the unregularized cost and which is the transport cost of the entropic plan.

    Solution. The divergence from the product is nonnegative, so on every coupling the entropic objective is at least the linear cost. Minimizing preserves the inequality, and the linear minimum is the unregularized cost. In Example 3.5 the unregularized cost is 0 and the transport cost of the entropic plan is 0.268941. The second number is not the full entropic objective. The objective is larger by \varepsilon times a divergence.

  3. ★★★ Displacement interpolation of two Gaussians. Let the endpoints be N(0,1) and N(1,4). Find the displacement interpolation, its mean and variance at time t, and the Benamou-Brenier action. Compare the action with Example 4.5.

    Solution. The optimal map is T(x) = 1 + 2x. The interpolation is T_t(x) = (1-t)x + t T(x) = t + (1+t)x. Applied to a standard normal, this is Gaussian with mean t and variance (1+t)^2. At time 1 the law is N(1,4).

    The Lagrangian velocity is 1+x, and its second moment under the standard normal is 2. The action on the unit time interval is therefore 2. Example 4.5 gives the same value from the static formula. The path is feasible for Definition 4.1, and Theorem 4.2 says no path has a smaller action.

    The same interpolation is tied to the squared cost. Replacing the action by an integral of unsquared speed is the dynamic problem for W_1, and the constant-speed squared geodesic is not the object that problem asks for.

  4. ★★★ The critic of a Wasserstein GAN. Using Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou (2017) and Proposition 5.3, write the population objective of the critic for a point mass at 0 against a point mass at 1. Explain why a Jensen-Shannon score does not see the move from 0 to 0.1, and say what weight clipping is doing in that paper.

    Solution. The critic maximizes f(0) - f(1) over 1-Lipschitz functions. Proposition 5.3 says the maximum is 1, attained by f(x) = -x. The same proposition says the Jensen-Shannon divergence equals \log 2 for every pair of distinct point masses, including the pair at 0.1 and 1, while W_1 falls to 0.9. Clipping the critic’s weights is the device used in the 2017 paper to keep a Lipschitz bound during training. The population identity does not by itself certify the clipped network.
