0. Review: risk, gradients and image tensors

Every later chapter trains a map from an image to a vector by minimising a scalar loss. This chapter fixes the three tools those chapters call without proof: the empirical risk, the gradient of the logistic loss, and the parameter count of a convolution. A short fourth tool, the sample covariance, is the object that Ex. 2 prints.

The official prerequisites include probability, linear algebra, statistical learning, and an introductory deep learning course. The proofs below use only the chain rule, the sigmoid, and a symmetric matrix. If a step looks familiar from Deep Learning, the goal here is the exact derivative you will reuse, not a second survey of architectures.

0.1 Empirical risk

A predictor is a function h from an image to a label, or to a probability. A loss \ell scores one pair. The training set is a finite list of pairs (x_i, y_i).

Definition 0.1 — Empirical risk For a loss \ell and n pairs, the empirical risk of h is \hat R_n(h) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell(h(x_i), y_i).

Training chooses h inside a class by making this average small. The average is a random variable, because the pairs are a sample. Chapters 2 and 3 ask what changes when the sample and the test images do not share one distribution.

The population risk replaces the average by an expectation under the distribution you care about. The empirical risk is the estimator you can compute. When the class of h is large and n is small, a small training average does not imply a small test average. Medical sets are often in that regime: many pixels, few labelled patients. That count is why later chapters freeze part of the map.

0.2 The logistic gradient

Binary labels in this chapter are 0 or 1. The sigmoid sends a logit z to a probability. Write q for that probability. The loss below is the negative log-likelihood of the Bernoulli model.

Proposition 0.2 — Logistic gradient Let q = \sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1} and \ell(z, y) = - y \log q - (1 - y) \log (1 - q).

The derivative of the loss with respect to the logit is \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = q - y.

Proof. Differentiate in q first. The derivative is -y/q + (1-y)/(1-q).

The sigmoid satisfies \partial q / \partial z = q(1-q). Multiply the two derivatives.

The product is (1-q)(-y) + q(1-y). Expand it. The terms qy and -qy cancel, and the remainder is q - y. ∎

The second derivative in z equals q(1-q), which is non-negative. The loss is therefore convex in the logit. Chapter 2 uses that fact when the logit is a linear function of a frozen feature. A descent step adds a positive multiple of y - q to the logit. If the label is 1 and the probability is below 1, the logit increases.

Example 0.3 — Logit 1 and a positive label Take z = 1 and y = 1. Then q = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-1}} = 0.7311 to four decimals. Because y = 1, the loss equals -\log q = 0.3133.

Proposition 0.2 gives the derivative q - y = -0.2689. The sign is negative, so gradient descent increases z. At z = 0 the same label would give q = 0.5 and loss \log 2 = 0.6931, which is larger. The numbers match the direction of the derivative.

You will meet the same gap q - y as a vector when the model has several classes. The gradient of the cross-entropy in the vector of logits equals the vector of probabilities minus the one-hot label. The binary case is the one proved here. The multi-class case is the same cancellation, one coordinate at a time, and Ex. 8 computes the probabilities themselves.

0.3 Convolutions on an image tensor

A radiograph in this course is a matrix of intensities. A CT volume is a three-way array. A convolutional layer reuses one small kernel at every site. For one input channel, eight output channels and a kernel of size 3 \times 3, the layer has 3 \times 3 \times 8 kernel weights plus 8 biases, hence 80 parameters.

A dense layer that reads a 256 \times 256 radiograph and writes one number has 65536 weights before any hidden layer. Those weights are free to memorise a corner marker. The convolution cannot assign a private weight to one corner unless the depth and the receptive field grow enough to reach it. Representation learning, as the next chapter defines it, looks for a short vector computed by shared weights.

The same count explains patches. A cube of side 256 holds 16777216 voxels. One patient-level label supervises that whole cube if you train only the final class head. Self-supervision in ch. 6 builds losses on the voxels themselves so that the shared kernels see more than one bit of supervision per scan.

0.4 Sample covariance

Let the rows of the data matrix be centred. With n rows, the sample covariance divides the Gram matrix by n - 1. C = \frac{1}{n-1} X^\top X. The matrix C is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so its eigenvalues are non-negative. Exercise 2 uses 40 points in dimension 2 and prints the largest eigenvalue.

That eigenvalue is the largest variance of a unit-length linear image of the sample. ch. 1 proves the variational claim. You do not need the proof to read the printed number. You do need the divisor: replacing 39 by 40 changes the value.

Key — chapter 0 A descent step on the logistic loss moves the logit by the gap between the label and the predicted probability. \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = q - y.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The empirical risk is the average loss on the n training pairs. It is not the risk on a new hospital.
  • For a binary logistic loss, the derivative in the logit equals the predicted probability minus the label.
  • At logit 1 and label 1, the probability is 0.7311, the loss is 0.3133, and the derivative is -0.2689.
  • A 3 \times 3 convolution with eight filters has 80 parameters. A dense weight per pixel of a 256 \times 256 image has 65536 parameters for one output.

1. Representations, principal components and latent factors

A representation is the vector you hand to a simple predictor. Hand-built features were edges, textures and atlas coordinates. This chapter defines the map, proves which linear map maximises variance, and shows why an isotropic Gaussian latent code does not name its own axes. Those two results are the linear and the generative halves of the official topic “representation learning”, including disentangled generative models.

Bengio, Courville and Vincent (2013) describe the goal as a map that is discriminative and organised. Discriminative means a simple function of the vector predicts the label. Organised means a coordinate, or a direction, tracks a factor a person can name: a position, a scanner, a pathology. The course never assumes that training produces such axes by itself. Proposition 1.4 is the obstruction.

1.1 The map

Definition 1.1 — Representation A representation is a map \varphi from the image space to a vector space Z. A linear probe is a predictor that reads \varphi(x) and does not change \varphi. Fine-tuning changes \varphi as well. Both names return in ch. 2.

The dimension of Z is the length of the vector. For a radiograph, the raw dimension is the number of pixels. A representation of length 32 or 512 is the object the later losses train.

An autoencoder pairs \varphi with a decoder g. The reconstruction loss is the mean squared error between x and g(\varphi(x)). If both maps are linear and the loss is squared error, a global minimum reconstructs through a principal subspace (Baldi and Hornik, 1989). The axes inside that subspace are not unique. The next proposition fixes one variational choice of axis. It does not claim that every autoencoder returns that axis.

1.2 The leading principal component

Let \Sigma be a d \times d covariance, hence symmetric and positive semidefinite. A unit vector u defines a one-dimensional projection. The variance of that projection is the scalar u^\top \Sigma u.

Proposition 1.2 — Rayleigh quotient The maximum of u^\top \Sigma u over unit vectors u equals the largest eigenvalue \lambda_{\max}. A unit eigenvector for \lambda_{\max} attains it.

Proof. The spectral theorem writes \Sigma = Q \Lambda Q^\top with \Lambda diagonal and Q orthogonal. Set v = Q^\top u. Then v is a unit vector, because Q preserves Euclidean length.

The quadratic form becomes \sum_i \lambda_i v_i^2. Every \lambda_i is at most \lambda_{\max}, and \sum_i v_i^2 = 1, so the sum is at most \lambda_{\max}.

Equality holds when v is supported on the largest eigenvalue, which means u is a matching unit eigenvector. ∎

Pearson (1901) studied the closest-fit line. The proposition is the variance form of that line. The fraction of variance kept by the leading axis is \lambda_{\max} divided by the trace of \Sigma. The trace equals the sum of the coordinate variances.

Example 1.3 — A diagonal covariance Let \Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(4, 1). The eigenvalues are 4 and 1. The maximum variance is 4, at the first canonical vector. The trace is 5, so the leading axis keeps the fraction 4/5 = 0.8.

The point (2, 3) projects to (2, 0). The residual is (0, 3), and the squared error is 9. Projecting on the second axis instead keeps variance 1 and is worse for the Rayleigh quotient. A cloud with \Sigma equal to a multiple of the identity has no preferred axis. That cloud is the finite-sample cousin of the rotation in Proposition 1.4.

1.3 Latent factors and a rotation

A generative model draws a latent code z and then an image from a decoder. Disentanglement asks the coordinates of z to match separate factors. The standard normal prior does not, by itself, mark which coordinate is which.

Proposition 1.4 — Rotation ambiguity Let z have law N(0, I_k). Let R be an orthogonal matrix of the same size. Then Rz has the same law as z.

For any decoder f, the image f(z) equals the image obtained after rotating the code and undoing that rotation inside the decoder. The law of the image does not determine the coordinates of z.

Proof. The covariance of Rz is R I_k R^\top = I_k, and the mean stays 0. A Gaussian is determined by its mean and covariance, so Rz has law N(0, I_k).

Set u = Rz. Then f(z) = f(R^\top u). As z runs through its law, u runs through the same law, and the pushforward images coincide.

The coordinates of u are mixtures of the coordinates of z whenever R is not a signed permutation. The marginal law of the image is unchanged by that mixture. ∎

Definition 1.5 — Beta-VAE penalty The beta-VAE objective of Higgins and coauthors (2017) is a reconstruction term plus a positive multiple \beta of the Kullback-Leibler divergence from the encoder’s conditional law of z to the standard normal. The case \beta = 1 is the variational autoencoder of Kingma and Welling (2014). L_\beta(x) = L_{\mathrm{rec}}(x) + \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big).

The prior density of N(0, I_k) is constant on spheres. It does not prefer an orientation. Raising \beta presses the code toward that prior. It does not, by the proposition, choose axes. ch. 8 points to the empirical study of Locatello and coauthors (2019).

A diagonal Gaussian encoder is an extra constraint. The course does not claim that the diagonal removes the ambiguity. It claims only what Proposition 1.4 proves: the law of the images is not enough.

Key — chapter 1 Among unit directions, the variance of a linear projection is the Rayleigh quotient, and its maximum is the largest eigenvalue. \max_{\|u\|=1} u^\top \Sigma u = \lambda_{\max}.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • A representation is a map from the image to a vector. A probe reads the vector. Fine-tuning changes the map.
  • The leading principal component maximises variance among unit directions. On \mathrm{diag}(4, 1) it keeps the fraction 0.8.
  • A linear autoencoder can recover the principal subspace and still fail to name unique axes inside it.
  • If the latent prior is isotropic Gaussian, an orthogonal change of coordinates leaves the law of the images unchanged.

2. Transfer learning

Transfer learning reuses a representation trained on a source task. The target task in this course is often a small labelled medical set. The source is a large set of natural images, or another medical collection. This chapter defines the two ways to reuse the map and proves that a probe has a convex training problem. The practical question is the number of free weights compared with the number of labels.

Yosinski, Clune, Bengio and Lipson (2014) observed, on natural images, that early-layer features transfer more readily than the features in the last layer. Pan and Yang (2010) survey the vocabulary. Esteva and coauthors (2017) fine-tuned an ImageNet-pretrained network on clinical skin images. Raghu, Zhang, Kleinberg and Bengio (2019) compare ImageNet initialisation with training from scratch on medical tasks and find that the gain is largest when the labelled medical set is small. The proposition below is the convexity reason a small set still has a well-posed probe.

2.1 Probe and fine-tuning

Definition 2.1 — Frozen probe and fine-tuning Fix a representation \varphi. A frozen probe chooses a matrix W and a bias, and predicts from the logit vector W \varphi(x) + b. The map \varphi stays at its source value.

Fine-tuning lets the parameters inside \varphi move, usually from that same source value. The probe is a convex problem under Proposition 2.2. Fine-tuning is not, once \varphi contains a nonlinearity.

The source value may be the result of supervised training or of the self-supervised training in ch. 6. Transfer does not require the source labels to share a meaning with the target labels. It requires the vector \varphi(x) to make the target easier. If the vector mostly encodes a scanner, transfer carries the scanner. That failure is the subject of ch. 3.

2.2 Convexity

Proposition 2.2 — The probe is convex Fix \varphi. For binary labels, the mean logistic loss of a probe is a convex function of the probe weights.

Proof. By the computation in Proposition 0.2, the scalar loss is convex in the logit. Its second derivative in the logit equals q(1-q), which is at least 0.

The logit of a probe is an affine function of the weights: a dot product with the frozen vector \varphi(x), plus a bias. An affine precomposition preserves convexity.

The mean of convex functions is convex. A local minimum of the probe loss is therefore a global minimum. ∎

The same argument applies to the multi-class cross-entropy, because that loss is convex in the vector of logits and the logits are affine in W. The course uses the binary proof, which is complete, as the model. Fine-tuning breaks the argument at the first step that is false: the map from the inner weights to the logit is no longer affine.

Example 2.3 — Forty labels and a vector of length 512 Suppose \varphi(x) lies in \mathbb{R}^{512} and the target has two classes. A probe has 512 \times 2 matrix entries and 2 biases, hence 1026 parameters. The training set in this example has 40 labelled images.

Forty images cannot determine 1026 weights without a preference for small weights, but the objective stays convex, so gradient descent does not stop at a bad local minimum of the probe. A network with 10^{6} trainable weights on the same 40 images has far more free parameters than labels, and the objective is not convex. Raghu and coauthors (2019) is the empirical companion: look first at whether the labelled set is small before you pay for full fine-tuning.

Ex. 4 counts a smaller encoder, from length 784 to length 32 and then to 10 classes, and gets 25450 parameters. The hidden vector of length 32 is the representation. The last layer is a probe on it.

On a medical set, “small” is a comparison of labelled patients with free weights, not a universal cutoff. The course states no official cutoff. The worked comparison is 40 images against 1026 probe weights and against 10^{6} fine-tuned weights.

Key — chapter 2 With the representation held fixed, the mean logistic loss of a linear probe is a convex function of the probe weights.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • A frozen probe trains a linear classifier on a fixed vector. Fine-tuning also moves the map that builds the vector.
  • The probe loss is convex in its weights because the logit is affine and the logistic loss is convex in the logit.
  • A two-class probe on a vector of length 512 has 1026 parameters. Forty labels do not determine a network with 10^{6} weights.
  • Published comparisons find the largest gain from ImageNet initialisation when the labelled medical set is small.

3. Domain adaptation

A representation trained on one distribution can fail on another even when the label set has the same names. Zech and coauthors (2018) document that failure for pneumonia classifiers across hospitals. This chapter writes the failure as a change of distribution, proves the identity that reweights a source sample when only the covariates shift, and defines the H-divergence that a domain classifier estimates.

The official topic is domain adaptation. The method named at the end, domain-adversarial training (Ganin and coauthors, 2016), trains the representation to make the divergence small. The proved statement is the weight identity. The adversarial method is the algorithm you implement when the identity’s hypothesis fails.

3.1 Covariate shift

Definition 3.1 — Covariate shift Write p_S and p_T for the source and target distributions of the pair (X, Y). Covariate shift means p_S(y \mid x) = p_T(y \mid x) whenever both sides are defined, while p_S(x) and p_T(x) may differ.

Label shift is the symmetric case: the class-conditional laws of x agree, and the class frequencies differ. A scanner that changes how a disease looks in the image breaks covariate shift, because the conditional of Y given X has changed.

Under covariate shift, a loss computed on source pairs is the wrong expectation for the target, but each source pair can be reweighted. The weight is the ratio of the two densities of X.

Proposition 3.2 — Importance weights Assume covariate shift, and assume p_T(x) = 0 whenever p_S(x) = 0. For any predictor h and any non-negative loss, R_T(h) = \mathbb{E}_S\left[ \frac{p_T(X)}{p_S(X)} \, \ell(h(X), Y) \right].

Proof. Start from the target expectation of the loss. Factor the target joint density as p_T(y \mid x) \, p_T(x).

Replace p_T(y \mid x) by p_S(y \mid x). Multiply and divide by p_S(x). The hypothesis on the supports keeps the ratio finite.

The integral is now the source expectation of the weighted loss. ∎

The identity says the target risk is a weighted source risk. It does not say the weights are easy to estimate. A ratio of densities in a high-dimensional image space is a hard estimation problem. That is why adaptation often matches distributions in the representation space rather than in the pixel space.

Example 3.3 — A one-dimensional weight Let the source law of X be standard normal and the target law be normal with mean 0.5 and variance 1. At x = 1 the log weight equals -0.5(1-0.5)^2 + 0.5(1)^2 = 0.375. The weight is e^{0.375} = 1.455 to three decimals.

A source point at x = 1 counts 1.455 times more in the target risk than in the source risk. Points on the far left are down-weighted. Ex. 6 draws a three-dimensional version of a mean shift and prints the norm of the difference of sample means.

If a balanced domain classifier has error 0.2, then |1 - 2 \times 0.2| = 0.6. Under Definition 3.4 that gap of 0.6 witnesses a divergence of at least 1.2.

3.2 The H-divergence

Definition 3.4 — H-divergence For a class \mathcal{H} of classifiers with values in \{0, 1\}, d_{\mathcal{H}}(D_S, D_T) = 2 \sup_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \big| \Pr_S(h = 1) - \Pr_T(h = 1) \big|.

The divergence lies in the interval from 0 to 2. It is 0 when no classifier in the class can tell the two distributions apart.

Train a domain classifier on a balanced mixture, with source labelled 1 and target labelled 0. Its error \varepsilon satisfies 1 - 2\varepsilon = \Pr_S(h=1) - \Pr_T(h=1). The absolute value is the gap inside the supremum. Twice that gap is the contribution of this one classifier to the divergence. If the classifier attains the supremum, the divergence equals that contribution.

Ben-David, Blitzer, Crammer, Kulesza, Pereira and Vaughan (2010) use this divergence to bound the target risk by the source risk, the divergence, and the joint error of the best hypothesis. The course does not reprove that uniform-convergence bound. It uses the definition, which you can estimate with a classifier, and the weight identity, which is proved.

Ganin and coauthors (2016) attach a domain classifier to the representation and reverse the gradient of the domain loss in the encoder. The encoder keeps the label loss small and pushes the divergence down. Tzeng, Hoffman, Saenko and Darrell (2017) train a separate adversarial objective with the same aim. Use those algorithms when covariate shift is false. A new scanner often makes it false.

When the weight identity does not apply If the scanner changes p(y \mid x), reweighting p(x) leaves a biased target risk. A corner token that predicts the hospital is a feature you want the divergence term to erase, not a covariate you want to reweight into the test set.

Key — chapter 3 Under covariate shift, the target risk equals a source expectation with weight p_T(x)/p_S(x).

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Covariate shift keeps p(y \mid x) and lets p(x) change. A scanner that changes the look of the disease breaks that hypothesis.
  • When the hypothesis holds, each source loss is multiplied by the density ratio of the covariates.
  • At x = 1, a shift of the mean from 0 to 0.5 gives the weight 1.455.
  • A domain error of 0.2 on a balanced mixture witnesses an H-divergence of at least 1.2 under Definition 3.4.

4. Multi-task learning

Multi-task learning trains one representation for several predictions. A medical network may segment an organ and regress its volume from the same encoder. Caruana (1997) argued that the extra tasks act as a preference on the shared map: a direction that helps only one task and hurts the others is a poor shared feature. This chapter writes the joint loss, proves it stays convex when each task is convex and the weights are non-negative, and computes a case where the task gradients disagree.

4.1 The joint objective

Definition 4.1 — Shared encoder Let \varphi_\theta be an encoder with parameters \theta. Task t has a head h_t and a loss L_t(\theta). Non-negative weights w_t define L(\theta) = \sum_t w_t L_t(\theta).

The heads may have private parameters. The shared object is \theta. If a weight is zero, that task does not affect \theta.

Kendall, Gal and Cipolla (2018) replace hand-chosen weights, in a regression setting, by a penalty that depends on a positive scale \sigma_t for each task. For squared losses their objective has the form \sum_t \left( \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2} L_t + \log \sigma_t \right).

A task with a large estimated noise \sigma_t receives a small coefficient in front of L_t. The \log \sigma_t term stops the scale from drifting to infinity. The course uses this formula as their weighting, not as a theorem proved here. The convexity proposition uses fixed weights.

Proposition 4.2 — Non-negative weights preserve convexity If every L_t is convex and every w_t is non-negative, then L is convex.

Proof. Fix two parameter vectors and a scalar \alpha in the unit interval. Convexity of L_t gives L_t at the mixture bounded by the mixture of the values.

Multiply by w_t. The inequality direction is unchanged because w_t is non-negative. Sum over tasks.

The left side is L at the mixture. The right side is the mixture of the values of L. That is convexity of L. ∎

The encoder of a deep network is not a convex function of \theta. The proposition applies to the heads when the encoder is frozen, and to any task loss that is already convex in the shared parameters. It tells you that the obstruction in the next example is the sign of the gradients, not a failure of the algebra of the weights.

Example 4.3 — Opposite gradients Let the shared parameter be a scalar. The derivative of task 1 is 4 and the derivative of task 2 is -1. With equal weights the joint derivative is 3. A small descent step decreases the parameter, which decreases task 1 and increases task 2.

With weights 1 and 4 the joint derivative is 1 \times 4 + 4 \times (-1) = 0. The weighted objective is stationary, and neither loss is forced down.

No step decreases both losses at this point. A decrease of L_1 needs a negative step, because the derivative is positive. A decrease of L_2 needs a positive step, because its derivative is negative. The two demands contradict. Ex. 7 asks you to write this argument.

For the Kendall weighting, take task losses 0.4 and 1.2 with scales 1 and 2. The objective equals 0.2 + 0.15 + \log 2, which is 1.043 to three decimals.

The noisier task is down-weighted from 1.2 to 0.15 inside that sum, before the \log 2 penalty.

In a segmentation-plus-volume model, a step that improves the volume and erodes the mask is the situation in the example. The remedy the definition allows is a different pair (w_1, w_2), or a stop on the shared encoder while one head finishes. The course does not claim a universal best weight.

Key — chapter 4 The gradient of a weighted sum of task losses is the same weighted sum of the task gradients. Opposite signs cannot be removed by positive weights.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • One encoder can feed several heads. The joint loss is a non-negative combination of the task losses.
  • Non-negative weights preserve convexity. They do not preserve a descent direction for every task.
  • Derivatives 4 and -1 give a joint derivative 3 at equal weights, and 0 at weights 1 and 4.
  • With scales 1 and 2 and losses 0.4 and 1.2, the Kendall objective equals 1.043.

5. Knowledge distillation

Knowledge distillation trains a small network, the student, to match a large network, the teacher. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015) replace hard labels by soft targets, the teacher’s class probabilities at a temperature T. A hospital may run the teacher once, offline, and ship the student. This chapter defines the soft targets, proves the entropy bound that says how uncertain a probability vector can be, and computes the same logits at two temperatures.

Uncertainty enters here as the entropy of the predictive distribution. A vector near the uniform distribution has high entropy and does not commit to a class. Calibration, ensembles and dropout estimators are left to ch. 8. The bound proved now is what makes the temperature meaningful.

5.1 Soft targets

Definition 5.1 — Temperature softmax For logits z_1, \ldots, z_K and a temperature T > 0, q_i(T) = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(z_j / T)}.

The soft target is the teacher’s probability vector at that temperature. The student uses the same temperature while it matches the vector. At test time the temperature returns to 1. As the temperature grows, each logit divided by the temperature tends to 0, so the vector tends to the uniform vector.

Hinton, Vinyals and Dean multiply the soft-target loss by T^2 when they form the training gradient. The factor keeps the scale of the gradient from collapsing as T grows. The course records their factor as part of the method. The proved statement is the entropy bound, which does not depend on that factor.

Proposition 5.2 — Entropy bound Let q be a probability vector on K classes, and let H(q) = -\sum_i q_i \log q_i. Then H(q) \le \log K, with equality at the uniform vector.

Proof. Let u be the uniform vector, with every coordinate equal to 1/K. The Kullback-Leibler divergence \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, u) is non-negative.

Expand the divergence. It equals -H(q) + \log K, because -\log u_i = \log K and the q_i sum to 1.

Non-negativity rearranges into H(q) \le \log K. Equality holds when q = u. ∎

The logarithm is natural in the proof and in the numerical values below. A change of base multiplies both sides by the same constant. For K = 3, the bound is \log 3 = 1.099 to three decimals. A soft target that reaches an entropy above that number is wrongly computed.

Example 5.3 — Two temperatures Take logits 2, 0 and -1. At temperature 1 the probabilities are 0.844, 0.114 and 0.042 to three decimals. The entropy is 0.524.

At temperature 4 the probabilities are 0.481, 0.292 and 0.227. The entropy is 1.048. The mass has moved toward the uniform vector, and the entropy has moved toward 1.099, in agreement with Definition 5.1 and Proposition 5.2.

A student with logits 1.5, 0.2 and -0.5, also read at temperature 4, has probabilities 0.429, 0.310 and 0.260. The Kullback-Leibler divergence from the teacher to this student is 0.0058 to four decimals. Exercise 8 asks for the two teacher vectors and the two entropies.

The hard label is a one-hot vector. Its entropy is 0. Matching only the hard label throws away the teacher’s ranking of the wrong classes. The soft target keeps that ranking. Distillation is useful when the student has fewer parameters than the teacher and you still want the ranking. The project in the assessment section fixes a synthetic image task where that comparison is measurable.

Key — chapter 5 Raising the temperature flattens the softmax toward the uniform vector, and no probability vector has entropy above \log K.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Soft targets are the teacher’s softmax probabilities at a temperature T. Test-time prediction uses T = 1.
  • The entropy of a probability vector on K classes is at most \log K.
  • Logits 2, 0 and -1 give entropy 0.524 at temperature 1 and entropy 1.048 at temperature 4.
  • The factor T^2 in front of the soft loss is the scaling stated by Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015).

6. Self-supervised learning and foundation models

Self-supervised learning builds a representation without class labels. A pretext task defines the loss from the image alone. A foundation model, in the sense used here, is a network pretrained on a large collection and then adapted to several downstream tasks with few labels. This chapter proves a one-line form of the contrastive loss and places rotation prediction, contrastive pairs and masked patches on one ladder.

Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton (2020) train on two random views of one image and treat the other images in the batch as negatives (SimCLR). He, Fan, Wu, Xie and Girshick (2020) keep a memory of negatives (MoCo). He, Chen, Xie, Li, Dollár and Girshick (2022) mask patches and reconstruct them (MAE). van den Oord, Li and Vinyals (2018) introduced the InfoNCE loss that SimCLR uses. Gidaris, Singh and Komodakis (2018) use a simpler pretext: predict which of the four rotations was applied.

6.1 The contrastive loss

Definition 6.1 — One-negative InfoNCE Let s^+ be the similarity of an anchor with a positive view, and let s^- be the similarity with one negative. For a temperature \tau > 0, \ell = - \log \frac{\exp(s^+ / \tau)}{\exp(s^+ / \tau) + \exp(s^- / \tau)}.

A full batch replaces the single negative by a sum. The one-negative case is the one proved below. Similarity in SimCLR is a dot product of normalised vectors.

Proposition 6.2 — Softplus form The one-negative loss equals \ell = \log \big(1 + \exp((s^- - s^+) / \tau)\big).

Proof. Divide the numerator and the denominator of Definition 6.1 by \exp(s^+ / \tau).

The fraction becomes 1 / (1 + \exp((s^- - s^+)/\tau)). The loss is the negative log of that fraction.

Negative log of a reciprocal is the log of the denominator. That denominator is the expression in the statement. ∎

The loss is the softplus of the margin (s^- - s^+)/\tau. It is always positive. It is small when the positive similarity exceeds the negative one by several times \tau. The gradient in s^+ is negative, so descent increases s^+. The gradient in s^- is positive, so descent decreases s^-.

Example 6.3 — A margin of 1 Take s^+ = 1, s^- = 0 and \tau = 0.5. The margin over temperature is (0 - 1)/0.5 = -2. The loss equals \log(1 + e^{-2}) = 0.1269 to four decimals.

If the negative had been identical to the positive, the margin would be 0 and the loss would be \log 2 = 0.6931. The gap between 0.1269 and 0.6931 is the reward for separating the negative. Ex. 9 asks what happens when the “negative” is a second crop of the same tumour.

6.2 From a pretext task to a foundation model

Rotation prediction is a four-way classification. You rotate the image by 0, 90, 180 or 270 degrees and ask the network to name the angle. The loss is an ordinary cross-entropy. The representation is the vector before the four-way head. The method needs no second view and no large batch. It is a baseline, not the strongest pretext in the papers above.

Contrastive pretraining needs an agreement about what a positive pair is. Two crops of one photograph are a standard positive pair. Two crops of one tumour are a dangerous negative if the batch happens to treat them as distinct images. The softplus then pushes those two tumour vectors apart. A later linear probe must map both vectors to the same label. Ex. 9 develops that failure.

Masked autoencoders hide a fraction of the patches and train the network to reconstruct the missing intensities. The loss is a reconstruction error on the hidden sites, in the spirit of the autoencoder of ch. 1, with a transformer encoder as in ch. 7. The representation is the encoder’s output, not the reconstructed pixels.

A cube of side 256 has 16777216 voxels and often one patient-level label. The pretext loss supervises the encoder from the voxels. The downstream probe supervises it from the label, sometimes from a few dozen labels, which is the regime of Proposition 2.2. Calling the pretrained encoder a foundation model records the reuse across tasks. It does not record a guarantee that the medical probe will beat a probe trained from scratch. That comparison is an experiment, and it is one of the projects.

Key — chapter 6 With one negative, the contrastive loss is the softplus of (s^- - s^+)/\tau. Descent increases the positive similarity and decreases the negative one.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • A pretext task defines a loss from the image alone. Rotation prediction is a four-way cross-entropy.
  • With one negative, InfoNCE equals \log(1 + \exp((s^- - s^+)/\tau)).
  • Similarities 1 and 0 at temperature 0.5 give the loss 0.1269.
  • A foundation model is a pretrained encoder reused on later tasks. A 256^3 volume has 16777216 voxels and may have a single label.

7. Attention, transformers and explanations

Attention builds a new vector for each token as a weighted sum of value vectors. Transformers stack that operation with a shared map applied at every token (Vaswani and coauthors, 2017). Dosovitskiy and coauthors (2021) cut an image into patches and run a transformer on the patches. This chapter proves that the weights form a convex combination, computes a two-token example, and separates that weight from a gradient-based localisation map.

Interpretability, in the sense of the official list, is the question of which input region the prediction used. Uncertainty, already bounded in ch. 5, is the entropy of the class distribution. Neither question is answered by reading a single attention weight. The zero-value calculation shows why.

7.1 Scaled dot-product attention

Definition 7.1 — Attention Let q be a query vector and let (k_i, v_i) be key-value pairs, with keys of the same dimension d as the query. The weights and the output are w_i = \frac{\exp(q \cdot k_i / \sqrt{d})}{\sum_j \exp(q \cdot k_j / \sqrt{d})}, \qquad o = \sum_i w_i v_i.

The scale \sqrt{d} keeps the dot products from growing with the dimension. The values may have a different dimension from the keys.

Proposition 7.2 — Convex combination The weights are non-negative and sum to 1. The output is a convex combination of the value vectors. If a value vector is 0, that token does not change the output.

Proof. Each exponential is positive, and the weights divide by their sum, so each w_i is positive and the sum of the w_i is 1. A weighted sum with those two properties is a convex combination.

If v_i = 0, the term w_i v_i is the zero vector. Dropping it does not change o. The weight w_i can stay large. ∎

Example 7.3 — Two tokens Take dimension 1, query 1, keys 1 and 0, and values 2 and 5. The scores are 1 and 0. The weights are e/(1+e) = 0.7311 and 1/(1+e) = 0.2689 to four decimals.

The output is 0.7311 \times 2 + 0.2689 \times 5 = 2.8068. Set the first value to 0 and the second value to 2e + 5 = 10.4366. The weights are unchanged, because they do not depend on the values. The output is unchanged, because 0.2689 \times 10.4366 = 2.8068. The first token still holds weight 0.7311 and contributes nothing. Ex. 10 asks for this construction.

A vision transformer cuts an image into patches of size 16 \times 16. On a 224 \times 224 image there are 14 \times 14 = 196 patches. A class token brings the sequence length to 197. Each patch is flattened and multiplied by a matrix. A learned vector is added to mark position. A block adds the attention output back to the tokens and applies the same two-layer map at each token, with a residual link around each of those two steps (Dosovitskiy and coauthors, 2021).

7.2 Localisation and entropy

Definition 7.4 — Grad-CAM weight Let A be one feature map and let y be the score of a class before the softmax. The Grad-CAM weight of Selvaraju and coauthors (2017) is the average, over the spatial sites, of \partial y / \partial A. The localisation map is the positive part of the weighted sum of the maps.

On a map with two sites, values 1 and 4, and gradients 0 and 2, the average gradient is 1. The localisation map equals the feature map itself, the pair (1, 4). The second site is brighter because it was brighter in the feature map, and the weight happened to be positive.

The average gradient can be negative. The positive part then erases that map. A large attention weight is not this gradient. Proposition 7.2 shows a large weight on a zero value. ch. 8 cites the sanity checks that ask whether a localisation map changes when the network is randomised.

Predictive uncertainty for a single softmax is the entropy H(q) from Proposition 5.2. Monte Carlo dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016) draws T random masks at test time, averages the probability vectors, and reads the entropy of the average. The average is \bar q = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} q^{(t)}.

The entropy of \bar q is at most \log K by the same proposition. A value near the bound means the averaged predictor does not commit. Deep ensembles average networks trained from different initialisations (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017). Both estimators are used in ch. 8. The formula to compute by hand in this chapter remains the two-token attention output.

Key — chapter 7 Attention outputs a convex combination of the values. A token with value zero does not move the output, whatever its weight.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Scaled dot-product attention builds non-negative weights that sum to 1, then sums the values with those weights.
  • Keys 1 and 0 with query 1 give weights 0.7311 and 0.2689, and values 2 and 5 give the output 2.8068.
  • Replacing the values by 0 and 10.4366 keeps both the weights and the output. The large weight explains nothing by itself.
  • A Grad-CAM weight is an average gradient of the class score. Predictive entropy is the uncertainty of a probability vector.

8. Research directions and open problems

The eight lectures proved the identities you can check by hand. This chapter lists three research questions that those identities do not close, and a reading order for about four hours. The voice is the literature’s. Nothing here is a result of these notes.

8.1 Disentangled factors

Proposition 1.4 shows that an isotropic Gaussian prior does not mark axes. Higgins and coauthors (2017) increase the weight \beta on the divergence to the standard normal and report latent coordinates that track simple visual factors on synthetic scenes. Locatello, Bauer, Lucic, Gelly, Schölkopf, Bachem and coauthors (2019) argue that unsupervised disentanglement is not identified without an inductive bias on the model class, and that random seeds and hyperparameters can dominate the comparison of methods.

The open question for medical images is narrower. A factor you can name, such as scanner vendor against lesion size, is useful only if it stays stable when the decoder class changes. The rotation proof says the law of the images will not force that stability. An inductive bias might. Which bias is enough for radiographs is not settled by the proposition.

8.2 Uncertainty

Proposition 5.2 bounds the entropy of one softmax. Gal and Ghahramani (2016) read a trained dropout network as a variational approximation and average several stochastic passes. Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell (2017) average an ensemble instead. Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger (2017) show that modern networks can be accurate and still poorly calibrated: the predicted probability is not the frequency of correctness.

The open question is whether a student distilled in ch. 5 can match the teacher’s entropy, not only its argmax. Soft targets carry some of that information. A quantitative guarantee, under a stated distance between student and teacher, is not the entropy bound.

8.3 Explanations that survive a check

Definition 7.4 gives a Grad-CAM weight, and Proposition 7.2 shows an attention weight that does not change the output. Selvaraju and coauthors (2017) define the localisation map used in many medical papers. Adebayo, Gilmer, Muelly, Goodfellow, Hardt and Kim (2018) propose a sanity check: randomise the model in a cascade and ask whether the map changes. A map that stays still when the weights change is not an explanation of those weights. Jain and Wallace (2019) argue that attention weights remain a weak explanation even when they correlate with other measures.

The open question is which map, on a chest radiograph, changes under their randomisation and still highlights the lesion rather than the corner token of Zech and coauthors (2018). A passing sanity check is not a clinical validation. It is a filter that removes maps independent of the model.

8.4 A four-hour reading order

  • First hour. Reread Proposition 1.4. Read the objective in Higgins and coauthors (2017) and write the role of \beta in one paragraph of your own.
  • Second hour. Read the claim of Locatello and coauthors (2019) that unsupervised comparisons need a bias. List one bias you would impose on a radiograph model, and one failure if the bias is wrong.
  • Third hour. Compare one stochastic pass of Gal and Ghahramani (2016) with the ensemble average of Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell (2017). State which average you can run on a laptop.
  • Fourth hour. Apply the randomisation question of Adebayo and coauthors (2018) to the two-site map in Definition 7.4. Say what a map that ignores the gradient would look like on that example.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • An isotropic prior plus a flexible decoder does not identify latent axes. Extra bias may, and may also be wrong.
  • Entropy bounds one softmax. Calibration and ensembles ask whether the number is a frequency.
  • A localisation map that does not change when the network is randomised is not an explanation of that network.

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 2 hours, one handwritten sheet of notes, no computer and no phone. Three questions, 20 points. An oral exam, if chosen because of the number of students, asks the same three questions.

  1. Conceptual question (6 points). Define a representation. Say what a frozen linear probe is, and why its logistic loss is a convex function of its weights. A labelled set has 40 images. A probe has about 10^3 weights and a fine-tuned network has about 10^6 weights. Give one reason to fit the probe first.

    Expected: Definition 1.1 and Proposition 2.2. The logit is affine in the probe weights and the logistic loss is convex in the logit, so a local minimum is global. The network has far more free weights than labels, so the probe is the better-posed first fit. Cite the small-set regime discussed with Raghu and coauthors (2019).

  2. Computation (7 points). For logits 2, 0 and -1, give the softmax at temperatures 1 and 4, to three decimals, and both entropies, to three decimals. Which vector is more uncertain, and what number can the entropy not exceed?

    Expected: Example 5.3. Temperature 1 gives 0.844, 0.114, 0.042 and entropy 0.524. Temperature 4 gives 0.481, 0.292, 0.227 and entropy 1.048. The second vector is more uncertain. Proposition 5.2 forbids an entropy above \log 3 = 1.099.

  3. Short proof (7 points). Define scaled dot-product attention for one query and several key-value pairs. Prove that the output is a convex combination of the values. Deduce that a value equal to the zero vector does not change the output.

    Expected: Definition 7.1 and Proposition 7.2. The exponential is positive and the weights divide by their sum, so the weights are positive and sum to 1. The output is that weighted sum of values. A zero value adds the zero vector. The matching weight may stay large.
