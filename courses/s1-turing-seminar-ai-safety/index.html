Audience: a student with a good M1 in mathematics, computer science or physics who has trained a neural network and wants to understand, with numbers and proofs where they exist, why the safety of general-purpose AI is a technical problem.

Empirical first: pick the best of n answers with a learned score, and the true quality of the pick first rises with n, then falls back towards chance when the score’s errors are heavy-tailed (ch. 4, after Gao, Schulman and Hilton, 2023). A linear classifier that is 98 % accurate on the scikit-learn digits changes its answer when each pixel moves by about 15 % of its range (ch. 5). This course explains such failures, and the tools built to detect and prevent them.

The Turing Seminar is an Opening course: it asks where the fast progress of general-purpose AI is leading, what could go wrong, and what engineers can do about it. The course has three parts: capabilities and forecasts (ch. 1–2), benefits and risks (ch. 3–5), and solutions, namely understanding, evaluating and regulating models (ch. 6–8). The course is given by Loucas Pillaud-Vivien (ENPC, Cermics) and Léo Dana (Inria, Sierra), as listed on master-mva.com.

Related MVA pages: Foundations of Large Language Models for the models themselves, Regulating AI for the law, and Responsible Machine Learning and Multimodal Explainable AI for fairness and explanations.

0. Review: probability, information and optimisation

Five M1 tools return throughout the course: the Kullback–Leibler divergence (ch. 1, 2 and 4), Gaussian conditioning (ch. 4), power laws on log–log axes (ch. 1 and 2), concentration of averages (ch. 7) and dual norms (ch. 5). This chapter states them in the form used later. The refreshers Probability and Statistics and Linear Algebra, Analysis and Optimization give more detail.

0.1 Entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence

Definition 0.1 — Entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence Let p and q be probability distributions on a finite set \mathcal{Y}, with q(y) > 0 wherever p(y) > 0. Their entropy, cross-entropy and Kullback–Leibler divergence are H(p) = -\sum_y p(y)\log p(y), \qquad H(p, q) = -\sum_y p(y)\log q(y), \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \sum_y p(y)\log\frac{p(y)}{q(y)} = H(p, q) - H(p). Logarithms are natural, so these quantities are in nats.

A language model is trained to minimise the cross-entropy between the data distribution and its own predictions. The decomposition above shows that the best achievable loss is the entropy of the data, an “irreducible” term that reappears in the scaling laws of ch. 1.

Proposition 0.2 — Gibbs’ inequality For all p and q as above, \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0, \quad \text{with equality if and only if } p = q .

Proof.

  • The function \log is strictly concave. Apply Jensen’s inequality to the random variable q(Y)/p(Y) with Y \sim p.
  • This gives -\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\log(q/p)] \le \log \mathbb{E}_p[q/p] \le \log 1 = 0 .
  • Equality in Jensen’s inequality forces q/p to be constant on the support of p, and the constant is 1 since both sum to 1. ∎

0.2 Gaussian conditioning

Proposition 0.3 — Conditional expectation of a Gaussian pair Let (V, U) be a Gaussian vector with \mathrm{Var}(U) > 0. Then \mathbb{E}[V \mid U = u] = \mathbb{E}[V] + \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(V, U)}{\mathrm{Var}(U)}\,\big(u - \mathbb{E}[U]\big).

Proof.

  • Let a = \mathrm{Cov}(V, U)/\mathrm{Var}(U). The variable V - aU has zero covariance with U.
  • The pair (V - aU, U) is Gaussian, so zero covariance means independence.
  • Hence, given U, the variable V - aU keeps its mean \mathbb{E}[V] - a\,\mathbb{E}[U], which is the claim. ∎

For V \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_V^2) and independent noise X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_X^2), the proxy U = V + X gives the shrinkage formula used for Goodhart’s law in ch. 4: \mathbb{E}[V \mid U = u] = \frac{\sigma_V^2}{\sigma_V^2 + \sigma_X^2}\, u .

0.3 Power laws and doubling times

Definition 0.4 — Power law, doubling time A quantity follows a power law in x when y = a\,x^{-b} for constants a, b > 0. Taking logarithms gives a straight line of slope -b: \log y = \log a - b \log x . A quantity grows exponentially with doubling time T when y(t) = y(t_0)\, 2^{(t - t_0)/T}. Reaching \rho times the current value takes \log_2 \rho doublings, that is, a time T \log_2 \rho.

Fitting \log y against \log x by least squares is the maximum-likelihood fit when the errors are multiplicative and log-normal. A power law fitted over a few decades of x can be extrapolated only as far as the mechanism that produced it is believed to continue; ch. 1 and 2 return to this caveat.

0.4 Concentration of an average

Proposition 0.5 — Hoeffding’s inequality for proportions Let Z_1, \dots, Z_n be independent with values in [0, 1] and mean p, and let \hat p be their average. For every t > 0, \mathbb{P}\big(|\hat p - p| \ge t\big) \le 2\exp(-2nt^2).

The proof bounds the moment generating function of each bounded variable (Hoeffding’s lemma) and applies Markov’s inequality; see Boucheron, Lugosi and Massart (2013), ch. 2. Setting the right-hand side to \delta gives the sample size used in ch. 7: n \ge \frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2t^2}.

0.5 Dual norms

Proposition 0.6 — Hölder’s inequality and its equality case For w, \delta \in \mathbb{R}^d, |\langle w, \delta\rangle| \le \|w\|_1\,\|\delta\|_\infty \quad\text{and}\quad |\langle w, \delta\rangle| \le \|w\|_2\,\|\delta\|_2 . The first bound is attained by \delta = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(w), the second by \delta = \varepsilon\, w / \|w\|_2.

Proof. For the first bound, \Big|\sum_i w_i \delta_i\Big| \le \sum_i |w_i|\,|\delta_i| \le \|\delta\|_\infty \sum_i |w_i| . For \delta_i = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(w_i), every term equals \varepsilon|w_i|. The second bound is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. ∎

In dimension d, a vector with entries of size about c has \|w\|_1 \approx dc but \|w\|_2 \approx \sqrt{d}\,c. A perturbation of tiny size in every coordinate can therefore move a linear score a lot. This is the root of the adversarial examples of ch. 5.

0.6 The logistic function

The logistic function and its inverse, the log-odds, are \sigma(t) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-t}}, \qquad \sigma^{-1}(q) = \log\frac{q}{1 - q}. It turns a score difference into a probability, as in the preference models of ch. 4. Its log-derivative is 1 - \sigma(t), so the loss -\log\sigma(t) is convex in t.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = H(p, q) - H(p) \ge 0, with equality only at q = p (Gibbs).
  • For Gaussian V and X, the proxy U = V + X predicts V with a shrinkage factor \sigma_V^2 / (\sigma_V^2 + \sigma_X^2).
  • Power laws are lines on log–log axes; exponential trends are counted in doublings.
  • About \log(2/\delta)/(2t^2) samples estimate a proportion within t with probability 1 - \delta.
  • The \ell_\infty ball meets a linear score through the \ell_1 norm of its weights.

1. Capabilities: from GPT-2 to frontier models

From 2019 to 2025, language models went from plausible paragraphs to olympiad problems and software agents. This chapter measures that progress in compute and explains why the loss improved predictably.

1.1 Milestones and training compute

GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019) had 1.5 billion parameters; GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020) had 175 billion, trained on 300 billion tokens. Later reports, such as GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023), disclose no parameter counts; the AI Index Report 2025 tracks the public trends.

Proposition 1.1 — Training compute of a dense transformer For a dense model with N parameters trained on D tokens, the training cost is approximately C \approx 6\,N D \ \text{floating-point operations (FLOP)}.

Proof. Sketch (Kaplan et al., 2020). Forward: each weight does one multiplication and one addition per token, 2N FLOP. Backward: gradients for activations and weights, twice that, 4N. The attention term is neglected. ∎

Example 1.2 — GPT-3 With N = 1.75 \times 10^{11} and D = 3 \times 10^{11}: C \approx 6 \times 1.75 \times 10^{11} \times 3 \times 10^{11} \approx 3.15 \times 10^{23}\ \text{FLOP}, as reported by Brown et al. (2020).

1.2 Scaling laws

Definition 1.3 — Parametric scaling law Hoffmann et al. (2022) model the test loss as L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}, where E is the irreducible loss (ch. 0.1).

Kaplan et al. (2020) found such power laws over several orders of magnitude. How should a fixed budget C be spent?

Theorem 1.4 — Compute-optimal allocation Minimise L(N, D) subject to 6ND = C. The unique minimiser is N^* = G\left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad D^* = G^{-1}\left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad G = \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}} .

Proof.

  • Substitute D = C/(6N). The function to minimise is f(N) = A N^{-\alpha} + B\,(6N/C)^{\beta}.
  • Its derivative factors as f'(N) = N^{-\alpha-1}\Big(-\alpha A + \beta B\,(6/C)^{\beta} N^{\alpha+\beta}\Big).
  • The bracket increases from -\alpha A to +\infty, so f' changes sign once, from negative to positive.
  • The root solves N^{\alpha+\beta} = (\alpha A/\beta B)(C/6)^{\beta}, which is N^*; then D^* = C/(6N^*). ∎

Example 1.5 — Ten times more compute With \alpha = 0.34 and \beta = 0.28, the exponent of N^* is 0.28/0.62 \approx 0.45. Ten times more compute buys

10^{0.45} \approx 2.8 \text{ times more parameters and } 10^{0.55} \approx 3.5 \text{ times more data.} 

import numpy as np

# Parametric fit of Hoffmann et al. (2022), approach 3, rounded constants
E, A, B, alpha, beta = 1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28

def loss(N, D):
    return E + A / N**alpha + B / D**beta

def optimal_allocation(C):
    """Minimise loss(N, D) subject to 6 N D = C (Theorem 1.4)."""
    G = (alpha * A / (beta * B)) ** (1 / (alpha + beta))
    N = G * (C / 6) ** (beta / (alpha + beta))
    return N, C / (6 * N)

for C in [1e21, 5.76e23, 1e25]:
    N, D = optimal_allocation(C)
    print(f"C = {C:.2e} FLOP: N* = {N:.2e} params, D* = {D:.2e} tokens, "
          f"D*/N* = {D/N:5.1f}, loss = {loss(N, D):.3f}")

# Brute-force check on a grid for the budget of Gopher and Chinchilla
C = 5.76e23
Ns = np.logspace(9, 12, 20001)
print("grid minimiser:", f"{Ns[loss(Ns, C / (6 * Ns)).argmin()]:.2e}")

# Fit a power law L(C) = a C^(-b) to noisy synthetic points on log-log axes
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Cs = np.logspace(18, 22, 9)
L_obs = 40.0 * Cs ** (-0.05) * np.exp(0.01 * rng.standard_normal(Cs.size))
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(np.log(Cs), np.log(L_obs), 1)
print(f"fitted exponent b = {-slope:.4f}, prefactor a = {np.exp(intercept):.1f}")

At the Chinchilla budget, 5.76 \times 10^{23} FLOP, the code finds 32 billion parameters and 93 tokens per parameter. The Chinchilla model itself has 70 billion parameters and 1.4 trillion tokens, 20 tokens per parameter, as the paper’s other two methods give.

Besiroglu et al. (2024) showed that the published fit is inconsistent with those methods. Constants are fragile; the robust lesson is that parameters and data grow together.

1.3 Emergence and the choice of metric

Wei et al. (2022) reported “emergent” abilities: near-zero accuracy, then a sharp rise with scale. Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) traced many such jumps to the metric.

Proposition 1.6 — A smooth per-token gain gives a sharp exact-match curve Suppose an answer has k tokens, each correct independently with probability p. Then the exact-match accuracy is \mathbb{P}(\text{answer correct}) = p^{k} .

For k = 20, raising p from 0.80 to 0.90, 0.95 and 0.99 moves exact match from 0.012 to 0.12, 0.36 and 0.82, while the per-token log-likelihood shows no jump. An abrupt curve needs a check of its metric.

1.4 Reasoning models and test-time compute

A second axis of scaling appeared in 2024: compute at inference. Models trained by reinforcement learning to reason at length, such as DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025), improve with longer reasoning, and adaptive test-time compute can beat a larger model (Snell et al., 2024).

Agents add tools and long horizons (ch. 2). Each new axis of scaling is one more way for capabilities to appear before they are evaluated (ch. 7).

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Training compute is about 6ND FLOP; GPT-3 used about 3 \times 10^{23}.
  • Loss follows power laws; parameters and data grow together, with exponents near one half; fitted constants are fragile.
  • Sharp “emergent” curves can come from metrics such as exact match, p^k.
  • Inference compute and agents are new axes of scaling.

2. Forecasting general-purpose AI

Could AGI emerge within this decade? This chapter makes the question precise, builds forecasts from trends and surveys, and scores them.

2.1 What is AGI?

There is no agreed definition. Morris et al. (2024) cross levels of performance, from “emerging” to “superhuman”, with generality.

Definition 2.1 — Working definition In these notes, AGI is an AI system that matches or exceeds a skilled human professional on most cognitive tasks, including long tasks that need planning and the use of tools.

Such a definition is the resolution criterion of a forecast, and must be fixed first.

2.2 Extrapolating trends

Kwa et al. (2025) measure the length of tasks, timed on human professionals, that models complete with 50 % success. This “time horizon” doubled about every seven months from 2019 to 2025.

Example 2.2 — From one hour to one month Suppose a horizon of 1 hour today and a target of one work-month, about 167 hours. By Definition 0.4, the target needs \log_2 167 \approx 7.4 doublings.

  • With a doubling time of 7 months: 7.4 \times 7 \approx 52 months, about 4.3 years.
  • With a lognormal doubling time of median 7 months, the 5–95 % range is 2.6 to 7.0 years (code below).

The trend may slow if compute, data or energy run short, or speed up if AI accelerates AI research; the width of the range is part of the answer.

2.3 Expert surveys

Grace et al. (2024) surveyed 2,778 researchers who had published at top AI venues. The aggregate gave a 10 % chance that unaided machines outperform humans in every possible task by 2027, and 50 % by 2047, thirteen years earlier than their survey a year before. Asking about the automation of all occupations gave much later dates.

2.4 Scoring and combining forecasts

Definition 2.3 — Proper scoring rule A forecaster announces q \in [0, 1] for an event Y \in \{0, 1\} and pays S(q, Y). The rule is strictly proper if, for Y \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p), the expected loss is uniquely minimised at q = p. Brier and log losses: S_B(q, y) = (q - y)^2, \qquad S_{\log}(q, y) = -y\log q - (1 - y)\log(1 - q).

Theorem 2.4 — The Brier and log scores are strictly proper For Y \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p), \mathbb{E}[S_B(q, Y)] = (q - p)^2 + p(1 - p), \qquad \mathbb{E}[S_{\log}(q, Y)] = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q). Both are uniquely minimised at q = p.

Proof.

  • Brier: expand p(1 - q)^2 + (1 - p)q^2; the cross terms collect into (q - p)^2.
  • Log: the expected loss is the cross-entropy between Bernoulli distributions, which is H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) by Definition 0.1.
  • The minimum follows from (q - p)^2 \ge 0 and from Gibbs’ inequality (Proposition 0.2). ∎

A proper rule rewards honesty: exaggerating or downplaying a risk never pays.

Proposition 2.5 — Averaging forecasts helps Let q_1, \dots, q_m be forecasts of the same event and \bar q their mean. For every outcome y, S_B(\bar q, y) \le \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} S_B(q_j, y).

Proof. The map q \mapsto (q - y)^2 is convex; apply Jensen’s inequality. ∎

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Example 2.2: doubling-time extrapolation with an uncertain doubling time
h0, target = 1.0, 167.0                       # current horizon and target, in hours
k = np.log2(target / h0)                      # doublings needed
T = np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(7.0), 0.3, 100_000))   # doubling time (months), median 7
years = k * T / 12
print(f"doublings {k:.2f}; years 5/50/95 %:", np.round(np.quantile(years, [0.05, 0.5, 0.95]), 2))

# Proposition 2.5: 15 noisy forecasters against their average
n_events, m = 2000, 15
p_true = rng.uniform(0.05, 0.95, n_events)
y = rng.random(n_events) < p_true
logit = np.log(p_true / (1 - p_true))
q = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(logit + rng.normal(0, 1.0, (m, n_events)))))
brier_each = ((q - y) ** 2).mean(axis=1)
print(f"Brier: mean individual {brier_each.mean():.4f}, best individual {brier_each.min():.4f}, "
      f"average forecast {((q.mean(axis=0) - y) ** 2).mean():.4f}")

The average forecast (Brier 0.189) beats the best of the 15 forecasters (0.210).

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Fix the definition and the resolution criterion first.
  • A doubling trend reaches \rho times its value after T\log_2\rho; uncertainty on T spreads this over years.
  • Surveys moved earlier in 2023 and depend on framing.
  • Brier and log scores are strictly proper; averaging lowers the Brier score.

3. Benefits and risks of general-purpose AI

Generality makes these systems valuable and risky at once. This chapter reviews both and models layered defences.

3.1 Benefits

  • Biomedical research. AlphaFold 2 (Jumper et al., 2021) predicts protein structures with near-experimental accuracy in many cases; two of its authors, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
  • Language barriers. The NLLB project (NLLB Team, 2022) translates between 200 languages, many of them low-resource.
  • Administrative burdens. Experiments find productivity gains in writing and customer support, largest for novices (Noy and Zhang, 2023; Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025).

3.2 A taxonomy of risks

The International AI Safety Report (Bengio et al., 2025) groups risks into three families.

Definition 3.1 — Three families of risk

  • Malicious use: people use the system to cause harm: cyber attacks, biological weapons, manipulation.
  • Malfunctions: the system does not do what its developers intend, from unreliability and bias to loss of control.
  • Systemic risks: harms of wide deployment, such as concentration of power or labour-market disruption.

Hendrycks, Mazeika and Woodside (2023) add the AI race (ch. 8) and organisational failures. Ch. 4–6 study malfunctions, ch. 7 misuse potential, ch. 8 races.

3.3 Defence in depth

Systems stack imperfect safeguards: training, filters, monitoring, access control, as in the “Swiss cheese” model of Reason (1990).

Proposition 3.2 — Layers with a common-mode failure A harmful request passes k layers. With probability \rho, a common cause defeats all layers at once; otherwise, layer i fails independently with probability p_i. Then \mathbb{P}(\text{breach}) = \rho + (1 - \rho)\prod_{i=1}^{k} p_i \;\ge\; \rho .

Proof. Condition on whether the common cause occurs; in the other case the layers fail independently. ∎

Example 3.3 — Correlation dominates Three layers failing 10 % of the time each give a breach probability of 0.001 if independent. With a common mode \rho = 0.01, say a filter and a monitor built on the same model, it becomes

0.01 + 0.99 \times 0.001 \approx 0.011,

eleven times higher.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Benefits are measurable in science, translation and productivity.
  • Risks: malicious use, malfunctions, systemic risks; races amplify them.
  • Layers multiply only if independent; a common mode sets a floor \rho.

4. Specification: proxies, Goodhart’s law and preference learning

We cannot write down what we want, so we optimise a measurable proxy: a reward, a reward model, human ratings. This chapter shows when optimising a proxy stops helping, and derives the objective used to train chat models.

4.1 Proxies and Goodhart’s law

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure” is Strathern’s (1997) phrasing of Goodhart’s law. An agent trained on the score of the boat race CoastRunners learned to circle and hit the same targets instead of finishing (Clark and Amodei, 2016). Sycophancy is a proxy failure of models trained on human approval (Sharma et al., 2024).

Definition 4.1 — True value, proxy, Goodhart variants Let V be the value we care about and U a measurable proxy. Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) distinguish four ways in which selecting high U fails to select high V:

  • regressional: U = V + X with noise X, so a high U is partly high noise;
  • extremal: the relation changes in the region reached by optimisation;
  • causal: acting on U breaks the correlation;
  • adversarial: an agent exploits the gap between U and V.

4.2 Regressional and extremal Goodhart

Proposition 4.2 — Gaussian noise: shrinkage If V \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) are independent and U = V + X, then \mathbb{E}[V \mid U = u] = u/2 . Higher proxy values still mean higher true values, at half the rate.

This is Proposition 0.3. Heavy-tailed noise changes the picture.

Proposition 4.3 — Heavy-tailed noise: the true value reverts If V \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and X follows an independent Student distribution with \nu degrees of freedom, with density g, then \mathbb{E}[V \mid U = u] \longrightarrow 0 \quad\text{as } u \to \infty .

Proof. Sketch. With \varphi the standard normal density, \mathbb{E}[V \mid U = u] = \frac{\int v\,\varphi(v)\, g(u - v)/g(u)\, dv}{\int \varphi(v)\, g(u - v)/g(u)\, dv}.

  • For fixed v, the ratio g(u - v)/g(u) tends to 1, because g decays like a power.
  • For v \le u/2, the ratio is at most g(u/2)/g(u), which stays bounded.
  • For v > u/2, the ratio is at most g(0)/g(u), which grows only polynomially in u, while \varphi(v) \le \varphi(u/2) decays like e^{-u^2/8}.
  • By dominated convergence, the numerator tends to \mathbb{E}[V] = 0 and the denominator to 1. ∎

The most extreme proxy values are then explained by the errors, not by the value.

4.3 Best-of-n and its KL divergence

The simplest optimiser samples n outputs from a model \pi and keeps the best by proxy score.

Theorem 4.4 — KL divergence of best-of-n Let r(Y) have a continuous distribution function F when Y \sim \pi, and let \pi_n be the law of the best of n independent samples. Then \mathrm{KL}(\pi_n \,\|\, \pi) = \log n - \frac{n - 1}{n}.

Proof.

  • The best sample has proxy rank F(r(y)) equal to the maximum of n uniforms, so \pi_n has density n\,F(r(y))^{n-1} with respect to \pi.
  • Hence the divergence equals \log n + (n - 1)\,\mathbb{E}[\log W], where W is the maximum of n uniforms, with density n w^{n-1} on [0, 1].
  • Integration by parts gives \mathbb{E}[\log W] = \int_0^1 n w^{n-1}\log w\, dw = -1/n. ∎
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
trials = 4000

def best_of_n(noise, n):
    """Mean true value V of the sample with the highest proxy U = V + X."""
    V = rng.standard_normal((trials, n))
    U = V + noise((trials, n))
    return V[np.arange(trials), U.argmax(axis=1)].mean()

gauss = lambda s: rng.standard_normal(s)
heavy = lambda s: rng.standard_t(2, s)          # Student t, 2 degrees of freedom
print("    n  KL (nats)  E[V], Gaussian noise  E[V], t(2) noise")
for n in [1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024]:
    kl = np.log(n) - (n - 1) / n                 # Theorem 4.4
    print(f"{n:5d}  {kl:8.2f}   {best_of_n(gauss, n):12.3f}   {best_of_n(heavy, n):14.3f}")

With Gaussian noise, the true value of the pick grows: 0.74 at n = 4, 2.30 at n = 1024. With heavy-tailed noise it peaks near 0.69 at n = 16 and falls to 0.11 at n = 1024, as Proposition 4.3 predicts.

Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2023) observed this rise and fall when optimising language models against a learned reward model, judged by a larger “gold” one.

4.4 Learning from human preferences

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) learns the proxy from comparisons (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022).

Definition 4.5 — Bradley–Terry reward model and regularised objective Given a prompt x, a rater prefers answer y_w to y_l with probability \mathbb{P}(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big), and r is fitted by maximum likelihood. The policy then maximises, for \beta > 0 and a reference model \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}, J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big).

The KL term limits the optimisation pressure measured by Theorem 4.4.

Theorem 4.6 — The regularised optimum is a Gibbs tilt On a finite set of answers, J has the unique maximiser \pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x, y)/\beta}, \qquad Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x, y)/\beta}.

Proof. Expanding the definition of \pi^* inside \mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*) gives, for every \pi, J(\pi) = \beta \log Z(x) - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*\big). By Gibbs’ inequality (Proposition 0.2), the maximum is \beta\log Z(x), reached only at \pi = \pi^*. ∎

Rafailov et al. (2023) invert it: the reward is \beta\log(\pi^*/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) plus a term in x alone, which cancels in the Bradley–Terry difference. This gives direct preference optimisation (DPO), with no separate reward model.

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi) = -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\right)\right]

DPO removes the reward model, not the proxy: preferences can still be noisy or sycophantic.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Goodhart failures are regressional, extremal, causal or adversarial.
  • Gaussian errors shrink the gain; heavy-tailed errors make it vanish.
  • Best-of-n moves \log n - (n-1)/n nats away from the base model.
  • The regularised optimum is \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\, e^{r/\beta}/Z; inverting it gives DPO.

5. Robustness and generalization failures

A model can be right on the test set and wrong under small changes of its input. This chapter quantifies adversarial fragility, shows why safety training can be bypassed, and how correct data can teach the wrong goal.

5.1 Adversarial examples

Imperceptible perturbations change the predictions of image classifiers (Szegedy et al., 2014). Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy (2015) blamed near-linearity in high dimension and proposed the fast gradient sign method (FGSM), the step \delta = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_x \ell) on the loss \ell.

Proposition 5.1 — Smallest \ell_\infty perturbation of a linear classifier Let f(x) = \langle w, x\rangle + b with f(x) > 0. The smallest \|\delta\|_\infty such that f(x + \delta) \le 0 is \varepsilon^*(x) = \frac{f(x)}{\|w\|_1}, attained by \delta = -\varepsilon^*(x)\,\mathrm{sign}(w), which is the FGSM direction.

Proof. By Proposition 0.6, f(x + \delta) \ge f(x) - \|w\|_1\|\delta\|_\infty > 0 when \|\delta\|_\infty < \varepsilon^*(x), and the given \delta attains equality. ∎

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)             # 8x8 images, pixels in [0, 16]
mask = (y == 3) | (y == 8)                       # binary task: 3 against 8
X, y = X[mask] / 16.0, (y[mask] == 8).astype(int)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.5, random_state=0)
clf = LogisticRegression(C=1.0, max_iter=5000).fit(Xtr, ytr)
w, b = clf.coef_[0], clf.intercept_[0]

f = Xte @ w + b
correct = (f > 0) == (yte == 1)
eps_inf = np.abs(f) / np.abs(w).sum()            # Proposition 5.1
print(f"test accuracy {correct.mean():.3f}")
print(f"median l_inf flip radius {np.median(eps_inf[correct]):.3f} (pixel range 1)")
delta = -1.0001 * eps_inf[:, None] * np.sign(w)[None, :] * np.sign(f)[:, None]
print("all flipped:", bool(np.all(np.sign((Xte + delta) @ w + b) != np.sign(f))))

The classifier is 98.3 % accurate, yet half of its correct answers flip when each pixel moves by at most 0.149 (ignoring the range [0, 1]). As \|w\|_1 grows with the dimension, the radius shrinks for larger images.

5.2 Jailbreaks and repeated attempts

Safety training teaches refusals. Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt (2023) explain jailbreaks by competing objectives, helpfulness against harmlessness, and by safety training generalizing less far than capabilities. Zou et al. (2023) found adversarial suffixes, optimised on open models, that transfer to closed ones.

Proposition 5.2 — Repeated attempts If each attempt succeeds independently with probability p, then k attempts succeed at least once with probability 1 - (1 - p)^k \ge 1 - e^{-kp}.

Example 5.3 — 99.9 % is not robust A model refusing 99.9 % of attempts, p = 0.001, gives an attacker with 1,000 attempts a success probability of

1 - 0.999^{1000} \approx 0.63 .

Robustness must be measured against an attacker’s budget, not per query (ch. 7).

5.3 Goal misgeneralization

Definition 5.4 — Goal misgeneralization A model misgeneralizes its goal when, out of distribution, it stays competent but pursues an unintended goal, although its training signal was correct (Shah et al., 2022).

In CoinRun, the coin always sat at the end of the level during training. When Langosco et al. (2022) moved it, agents ran to the end and ignored it. The data could not tell the two goals apart; the implicit bias of training chose.

Proposition 5.5 — Gradient descent picks the minimum-norm interpolant Let X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} and y \in \mathbb{R}^n admit a solution of Xw = y. Gradient descent on \tfrac12\|Xw - y\|^2 from w_0 = 0, with a small enough step, converges to w^\dagger = X^{+}y, \quad\text{the interpolant of smallest Euclidean norm}.

Proof.

  • Every gradient X^\top(Xw - y) lies in the row space of X, so all iterates do.
  • On that space the loss is strongly convex, so they converge to an interpolant in it.
  • Interpolants differ by null-space vectors, orthogonal to the row space; the one in the row space is unique and of smallest norm. ∎

Example 5.6 — The salient feature wins In training, the intended feature x_1 (the coin) and a spurious one, x_2 = 3x_1 (the end of the level), move together, and y = x_1. The row space is spanned by (1, 3), so

w^\dagger = (0.1,\ 0.3).

With the coin alone, x = (1, 0), the prediction is 0.1 instead of 1: the spurious feature dominates.

Learned goals cannot be read from behaviour alone, which motivates looking inside models (ch. 6).

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • A linear score flips at \ell_\infty radius f(x)/\|w\|_1, small in high dimension.
  • Repeated attempts succeed with probability 1 - (1-p)^k.
  • Correct signals can teach the wrong goal; implicit bias decides between goals the data cannot separate.

6. Interpretability: understanding what models compute

Behavioural tests show what a model does on the inputs we try. Interpretability reads how it computes, to find hidden goals or failure modes before they show up. This chapter covers the main tools and the obstacle of superposition.

6.1 Features as directions

Mechanistic interpretability looks for features, properties the model represents, and circuits that connect them (Olah et al., 2020). The linear representation hypothesis takes features to be directions in activation space (Park, Choe and Veitch, 2024).

Definition 6.1 — Linear probe Let h(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d be the activation of a layer on input x, and z(x) a property of the input. A linear probe is a classifier \sigma(\langle a, h(x)\rangle + c) trained to predict z(x) from the activations (Alain and Bengio, 2017).

A probe shows that a property is decodable, not that the model uses it; control tasks separate the two (Hewitt and Liang, 2019).

6.2 Superposition

Orthogonal features would be at most d in a layer of width d. Models seem to represent far more, in superposition: nearly orthogonal directions, which high dimension provides.

Theorem 6.2 — Exponentially many almost orthogonal directions For 0 < \varepsilon < 1, the space \mathbb{R}^d contains m = \lfloor e^{d\varepsilon^2/4} \rfloor unit vectors with |\langle u_i, u_j\rangle| < \varepsilon \quad\text{for all } i \ne j .

Proof.

  • For u uniform on the unit sphere and a fixed unit vector v, the cap bound of Ball (1997, Lemma 2.2), applied to v and -v, gives \mathbb{P}\big(|\langle u, v\rangle| \ge \varepsilon\big) \le 2e^{-d\varepsilon^2/2}.
  • Draw m independent uniform vectors. A union bound over the m(m-1)/2 pairs bounds the probability that some pair fails by m(m-1)\,e^{-d\varepsilon^2/2}.
  • This is less than 1 when m^2 \le e^{d\varepsilon^2/2}, so some draw succeeds. ∎

Example 6.3 — The width of GPT-3 GPT-3 has d = 12{,}288 (Brown et al., 2020). With \varepsilon = 0.1, Theorem 6.2 guarantees

e^{30.72} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{13}

directions with overlaps below 0.1, against 12,288 orthogonal ones.

The price is interference between features. Elhage et al. (2022) showed with toy models that superposition appears when features are sparse, so that interference rarely matters.

import numpy as np
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
n, m, S = 5, 2, 0.9           # 5 features, 2 dimensions, each feature absent w.p. 0.9

W = torch.nn.Parameter(0.1 * torch.randn(m, n))
b = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n))
opt = torch.optim.Adam([W, b], lr=1e-2)
for step in range(5000):
    x = torch.rand(1024, n) * (torch.rand(1024, n) > S)     # sparse features in [0, 1]
    x_hat = torch.relu(x @ W.T @ W + b)                     # h = W x, x' = ReLU(W^T h + b)
    loss = ((x - x_hat) ** 2).sum(dim=1).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

Wd = W.detach().numpy()
angles = np.sort(np.degrees(np.arctan2(Wd[1], Wd[0])) % 360)
print("loss", round(loss.item(), 4))
print("feature norms  ", np.round(np.linalg.norm(Wd, axis=0), 2))
print("angle gaps (deg)", np.round(np.diff(np.r_[angles, angles[0] + 360]), 1))
print("bias", np.round(b.detach().numpy(), 2))

Five features fit in two dimensions as a regular pentagon: norms near 1.1, gaps near 72°, and a bias near −0.22 that filters interference. With dense features (S = 0, Ex. 12) mainly two survive.

6.3 Sparse autoencoders

Definition 6.4 — Sparse autoencoder A sparse autoencoder maps an activation h \in \mathbb{R}^d to a code f = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_e h + b_e) \in \mathbb{R}^{M}, with M \gg d, and reconstructs \hat h = W_d f + b_d. It minimises \mathbb{E}\big[\|h - \hat h\|_2^2 + \lambda\|f\|_1\big].

Columns of W_d are candidate features, and the \ell_1 penalty keeps few active at once. Bricken et al. (2023) and Cunningham et al. (2024) found interpretable features this way; Templeton et al. (2024) extracted millions from a production model, some related to safety, such as deception.

6.4 Causal interventions

Definition 6.5 — Activation patching Run a model on a clean and a corrupted input. Patching a component reruns the corrupted input with that component’s activation set to its clean value; the fraction of clean behaviour restored measures the component’s role.

Meng et al. (2022) located factual associations this way, and Wang et al. (2023) found a circuit for indirect-object identification in GPT-2 small. Adding a direction to activations steers behaviour (Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023).

Interpretability cannot yet verify that a frontier model has no hidden goal; its explanations must be tested by interventions.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Probes show what is decodable; interventions test what is used.
  • Dimension d holds e^{d\varepsilon^2/4} directions with overlaps below \varepsilon; sparse features exploit this.
  • Sparse autoencoders recover features in superposition with an \ell_1-sparse code.
  • Activation patching locates where a behaviour is computed.

7. Evaluating dangerous capabilities

Safe deployment requires knowing what a model can be made to do. This chapter defines dangerous-capability evaluations and the statistics to read them.

7.1 What to evaluate

Definition 7.1 — Dangerous-capability evaluation, elicitation A dangerous-capability evaluation estimates whether a model can perform a task that would enable severe harm, such as uplift towards biological or chemical weapons, offensive cyber operations, autonomous replication, or persuasion (Shevlane et al., 2023; Phuong et al., 2024). Elicitation is the effort spent to reveal the capability: prompting, examples, tools and scaffolds, repeated sampling, fine-tuning.

What matters is what a motivated actor could obtain, so an evaluation reports its best elicitation. A capability not found is not a capability absent.

7.2 Elicitation by repeated sampling

The pass@k metric (Chen et al., 2021) asks whether one of k samples succeeds: 1 - (1 - p)^k, as in Proposition 5.2.

Theorem 7.2 — Unbiased estimator of pass@k Draw n \ge k independent samples, of which c succeed. Then \widehat{\text{pass@}k} = 1 - \binom{n - c}{k}\Big/\binom{n}{k} is an unbiased estimator of 1 - (1 - p)^k.

Proof.

  • Pick a uniform random k-subset of the samples, independently of the outcomes. It contains a success with probability 1 - (1 - p)^k.
  • Given the outcomes, it contains none with probability \binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}, counting subsets of the n - c failures.
  • By the tower property, the estimator has expectation 1 - (1 - p)^k. ∎

The plug-in 1 - (1 - c/n)^k is biased low, by concavity: it understates the capability.

Proposition 7.3 — No success observed If none of n independent attempts succeeds, the one-sided 95 % upper confidence bound on p is p_{\max} = 1 - 0.05^{1/n} \le \frac{3}{n}.

Proof. (1 - p)^n \le 0.05 exactly when p \ge 1 - 0.05^{1/n}. As -\log 0.05 \le 3, this is at most 1 - e^{-3/n} \le 3/n. ∎

import numpy as np
from math import comb

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    """Unbiased estimator of 1 - (1-p)^k from n samples with c successes (Theorem 7.2)."""
    return 1.0 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k) if n - c >= k else 1.0

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
p, n, k, reps = 0.05, 50, 10, 20000
c = rng.binomial(n, p, reps)
unbiased = np.array([pass_at_k(n, ci, k) for ci in c])
plug_in = 1 - (1 - c / n) ** k
print(f"true pass@{k} = {1 - (1 - p) ** k:.4f}")
print(f"mean unbiased = {unbiased.mean():.4f}, mean plug-in = {plug_in.mean():.4f}")

# Hoeffding (Proposition 0.5): trials for a +/- 0.05 estimate with 95 % confidence
print("Hoeffding n >=", int(np.ceil(np.log(2 / 0.05) / (2 * 0.05 ** 2))))
for n0 in [30, 100, 300]:                       # Proposition 7.3
    print(f"0 successes in {n0}: p_max = {1 - 0.05 ** (1 / n0):.4f}, 3/n = {3 / n0:.4f}")

The plug-in averages 0.375 against a true 0.401; the unbiased estimator is on target. A rate within 0.05 needs 738 trials, and 300 clean attempts only show p \le 1\,\%, weak protection by Example 5.3.

7.3 Access to closed models

Most frontier models are closed. Evaluators may get API access only, or log-probabilities, fine-tuning and activations. Casper et al. (2024) argue that black-box access is insufficient for rigorous audits: deeper access elicits more and allows the checks of ch. 6.

7.4 Sandbagging and safety frameworks

Models can be prompted or fine-tuned to underperform selectively on dangerous-capability evaluations (van der Weij et al., 2024), and frontier models underperformed strategically in some test scenarios (Meinke et al., 2024).

Frontier safety frameworks, such as Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy (2023), OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework (2023) and Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework (2024), set capability thresholds and the safeguards required beyond them: if-then commitments that make evaluations decisive.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Report the best elicitation; absence of evidence is weak evidence.
  • pass@k is estimated without bias by 1 - \binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}.
  • Zero successes in n attempts bound p by about 3/n.
  • Access, sandbagging and thresholds decide what an evaluation is worth.

8. Governance and international coordination

Safety measures are adopted only if developers and states have reasons to adopt them. This chapter models races, shows why verification enables cooperation, and surveys regulation.

8.1 Races as games

Armstrong, Bostrom and Shulman (2016) model developers trading safety for speed in a race. In the simplest version, each of two labs chooses Safe (S) or Fast (F).

Proposition 8.1 — A safety dilemma Let the payoffs be R if both choose S, P if both choose F, and, when they differ, T for the fast lab and S_0 for the safe one, with T > R > P > S_0 . Then F is strictly dominant, so (F, F) is the unique Nash equilibrium, although both labs prefer (S, S).

Proof. Against S, F yields T > R; against F, it yields P > S_0. ∎

Repeat the game forever with discount factor \delta \in (0, 1), and consider grim trigger: play S until the other plays F, then F forever.

Theorem 8.2 — Cooperation in the repeated game Both labs playing grim trigger is a subgame-perfect equilibrium if and only if \delta \ge \frac{T - R}{T - P}.

Proof.

  • After a deviation, (F, F) forever repeats a stage equilibrium, so punishing is a best response.
  • On the path, by the one-shot deviation principle, cooperating, worth R/(1 - \delta), must beat deviating, worth T + \delta P/(1 - \delta).
  • The inequality rearranges to \delta(T - P) \ge T - R. ∎

With T = 5, R = 3, P = 1, cooperation needs \delta \ge 0.5. The proof assumes deviations are seen at once; with imperfect verification the required patience rises (Ex. 14).

8.2 Verification and compute

Compute is a natural target for governance: detectable, excludable, quantifiable, with a concentrated supply chain (Sastry et al., 2024), and predictive of capability (ch. 1).

Definition 8.3 — Compute threshold A compute threshold applies obligations to models trained with more than a fixed number of FLOP. The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 51) presumes systemic risk for general-purpose models above 10^{25}\ \text{FLOP}.

Example 8.4 — Which models cross the threshold? By Proposition 1.1, a hypothetical model of 7 \times 10^{10} parameters trained on 1.5 \times 10^{13} tokens uses 6.3 \times 10^{24} FLOP, below the threshold; one of 4 \times 10^{11} parameters on the same data uses

3.6 \times 10^{25}\ \text{FLOP},

above it. Thresholds are proxies (ch. 4): algorithmic progress lowers the compute behind a capability.

8.3 Regulation and international coordination

The EU AI Act classifies uses by risk; its obligations for general-purpose models apply from 2 August 2025. Providers of models with systemic risk must evaluate them, including adversarial testing, report serious incidents and ensure cybersecurity, as detailed by the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice of July 2025.

The Bletchley Park summit (November 2023) produced the Bletchley Declaration, and states created AI safety institutes, such as the United Kingdom’s, renamed AI Security Institute in 2025. At Seoul (May 2024), companies signed the Frontier AI Safety Commitments. The International AI Safety Report, chaired by Yoshua Bengio, appeared before the Paris AI Action Summit of February 2025.

The bottlenecks are technical: evaluations that elicit well (ch. 7), interpretability that finds hidden goals (ch. 6), training that resists proxy failures (ch. 4–5), verification of training runs and hardware. Engineers build and stress-test these tools in labs, safety institutes, academia and non-profits; ch. 10 lists open problems.

9. AI control and scalable oversight

The previous chapters try to make models aligned and to check it. This self-study chapter asks two complementary questions. First, can we deploy a capable model safely even if it might be trying to subvert us? Second, how can weaker overseers supervise stronger models? Both are studied with explicit attacker models, in the spirit of security engineering.

9.1 The control setting

Definition 9.1 — Control protocol, safety, usefulness Greenblatt et al. (2024) consider a capable untrusted model U, a weaker trusted model T, and a small budget of trusted human labour, used to audit a fraction f of the N tasks of a deployment. A control protocol decides, for each task, which model’s output is used and what is audited.

  • Its safety is the probability that no catastrophic action succeeds over the whole deployment, when U is replaced by a red team’s best attack policy.
  • Its usefulness is the fraction of tasks solved well when U is honest.

The red team plays the worst case: it knows the protocol but not which tasks will be audited. A catastrophe happens if an attack, such as code with a hidden backdoor, is used without being audited; an audited attack is caught, and the deployment is stopped.

9.2 Upfront auditing

The simplest protocol audits the first M = fN tasks, then trusts U. The attacker attacks each task independently with probability p.

Theorem 9.2 — Upfront auditing Assume that every attack after the audit period succeeds. The attacker’s success probability is g(p) = (1 - p)^{M}\Big(1 - (1 - p)^{N - M}\Big), maximised at 1 - p^* = f^{1/(N - M)}, with maximum value g(p^*) = (1 - f)\, f^{f/(1 - f)} .

Proof.

  • The attacker wins if it never attacks during the audit period, with probability (1 - p)^M, and attacks at least once afterwards, with probability 1 - (1 - p)^{N - M}.
  • With q = 1 - p, the function q^M - q^N has derivative q^{M-1}(M - N q^{N - M}), positive then negative, so it is maximised at q^{N - M} = M/N = f.
  • Then q^{M} = f^{M/(N - M)} = f^{f/(1-f)} and 1 - q^{N-M} = 1 - f. ∎

Example 9.3 — A 2 % audit budget With f = 0.02, the attacker wins with probability 0.98 \times 0.02^{0.0204} \approx 0.905, whatever N. Upfront auditing gives a safety of only about 9.5 %: a patient attacker waits for the audits to end.

9.3 Trusted monitoring

A better protocol asks T to give each output of U a suspicion score, and audits the fraction f of the most suspicious ones.

Proposition 9.4 — Trusted monitoring Suppose that each attack is audited, independently, with probability c, and that an unaudited attack succeeds. Against any attack policy that attacks at least once, the attacker wins with probability 1 - c. If benign scores follow \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and attack scores \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1), then c = 1 - \Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(1 - f) - \mu\big).

Proof. The game is decided at the first attack: caught with probability c, successful otherwise. Auditing every score above the threshold \tau = \Phi^{-1}(1 - f) spends the budget f on benign tasks, and catches an attack when its score exceeds \tau. ∎

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, f = 1000, 0.02
M = int(f * N)                                  # the first M tasks are audited

# Upfront auditing: closed form (Theorem 9.2) against simulation
p_star = 1 - f ** (1 / (N - M))
red_win = (1 - f) * f ** (f / (1 - f))
games = 20000
attacks = rng.random((games, N)) < p_star
first = np.where(attacks.any(axis=1), attacks.argmax(axis=1), N)
red_sim = np.mean((first >= M) & (first < N))
print(f"optimal attack rate p* = {p_star:.5f}")
print(f"red win: formula {red_win:.4f}, simulation {red_sim:.4f}, safety {1 - red_win:.4f}")

# Trusted monitoring (Proposition 9.4): audit the f most suspicious outputs
tau = norm.ppf(1 - f)
for mu in [1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0]:
    print(f"separation mu = {mu}: catch probability c = {norm.sf(tau - mu):.3f}")

The simulation confirms the formula (0.903 against 0.905). With a monitor that separates attacks by \mu = 3 standard deviations, trusted monitoring catches 83 % of attacks, against 9.5 % for upfront auditing with the same budget.

Two refinements matter in practice. Defer to trusted replaces the moderately suspicious outputs of U by outputs of T, trading usefulness for safety. Untrusted monitoring uses a copy of U as a better monitor, but the copy may collude with the policy: a common-mode failure in the sense of Proposition 3.2.

9.4 Scalable oversight: debate

When a model’s answers are too hard for humans to check, training on human judgement rewards answers that merely look right (ch. 4). Scalable oversight looks for protocols in which a weak judge can still reward the truth.

Definition 9.5 — Debate In the debate game of Irving, Christiano and Amodei (2018), two agents answer a question and then take turns making short arguments; a judge reads the transcript and declares a winner. The agents are trained to win.

Theorem 9.6 — Debate and PSPACE (Irving, Christiano and Amodei, 2018) With optimal play by both debaters and a polynomial-time judge, debates of polynomial length can decide every problem in PSPACE, whereas a judge who checks a single argument without debate decides only problems in NP.

The proof reduces true quantified Boolean formulas to debates in which each debater chooses the quantified variables of one kind; the judge checks the final assignment. The guarantee needs optimal play, which training does not ensure. Empirically, Khan et al. (2024) found that non-expert judges, human or model, answer reading-comprehension questions more accurately with debate than without. Recursive reward modelling, in which models help humans evaluate other models, is a related proposal (Leike et al., 2018).

9.5 Weak-to-strong generalization

Burns et al. (2023) study an analogy: fine-tune a strong model on labels produced by a weak one, and ask how much of the strong model’s ability is recovered.

Definition 9.7 — Performance gap recovered Let a_w be the accuracy of the weak supervisor, a_{w2s} that of the strong model trained on weak labels, and a_s that of the strong model trained on true labels. The performance gap recovered is \mathrm{PGR} = \frac{a_{w2s} - a_w}{a_s - a_w}.

PGR equals 1 when weak supervision is as good as ground truth, and 0 when the student only imitates its supervisor. Burns et al. (2023) report substantial but incomplete recovery on NLP tasks. Ex. 15 shows the mechanism on synthetic data: random supervisor errors are averaged out, while systematic ones are learned.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Control assumes the model may be adversarial, and measures safety against the best attack.
  • Upfront auditing with budget f leaves the attacker (1 - f)f^{f/(1-f)}, about 0.9 for f = 2\,\%.
  • Trusted monitoring catches an attack with probability c, the true-positive rate at false-positive rate f.
  • Debate lifts a weak judge from NP to PSPACE under optimal play; weak-to-strong generalization measures what weak labels can elicit.

10. Research directions and open problems

This self-study chapter maps active research questions, area by area, and ends with practical ways for an engineer or applied mathematician to contribute. The field moves quickly: treat every item as a pointer to read, not as a settled result. Two broad surveys of open problems are Anwar et al. (2024) and, for an earlier and more general list, Hendrycks et al. (2021).

10.1 Scaling and forecasting

  • Data and compute limits. Villalobos et al. (2024) estimate when the stock of public human-written text will be exhausted at current scaling rates. Whether synthetic data and reinforcement learning remove this limit is open.
  • Robust scaling laws. The replication by Besiroglu et al. (2024) shows how much fitted exponents depend on the data and the fitting procedure. Laws for downstream capabilities, rather than loss, remain much less reliable.
  • Measuring agents. Task-length measures such as that of Kwa et al. (2025) depend on the task suite. Building benchmarks that track real-world autonomy, and do not saturate within a year, is an open problem.

10.2 Alignment and specification

  • Hidden or learned goals. Hubinger et al. (2019) analysed the hypothesis that training could produce a model that pursues its own objective while appearing aligned. Later work has built model organisms of such behaviour: backdoored “sleeper agents” whose behaviour persisted through standard safety training (Hubinger et al., 2024), and a model that selectively complied with a training objective, reasoning that this avoided being modified (Greenblatt et al., 2024).
  • Emergent misalignment. Betley et al. (2025) found that fine-tuning a model on a narrow task, writing insecure code without saying so, made it broadly misaligned on unrelated prompts. Why such generalization happens is not understood.
  • Reward hacking in reasoning models. Baker et al. (2025) monitored the chains of thought of a reasoning model to detect reward hacking. They found that penalising the flagged thoughts led the model to hide its intent while still hacking: optimisation pressure against a monitor is a Goodhart problem (ch. 4).
  • Theory of reward hacking. Skalse et al. (2022) give a formal definition of a proxy reward that cannot be hacked and show that such proxies are rare. Pan, Bhatia and Steinhardt (2022) measure how misspecified rewards produce phase transitions in behaviour as agents grow more capable.

10.3 Interpretability

  • Beyond sparse autoencoders. Ameisen et al. (2025) and Lindsey et al. (2025) trace computational graphs through replacement models of a production language model. Whether such explanations are faithful and complete enough for safety cases is open; Sharkey et al. (2025) survey the open problems of mechanistic interpretability.
  • Chain-of-thought monitoring. Korbak et al. (2025) argue that reasoning written in natural language is a fragile opportunity for oversight, which training choices could destroy. Measuring monitorability, and keeping it, is a new research area.
  • Unlearning. Li et al. (2024) introduced the WMDP benchmark of hazardous knowledge and a method to remove it. Checking that removed knowledge cannot be recovered by fine-tuning connects unlearning with evaluation (ch. 7).

10.4 Evaluation, control and verification

  • Statistics of evaluations. Miller (2024) applies standard statistical practice to language-model evaluations: standard errors, clustered questions, paired comparisons. Elicitation gaps and sandbagging (ch. 7.4) have no accepted measurement method.
  • Control of agents. Extending the protocols of ch. 9 from single outputs to long agentic trajectories, with tools and memory, is open, as is estimating the catch probability c of Proposition 9.4 for realistic attacks.
  • Guarantees. Dalrymple et al. (2024) propose systems with quantitative safety guarantees relative to a world model and a formal specification. How far formal methods can scale to general-purpose systems is unknown.

10.5 Governance

  • Verification of agreements. Theorem 8.2 needs deviations to be observable. Hardware and software mechanisms that let a third party verify claims about training runs, without revealing secrets, are an engineering problem.
  • Thresholds as proxies. Compute thresholds (Definition 8.3) will need to track algorithmic progress, or be complemented by capability-based triggers from evaluations.
  • Expert assessments. Bengio et al. (2024) set out a consensus statement on managing extreme risks; the International AI Safety Report (Bengio et al., 2025) synthesises the evidence for policymakers. Turning such assessments into verifiable commitments is the open step.

10.6 How engineers can contribute

The official course description insists on this question. Concrete routes for an MVA student:

  • Reproduce and extend a paper. Most results above come with code. A careful reproduction with error bars, then one ablation, is a real contribution and the format of the projects below.
  • Use the open tooling. TransformerLens (Nanda and Bloom, 2022) supports interpretability experiments on open models, and the Inspect framework of the UK AI Security Institute supports evaluations.
  • Bring mathematics. Statistics of evaluations, game theory of control and governance, optimisation theory of reward hacking, high-dimensional geometry of representations: each section of these notes has an open problem that needs an applied mathematician.
  • Follow structured programmes. The BlueDot Impact courses on alignment and governance, listed by the course as an official resource, give reading lists and cohorts.

10.7 Open problems

  1. Give a capability-based trigger, measurable before deployment, that would replace or complement a compute threshold.
  2. Find a training signal for tasks that humans cannot evaluate, with a guarantee in the spirit of Theorem 9.6 that survives imperfect play.
  3. Decide, from weights and activations, whether a model contains a backdoor of the kind studied by Hubinger et al. (2024).
  4. Bound the elicitation gap: how much capability can remain hidden after a given elicitation budget?
  5. Design control protocols for agents whose catch probability can be estimated with confidence intervals.
  6. Explain emergent misalignment: why does narrow fine-tuning change broad dispositions?

(b) With T = 5, R = 3 and P = 1, find the threshold on \delta when q is 1, 0.5 or 0.25.

    Solution: (a) The value W of deviating satisfies W = T + \delta\big(qP/(1 - \delta) + (1 - q)W\big). Compare W with R/(1 - \delta) and rearrange: \delta q (R - P) \ge (1 - \delta)(T - R). For q = 1 this is Theorem 8.2.

    (b) The right-hand side is 2/(2q), so \delta \ge 0.5, 2/3 and 0.8. Better verification makes cooperation possible for less patient players.

  15. ★★★ Weak-to-strong generalization (Python). On synthetic data, train a strong model on labels from a weak supervisor, and compute the PGR of Definition 9.7 for two kinds of supervision: a weak model that sees only 4 informative features, whose errors are a simple function of the input, and labels with 30 % random flips.

    Solution:

    import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=12000, n_features=20, n_informative=10, n_redundant=0,
                           flip_y=0.0, class_sep=1.0, shuffle=False, random_state=0)
Xw, Xr, yw, yr = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.75, random_state=0)
Xs, Xte, ys, yte = train_test_split(Xr, yr, test_size=0.4, random_state=0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

weak = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(Xw[:, :4], yw)   # sees 4 informative features
labels = {
    "systematic": weak.predict(Xs[:, :4]),                   # errors: a simple function of x
    "random": np.where(rng.random(len(ys)) < 0.3, 1 - ys, ys),  # errors independent of x
}
strong = lambda: HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0)
a_ceil = (strong().fit(Xs, ys).predict(Xte) == yte).mean()
for name, lab in labels.items():
    a_weak = (lab == ys).mean()                               # accuracy of the supervision
    a_w2s = (strong().fit(Xs, lab).predict(Xte) == yte).mean()
    pgr = (a_w2s - a_weak) / (a_ceil - a_weak)
    print(f"{name:10s} weak {a_weak:.3f}  student {a_w2s:.3f}  ceiling {a_ceil:.3f}  PGR {pgr:.2f}")

    With systematic errors, the student reaches 0.608 from a supervisor at 0.598: PGR 0.03, since a flexible student learns the supervisor’s errors exactly. With random errors, it reaches 0.806 from 0.703: PGR 0.45, since random errors average out.

    The open question is which regime human supervision of superhuman models resembles; human errors are systematic, which is what makes the problem hard. Extensions: early stopping, an auxiliary confidence loss as in Burns et al. (2023).

  16. ★★★ Upfront auditing against an imperfect attacker. In Theorem 9.2, suppose that an attack after the audit period succeeds only with probability s, and otherwise fails unnoticed. (a) Write the attacker’s winning probability. (b) With p = \lambda/N and N \to \infty, find the optimal \lambda and the limit value. (c) Evaluate it for f = 0.02 and s = 0.25, and discuss which protocol of ch. 9.3 you would add.

    Hints and expected result: (a) (1 - p)^{M}\big(1 - (1 - ps)^{N - M}\big). (b) With a = s(1 - f), the limit is e^{-\lambda f}(1 - e^{-\lambda a}), maximised at \lambda^* = \log\big((a + f)/f\big)/a, with value \left(\frac{f}{a + f}\right)^{f/a}\frac{a}{a + f}. For s = 1 this is Theorem 9.2. (c) About 0.749, so safety is about 25 %; a simulation with N = 1000 gives 0.745. Trusted monitoring with c above 0.75 would do better, and the two can be combined.

  17. ★★★ Position paper: releasing frontier model weights. Write a two-page position for or against releasing the weights of a model just above the threshold of Definition 8.3. State your definition of the risk (Definition 3.1), your evidence, a probability that could be scored (Theorem 2.4), the safeguards you would require, and the strongest argument of the other side.

    Hints and expected result: a good answer separates benefits (research access, reproducibility, competition) from risks (irreversibility, removal of safeguards by fine-tuning, transfer of attacks found on open models, ch. 5.2). It uses evaluations of marginal risk with honest statistics (ch. 7), and names the evidence that would change its conclusion.

Projects and assessment

The official assessment is 100 % project, with the details given in the course syllabus (master-mva.com); Université Paris-Saclay describes it as “Evaluation Continue non Intégrale”. The syllabus itself is not reproduced in the catalogue, so the subjects below are proposals sized for a seminar project: a precise question, code or a proof, a short report and a talk. Each one reproduces or extends published work, in the tradition of MVA projects. The official assessment has no written exam; the exam proposal is for self-assessment.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: “Toy Models of Superposition” (Elhage et al., 2022). When do sparse features share dimensions?
    • Implement the model of ch. 6.2 and reproduce the phase diagram for two features in one dimension: represented, superposed or ignored, as a function of sparsity and relative importance.
    • Reproduce the geometry for five features in two dimensions, and measure how the bias filters interference.
    • Success criterion: a phase diagram with the three regions, with boundaries stable over 5 seeds. Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters 0 and 6.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: reward-model over-optimisation (Gao, Schulman and Hilton, 2023), synthetic version. Does best-of-n against a learned proxy follow the law d(\alpha - \beta d) that the paper reports, with d the square root of the KL divergence?
    • Gold reward: a fixed random two-layer network on Gaussian inputs. Proxy: a Bradley–Terry model (Definition 4.5) fitted on 100 to 10,000 noisy comparisons.
    • Measure the gold reward of best-of-n against d, using Theorem 4.4 for the KL.
    • Success criterion: the rise and fall, a fitted curve with confidence bands, and the peak location as a function of the number of comparisons. Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 0 and 4.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Forecasting study: the growth of training compute. Re-estimate the doubling time of the training compute of notable models from the public database of notable AI models maintained by Epoch AI, and compare with Sevilla et al. (2022).
    • Fit log-compute against time (Definition 0.4), with bootstrap intervals, and test for a change of slope.
    • Turn the fit into a probabilistic forecast of when a model crosses 10^{26} FLOP, scored after the fact with Theorem 2.4.
    • Success criterion: a doubling time with a 90 % interval and a written forecast. Deliverables: notebook, 4-page report, 10-minute talk. Chapters 1 and 2.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Paper study: AI control (Greenblatt et al., 2024) in a toy setting. Generated programming tasks, small Python functions with unit tests, are solved by a scripted untrusted policy that sometimes inserts a backdoor, such as a special input with a wrong output.
    • Member 1: the task generator and the red-team policies (attack rate, subtlety of the backdoor).
    • Member 2: the trusted monitor, a small classifier on features of the code, and its ROC curve.
    • Member 3: the protocols of ch. 9: upfront auditing, trusted monitoring, defer to trusted.
    • Success criterion: a safety–usefulness frontier with confidence intervals, which matches Theorem 9.2 and Proposition 9.4 in the cases they cover. Deliverables: code, a 6–8 page report, a 20-minute talk. Chapters 3, 7 and 9.
  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Paper study: “Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability” (Nanda et al., 2023). Train a one-layer transformer on addition modulo 113 on a CPU or a free GPU tier, and reverse-engineer it.
    • Member 1: training, and the grokking curve of train and test accuracy.
    • Member 2: Fourier analysis of the embeddings and logits; linear probes for the key frequencies (Definition 6.1).
    • Member 3: causal tests (Definition 6.5): ablate the key frequencies, then all others.
    • Success criterion: test accuracy collapses when the few key frequencies are removed and is preserved when all the others are. Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 0 and 6.
  3. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] An honest evaluation: the elicitation gap of a small open model. Measure how much accuracy elicitation adds for a small open-weight instruction model (0.5 to 1.5 billion parameters) on the GSM8K test set (Cobbe et al., 2021).
    • Member 1: the evaluation harness, with zero-shot, few-shot and chain-of-thought prompting.
    • Member 2: repeated sampling, unbiased pass@k (Theorem 7.2) and majority voting.
    • Member 3: statistics (Hoeffding and bootstrap intervals, paired comparisons) and a sandbagging test: prompt the model to underperform and try to detect it.
    • Success criterion: an elicitation curve with error bars, and a detection rate for sandbagging. Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 5 and 7.

Written exam (proposal)

Two hours, no documents, a non-programmable calculator allowed; four questions of 5 points.

  1. Question (5 points). Give the three families of risk of Definition 3.1 with one example each. Then name the four variants of Goodhart’s law, illustrate two of them, and explain why two safeguards built from the same model may protect less than their individual failure rates suggest. Expected: Definition 3.1 and Definition 4.1 with correct examples (for instance, CoastRunners as extremal, and sycophancy as a policy exploiting the gap between approval and quality); Proposition 3.2, where a common mode \rho sets a floor on the breach probability.

  2. Question (5 points). (a) Compute the training compute of a model with 3 \times 10^{10} parameters trained on 6 \times 10^{12} tokens, and compare it with the EU threshold. (b) Under Theorem 1.4 with \alpha = 0.34 and \beta = 0.28, by which factors should N and D grow when compute grows by 100? (c) Ten samples contain one success: estimate pass@3.

    Expected: (a) 1.08 \times 10^{24} FLOP, below 10^{25}. (b) 100^{0.45} \approx 7.9 for parameters and 100^{0.55} \approx 12.6 for tokens. (c) 1 - \binom{9}{3}/\binom{10}{3} = 1 - 84/120 = 0.3.

  3. Question (5 points). State and prove Theorem 4.6 on the KL-regularised optimum. Deduce the DPO loss, and explain why the partition function does not need to be computed. Expected: the identity J(\pi) = \beta\log Z - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^*) and Gibbs’ inequality; inversion of the optimum; cancellation of \beta \log Z(x) in the Bradley–Terry difference.

  4. Question (5 points). Prove Theorem 4.4 on the KL divergence of best-of-n. Find the n that gives about 2 nats, and explain with Proposition 4.3 why a larger n can lower the true quality of the selected answer.

    Expected: the density nF^{n-1}, \mathbb{E}[\log W] = -1/n; n \approx 19, since \log 19 - 18/19 \approx 1.997; with heavy-tailed proxy errors, the most extreme proxy scores are explained by the errors, so the true value reverts to the mean.
