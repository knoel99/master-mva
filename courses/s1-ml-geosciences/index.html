Audience: students with a good M1 in mathematics, computer science or physics who want to learn how statistical learning is adapted to images and signals produced by physical sensors, with subsurface exploration and energy as the running case.

Empirical first: on synthetic well logs built to mimic real ones, a random forest reaches 97 % accuracy under random cross-validation and 49 % on wells it has never seen (code 7.3). Neither the model nor the data are at fault; the evaluation is. This course is about such traps, and about the physics that explains them.

Geophysical tools never photograph the rock. They send a seismic, electromagnetic, ultrasonic or mechanical field into the ground, record a response, and leave the rest to inference. The course follows that chain from acquisition to interpretation: describe the measurement, compress it, sharpen it, judge its quality, align it, enhance and repair it, classify and segment it, and finally invert it.

At each step it puts a classical method, a statistical-learning method and a deep-learning method side by side, and it asks what changes when the model leaves the laboratory. Closely related MVA courses are Deep Learning, Image Denoising, Sub-Pixel Image Processing and Machine Learning for Time Series; in semester 2, Inverse Problems and Imaging and Remote Sensing Data take several threads further.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the forward model of a measurement system (well log, borehole image, seismic trace, ultrasonic echo) and read its resolution limits.
  • Compress images and logs with transform coding, PCA and learned codecs, and predict the distortion from the rate.
  • Formulate super-resolution as an ill-posed estimation problem and explain why mean-squared-error networks blur.
  • Choose and compute quality measures (PSNR, SSIM, noise estimates) and ranking metrics, and fit scores from pairwise expert preferences.
  • Align logs and images with cross-correlation, phase correlation, dynamic time warping and Procrustes registration.
  • Enhance contrast with histogram methods and fill gaps by variational and learned inpainting.
  • Train and validate facies classifiers and segmentation networks without leaking information across wells.
  • Solve linear and sparse inverse problems (Tikhonov, ISTA) and combine them with learning and physical constraints.
  • Diagnose distribution shift, quantify uncertainty with conformal prediction, and fit a model to compute and memory budgets.
  • Plan the path from a research prototype to a tool that interpreters can trust.

0. Review: linear algebra, signals, probability and optimisation

Every method of this course is a variation on one situation: an unknown property, a measurement, and an operator that links them. This review gathers the M1 tools that the later chapters use without stopping: the singular value decomposition, convolution and sampling, the Bayesian reading of regularisation, gradient descent, and the convolutional layer.

Definition 0.1 — Singular value decomposition and pseudo-inverse Every matrix A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} of rank r factorises as A = U \Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^\top, \qquad \sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0, with orthonormal families (u_i) in \mathbb{R}^m and (v_i) in \mathbb{R}^n. The pseudo-inverse is A^+ = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \frac{1}{\sigma_i}\, v_i u_i^\top . The vector x^+ = A^+ y minimises \|Ax - y\|_2, and among all minimisers it has the smallest norm.

Theorem 0.2 — Eckart–Young For k < r, let A_k = \sum_{i \le k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top. For every matrix B of rank at most k: \|A - B\|_F^2 \;\ge\; \|A - A_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{i > k} \sigma_i^2 .

Proof. Weyl’s inequality for singular values states that \sigma_{i+j-1}(X + Y) \le \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_j(Y).

Apply it with X = A - B, Y = B and j = k + 1. Since \operatorname{rank} B \le k, \sigma_{k+1}(B) = 0, hence for every i: \sigma_{i+k}(A) \le \sigma_i(A - B).

Summing the squares gives \|A - B\|_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i(A - B)^2 \ge \sum_i \sigma_{i+k}(A)^2 = \sum_{i > k} \sigma_i^2 . For B = A_k the residual is \sum_{i > k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top, whose squared Frobenius norm is exactly that sum, by orthonormality. ∎

This is the theorem behind PCA compression (ch. 2) and behind every truncated-SVD estimator of ch. 8.

Definition 0.3 — Circular convolution and discrete Fourier transform For x, h \in \mathbb{C}^n with indices taken modulo n: (x \circledast h)_k = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} x_m\, h_{k-m}, \qquad \hat x_\ell = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x_k\, e^{-2i\pi k\ell/n}.

Proposition 0.4 — Convolution theorem The DFT turns convolution into a pointwise product: \widehat{x \circledast h}_\ell = \hat x_\ell\, \hat h_\ell .

Proof. Substitute the definition and set j = k - m (modulo n): \sum_k \sum_m x_m h_{k-m} e^{-2i\pi k\ell/n} = \sum_m x_m e^{-2i\pi m\ell/n} \sum_j h_j e^{-2i\pi j\ell/n} = \hat x_\ell\, \hat h_\ell . The exchange of sums is legitimate because both are finite. ∎

Consequences used later: a linear, shift-invariant tool acts frequency by frequency; deconvolution divides by \hat h_\ell and explodes where it is small (ch. 8); cross-correlations for all shifts cost \mathcal{O}(n \log n) with the FFT (ch. 5).

Theorem 0.5 — Sampling (Shannon–Nyquist) and aliasing Let x(t) have finite energy and a Fourier transform supported in [-B, B]. If the sampling rate satisfies f_s > 2B, the samples determine the signal: x(t) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} x\!\left(\tfrac{k}{f_s}\right) \operatorname{sinc}(f_s t - k), \qquad \operatorname{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin \pi u}{\pi u}. If instead f_s < 2B, a cosine of frequency f and one of frequency |f - m f_s|, for an integer m, take the same values on the grid (aliasing).

Proof. Sketch. By the Poisson summation formula, the spectrum of the sampled signal is the periodisation of \hat x with period f_s. The copies do not overlap when f_s > 2B, so an ideal low-pass filter on [-f_s/2, f_s/2] recovers \hat x; in time, that filter is the sinc interpolation.

For aliasing, the two arguments below differ by 2\pi m k, so for every integer k: \cos(2\pi f k / f_s) = \cos(2\pi (f - m f_s) k / f_s). \qquad ∎

Every tool samples, in depth or in time. The sampling interval fixes the highest frequency that can be recovered; super-resolution (ch. 3) is precisely about what lies beyond it.

Proposition 0.6 — MAP estimation is regularised least squares Let y = Ax + \varepsilon with noise \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I) and prior x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2 I). The posterior mode is

Key result — the regularised least-squares estimator \hat x_\lambda = \arg\min_x \|y - Ax\|_2^2 + \lambda \|x\|_2^2 = (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1} A^\top y, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}.

Proof. Up to a constant, the negative log-posterior is \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|y - Ax\|^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|x\|^2 . Multiply by 2\sigma^2. The result is a strictly convex quadratic, since its Hessian 2(A^\top A + \lambda I) is positive definite. Its unique minimiser cancels the gradient 2A^\top(Ax - y) + 2\lambda x, which gives the formula. ∎

The ratio of noise variance to prior variance is the regularisation weight: the less we trust the data, the more we lean on the prior. Chapters 3 and 8 use this estimator.

Proposition 0.7 — Gradient descent on a smooth convex function Let f be convex with an L-Lipschitz gradient and a minimiser x^\star. The iterates x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{1}{L} \nabla f(x_k) satisfy f(x_k) - f(x^\star) \le \frac{L\, \|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{2k}.

Proof. Sketch. The descent lemma gives f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 . Combined with convexity, this yields f(x_{k+1}) - f^\star \le \frac{L}{2}\left(\|x_k - x^\star\|^2 - \|x_{k+1} - x^\star\|^2\right). Summing over k telescopes the right-hand side, and the values decrease, so the last one is below the average. ∎

The same argument proves the convergence of ISTA in ch. 8.

Definition 0.8 — Convolutional layer and receptive field A one-dimensional convolutional layer with kernel size k maps C input channels to C' output channels: y_{c,t} = b_c + \sum_{c'=1}^{C} \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} W_{c,c',j}\, x_{c',\, t + j - \lfloor k/2 \rfloor}, followed by a pointwise nonlinearity such as \mathrm{ReLU}(u) = \max(u, 0). It commutes with translations, and its weights are shared across positions.

Stacking L such layers with stride 1 gives each output a receptive field of 1 + L(k - 1) input samples. The proof is an induction: each layer adds \lfloor k/2 \rfloor samples on each side.

Two-dimensional layers work the same way on images. Encoder–decoder networks with skip connections (U-Net, ch. 7.2) combine a large receptive field with precise localisation.

Code 0.9 — checking the convolution theorem, aliasing and least squares.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 64
x, h = rng.standard_normal(n), rng.standard_normal(n)

# circular convolution, directly and through the DFT (Proposition 0.4)
direct = np.array([sum(x[m] * h[(k - m) % n] for m in range(n)) for k in range(n)])
via_fft = np.real(np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(x) * np.fft.fft(h)))
print("max |direct - FFT| =", np.abs(direct - via_fft).max())

# aliasing (Theorem 0.5): 70 Hz sampled at 100 Hz looks like 30 Hz
t = np.arange(50) / 100.0
gap = np.abs(np.cos(2 * np.pi * 70 * t) - np.cos(2 * np.pi * 30 * t)).max()
print("max |cos(70 Hz) - cos(30 Hz)| on the grid =", gap)

# least squares through the pseudo-inverse (Definition 0.1)
A = rng.standard_normal((20, 5))
y = rng.standard_normal(20)
x_ls = np.linalg.pinv(A) @ y
print("normal equations residual:", np.abs(A.T @ (A @ x_ls - y)).max())

All three printed numbers are at the level of rounding errors (about 10^{-14}): the two convolutions agree, the 70 Hz and 30 Hz cosines coincide on a 100 Hz grid, and the pseudo-inverse solves the normal equations.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The SVD diagonalises any linear operator; truncating it gives the best low-rank approximation (Eckart–Young).
  • Convolution becomes multiplication in the Fourier domain, and sampling fixes the highest recoverable frequency.
  • A Gaussian prior turns least squares into ridge regression, with weight equal to the ratio of noise to prior variance.
  • Gradient descent on a smooth convex function converges at rate 1/k.
  • A stack of convolutional layers has a receptive field that grows linearly with depth.

1. Measurement systems: images and logs

A geoscientist never observes porosity, a fluid or a fault directly. A tool emits a physical field, records a response, and the property is inferred. This chapter models that chain, so that every later task (compressing, enhancing, aligning, classifying, inverting) is stated on the right object.

1.1 Measurement as a forward model

Definition 1.1 — Measurement system A measurement system links an unknown property field x (for instance resistivity as a function of position) to recorded data y through y = \mathcal{M}(x) + \varepsilon . The forward operator \mathcal{M} contains the physics of the probing field, the response of the tool and the sampling; \varepsilon is the noise.

Three numbers summarise a tool: its resolution (the smallest feature it separates), its depth of investigation (how far from the sensor it sees) and its sampling interval.

Computing y from x is the forward problem, a simulation that is usually well-posed. Recovering x from y is the inverse problem of ch. 8. Most “images” of this course are not photographs: each pixel is a physical measurement with a unit, a resolution and an error.

1.2 Well logs

Definition 1.2 — Well log A well log is a depth-indexed multivariate series x(z_k) \in \mathbb{R}^p, \qquad z_k = z_0 + k\, \Delta z, \quad k = 0, \dots, n - 1, whose p curves (gamma ray, resistivity, density, neutron, sonic…) are recorded by tools pulled along the borehole. Conventional logs are sampled every few tens of centimetres or less; each curve has its own unit, range and missing intervals.

Proposition 1.3 — The vertical response of a tool is a convolution Let a tool average the true property with a vertical response g \ge 0 of integral 1: m(z) = \int g(z - z')\, x(z')\, dz' . If g is a box of width w, a bed of thickness h < w with contrast \Delta over a uniform background appears with an apparent contrast of at most \frac{h}{w}\, \Delta .

Proof. Write x = x_0 + \Delta\, \mathbb{1}_{\text{bed}}. Then m(z) - x_0 = \Delta \cdot \frac{|\text{window}(z) \cap \text{bed}|}{w}. The intersection of a window of length w with a bed of length h < w has length at most h, and exactly h when the window covers the bed. ∎

Example 1.4 — A thin sand in a shale A tool with a 60 cm box response logs a 15 cm sand (gamma ray 30 API) inside a shale (110 API), so \Delta = -80 API. The apparent contrast is at most \frac{15}{60} \times (-80) = -20 \text{ API}, so the log reads about 90 API at the sand and the sand is almost invisible. Thinly laminated reservoirs are underestimated for this reason, and the Fourier view (Proposition 0.4) says why: the tool is a low-pass filter.

Physics as a model — Archie’s law Archie (1942) observed that, in clean formations, the true resistivity R_t relates to porosity \phi and water saturation S_w through R_t = \frac{a\, R_w}{\phi^{m} S_w^{n}}, with R_w the water resistivity and empirical constants a, m, n. Resistivity spans several orders of magnitude, so models work with \log R_t; in that variable Archie’s law is linear. Such laws are the “physical models” with which learned models must stay consistent (ch. 8, ch. 9).

1.3 Borehole images

An imaging tool records many logs around the borehole wall at once. Resistivity tools press several pads against the wall and cover part of the circumference; rotating ultrasonic transducers cover all of it. Unrolling the cylinder gives an image I(z, \varphi), with depth z downwards and azimuth \varphi \in [0, 2\pi) across.

Theorem 1.5 — A plane cuts the borehole along a sinusoid Let a vertical cylindrical borehole of radius r be cut by a plane of dip \theta (angle to the horizontal) that dips towards azimuth \varphi_d. In the unrolled image, the trace of the plane is z(\varphi) = z_0 + r \tan\theta \, \cos(\varphi - \varphi_d).

Proof. Take the borehole axis as the z axis. Depth along the plane increases with slope \tan\theta in the direction (\cos\varphi_d, \sin\varphi_d), so the plane is z = z_0 + \tan\theta\,(X \cos\varphi_d + Y \sin\varphi_d). A point of the wall at azimuth \varphi has X = r\cos\varphi and Y = r\sin\varphi. Substituting and using the identity \cos\varphi\cos\varphi_d + \sin\varphi\sin\varphi_d = \cos(\varphi - \varphi_d) gives the formula. ∎

Fitting is linear. Expanding the cosine, z(\varphi) = a + b\cos\varphi + c\sin\varphi with b = r\tan\theta\cos\varphi_d and c = r\tan\theta\sin\varphi_d. Least squares on picked points gives (a, b, c), then \tan\theta = \frac{\sqrt{b^2 + c^2}}{r}, \qquad \varphi_d = \operatorname{atan2}(c, b).

Example 1.6 — Sizes of sinusoids In an 8.5-inch borehole, r = 10.8 cm.

  • A bed dipping at 30° gives an amplitude r \tan 30° = 6.2 cm, so the sinusoid spans 12.5 cm from top to bottom.
  • A fracture dipping at 80° gives r \tan 80° = 61 cm: its sinusoid spans 1.23 m.

Steep fractures therefore cross many image rows, and a pad gap (ch. 6.2) cuts them into separate pieces.

1.4 Seismic data: the convolutional model

Definition 1.7 — Impedance, reflectivity and the convolutional trace The acoustic impedance of a layer is Z = \rho v (density times velocity). At normal incidence, the interface between layers 1 and 2 reflects a fraction R = \frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1} of the amplitude; the formula follows from the continuity of pressure and particle velocity at the interface (Sheriff and Geldart, 1995).

The convolutional model writes a trace as s(t) = (w * r)(t) + n(t), \qquad r(t) = \sum_k R_k\, \delta(t - t_k), where t_k is the two-way travel time to interface k and w is the source wavelet, often a Ricker wavelet w(t) = (1 - 2\pi^2 f^2 t^2)\, e^{-\pi^2 f^2 t^2}.

Proposition 1.8 — Thin beds and tuning A bed with top reflection R and base reflection -R, separated by a two-way time \tau, produces s(t) = R\,[w(t) - w(t - \tau)] = R\, \tau\, w'(t) + \mathcal{O}(\tau^2). Below the resolution limit, the amplitude grows linearly with the thickness and the shape is the derivative of the wavelet; near a quarter wavelength the two reflections interfere constructively (tuning).

Proof. Taylor’s formula with integral remainder gives w(t - \tau) = w(t) - \tau w'(t) + \mathcal{O}(\tau^2), uniformly in t since w'' is bounded. Substitute into R[w(t) - w(t - \tau)]. ∎

Example 1.9 — Tuning with numbers Take v = 3000 m/s and a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet, so the wavelength is \lambda = v / f = 100 m. The bed has Z_2 = 7.5 \times 10^6 in a background Z_1 = 6.0 \times 10^6 (SI units), so R = 0.111.

The scale gap is the central fact of subsurface data. Seismic resolves tens of metres over kilometres; logs resolve decimetres along one well; borehole images resolve centimetres around it. Tying these scales together is the job of alignment (ch. 5) and inversion (ch. 8).

1.5 Ultrasonic pulse–echo and casing corrosion

An ultrasonic transducer emits a short pulse and records the echo. The travel time t to a reflector at distance d in a fluid of sound speed c gives d = ct/2; rotating the transducer maps the borehole radius around the wall, which reveals ovalisation and breakouts.

Proposition 1.10 — Thickness resonance of a plate A pulse entering a steel plate (a casing) of thickness e reverberates between its two faces. With compressional speed c in the plate, the echo train resonates at f_n = \frac{n\, c}{2e}, \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots

Proof. Inside the plate, the wave is reflected at both faces by a fluid of lower impedance, so each reflection flips the sign and a round trip adds no net sign change. Successive echoes are then delayed by a path of 2e, and they add in phase when 2e is a whole number n of wavelengths c / f, which is the stated condition. ∎

With c \approx 5900 m/s in steel and e = 10 mm, f_1 \approx 295 kHz. Corrosion thins the casing, so the resonance frequency rises; measured around and along the well, it becomes a thickness image e(z, \varphi) on which metal loss is segmented (ch. 7.2).

Electromagnetic inspection rests on another physics: eddy currents induced in the metal decay over the skin depth \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega \mu \sigma}}, which depends on the frequency \omega, the magnetic permeability \mu and the conductivity \sigma. Lower frequencies probe deeper.

1.6 What makes these data hard

  • Physical units and dynamic range. Resistivity spans orders of magnitude, so it is modelled in \log scale; each curve has its own noise and artefacts.
  • Several scales. Each tool sees its own resolution and depth of investigation (Example 1.9).
  • Labels are interpretations. Facies, faults or fractures are drawn by experts who may disagree, and labelled wells are few (ch. 9.1).
  • Every well is a new environment. Tool generation, borehole size, drilling mud and geology change the input distribution (ch. 7.1, ch. 9.2).
  • Volumes and budgets. 3D surveys are large and downhole links are slow (ch. 2, ch. 9.4).
  • Costly decisions. Predictions feed drilling and production decisions, so uncertainty and interpretability matter (ch. 9.3).

2. Compression

Subsurface data are large, and the channels that carry them can be tiny. A 3D seismic survey can reach terabytes; while drilling, data computed downhole travel to the surface by mud-pulse telemetry, at rates of the order of a few bits to a few tens of bits per second. This chapter asks how many bits a signal really needs for a given error.

2.1 Rate, distortion and quality

Definition 2.1 — Codec, rate, distortion, PSNR A codec is a pair of maps: an encoder from signals x \in \mathbb{R}^n to bit strings, and a decoder from bit strings back to reconstructions \hat x. Its rate R is the expected number of bits per sample, its distortion the mean squared error D = \frac{1}{n}\, \mathbb{E}\, \|x - \hat x\|_2^2 . For images with peak value P (1 for images in [0, 1], 255 for 8-bit images), the peak signal-to-noise ratio is \mathrm{PSNR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{P^2}{\mathrm{MSE}} \quad \text{(in dB)}.

PSNR is only a starting point: ch. 4 discusses measures that track structure and the downstream task.

2.2 Transform coding

The classical recipe has three steps: transform the signal into coefficients that concentrate its energy, quantise the coefficients, and code the quantised values without loss (entropy coding).

Proposition 2.2 — Orthonormal transforms preserve the error If T is orthonormal (T^\top T = I) and c = Tx, \hat c are coefficients, then \|x - T^\top \hat c\|_2 = \|c - \hat c\|_2 .

Proof. A square orthonormal matrix also satisfies T T^\top = I, hence \|T^\top (c - \hat c)\|_2^2 = (c - \hat c)^\top T T^\top (c - \hat c) = \|c - \hat c\|_2^2 . \qquad ∎

The error can therefore be budgeted coefficient by coefficient.

Theorem 2.3 — Best k-term approximation In an orthonormal basis, among all approximations that keep k coefficients of x and set the others to zero, the smallest error is obtained by keeping the k coefficients of largest magnitude. The squared error is the sum of the squares of the discarded coefficients.

Proof. By Proposition 2.2, keeping a set S of k coefficients leaves the error \sum_{i \notin S} c_i^2. This sum is smallest when the complement of S holds the n - k smallest values of c_i^2. ∎

Theorem 2.4 — PCA is the optimal linear code Let x be a zero-mean random vector with covariance C = \sum_{i} \lambda_i e_i e_i^\top, where \lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n. For every orthogonal projector P of rank k: \mathbb{E}\, \|x - Px\|^2 \;\ge\; \sum_{i > k} \lambda_i , with equality when P projects onto \mathrm{span}(e_1, \dots, e_k).

Proof.

  1. Write P = Q Q^\top with Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} orthonormal. Since I - P is a projector, \mathbb{E}\|x - Px\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\, x^\top (I - P) x = \operatorname{tr}(C) - \operatorname{tr}(Q^\top C Q).
  2. Expand in the eigenbasis: \operatorname{tr}(Q^\top C Q) = \sum_i \lambda_i\, \|Q^\top e_i\|^2 . The weights \|Q^\top e_i\|^2 lie in [0, 1] and sum to \operatorname{tr}(Q^\top Q) = k.
  3. A weighted sum of the \lambda_i with such weights is at most \lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k: move weight towards the largest values. Equality holds for Q = [e_1, \dots, e_k].
  4. Hence \mathbb{E}\|x - Px\|^2 \ge \operatorname{tr}(C) - \sum_{i \le k} \lambda_i = \sum_{i > k} \lambda_i . \qquad ∎

On data, C is the empirical covariance and Theorem 0.2 gives the same subspace through the SVD of the centred data matrix.

PCA must transmit its basis, which is costly. The discrete cosine transform (DCT) needs none, and for strongly correlated stationary signals it is close to the PCA basis (Ahmed, Natarajan and Rao, 1974); this is why JPEG uses it. Wavelets (Mallat, 2009) suit piecewise-smooth signals, such as blocky logs, because only coefficients near the jumps are large.

2.3 Quantisation and the rate–distortion limit

Proposition 2.5 — Error of a uniform quantiser Rounding to the nearest multiple of a step \Delta makes an error e \in [-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]. If the signal varies on a scale much larger than \Delta, the error is close to uniform on that interval, and \mathbb{E}\, e^2 \approx \frac{\Delta^2}{12} .

Proof. For e uniform on [-\Delta/2, \Delta/2], \mathbb{E}\, e^2 = \frac{1}{\Delta}\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2} u^2\, du = \frac{\Delta^2}{12}. The uniformity is the high-resolution approximation: a smooth density is almost constant over each cell. ∎

Theorem 2.6 — Rate–distortion function of a Gaussian source (Shannon) For independent samples of \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), the smallest rate at which a distortion D \le \sigma^2 can be reached is R(D) = \frac{1}{2} \log_2 \frac{\sigma^2}{D}.

Key result — six decibels per bit D(R) = \sigma^2\, 2^{-2R}: \quad \text{each extra bit per sample divides the distortion by 4, a gain of } 6.02 \text{ dB}.

Proof. Lower bound. Any codec at rate R satisfies R \ge I(X; \hat X), the mutual information. Since the Gaussian has maximal entropy for a given variance, I(X; \hat X) = h(X) - h(X \mid \hat X) \ge h(X) - h(X - \hat X) \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e \sigma^2) - \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e D). Achievability uses random coding over long blocks; see Cover and Thomas (2006, ch. 10). ∎

For independent components \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2), the optimal allocation is reverse water-filling: each component receives the distortion D_i = \min(\theta, \sigma_i^2) for a common level \theta. Components with variance below \theta are not coded at all, which is exactly why discarding small transform coefficients is sound.

2.4 Learned compression

  • Linear autoencoders. A network x \mapsto W_2 W_1 x trained with squared error learns the PCA subspace: all its local minima are global and span the top principal directions (Baldi and Hornik, 1989). Theorem 2.4 is therefore the baseline of every learned codec.
  • Nonlinear transform coding. An encoder f_\theta, a quantiser Q, an entropy model p_\psi of the quantised code, and a decoder g_\phi are trained together on a rate–distortion loss (Ballé, Laparra and Simoncelli, 2017).
  • The training trick. Rounding has zero gradient, so during training Q is replaced by additive uniform noise on [-1/2, 1/2], which has the same error statistics as Proposition 2.5.

The loss reads \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\big[-\log_2 p_\psi(Q(f_\theta(x)))\big] + \lambda\, \mathbb{E}\, \|x - g_\phi(Q(f_\theta(x)))\|^2 , where the first term is the code length after entropy coding and \lambda selects a point on the rate–distortion curve.

Industrial constraints shape the design. The encoder runs downhole on small, hot, low-power hardware, while the decoder runs at the surface and can be large (ch. 9.4). And a codec must not invent geology: a compressed image is judged on the features an interpreter picks from it (ch. 4.1), not only on its PSNR.

Example 2.7 — DCT against patch PCA on a borehole-like image Code 2.8 builds a 256 × 256 unrolled image with dipping beds, two fractures and noise, scaled to [0, 1].

  • Keeping the largest 1 %, 5 % and 10 % of global DCT coefficients gives 28.7, 32.7 and 34.2 dB.
  • PCA on 8 × 8 patches with 3 or 6 components out of 64 (4.7 % and 9.4 %) gives 28.0 and 30.8 dB.
  • Measured quantisation errors match \Delta^2 / 12 within 2 % at 4, 6 and 8 bits.

The percentages ignore the cost of coding the positions of the kept DCT coefficients and the PCA basis; a real codec must pay for both.

Code 2.8 — transform coding of a synthetic borehole image.

import numpy as np
from scipy.fft import dctn, idctn

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
H, W = 256, 256
z, j = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W].astype(float)                    # depth, azimuth index
phi = 2 * np.pi * j / W
# synthetic unrolled borehole image: dipping layers + two fractures + noise
img = np.sin(2 * np.pi * (z + 12 * np.cos(phi - 1.0)) / 23)
for z0, amp in [(80, 30), (170, 18)]:
    img += 2.0 * np.exp(-((z - z0 - amp * np.cos(phi - 2.5)) / 1.5) ** 2)
img += 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((H, W))
img = (img - img.min()) / (img.max() - img.min())          # range [0, 1]

def psnr(x, y, peak=1.0):                                  # Definition 2.1
    return 10 * np.log10(peak ** 2 / np.mean((x - y) ** 2))

c = dctn(img, norm="ortho")                                # orthonormal 2D DCT
for frac in [0.01, 0.05, 0.10]:
    thr = np.quantile(np.abs(c), 1 - frac)                 # keep the largest (Thm 2.3)
    rec = idctn(np.where(np.abs(c) >= thr, c, 0), norm="ortho")
    print(f"DCT, keep {frac:4.0%}: PSNR = {psnr(img, rec):5.2f} dB")

# PCA on 8x8 patches (Theorem 2.4): k of 64 coefficients per patch
P = img.reshape(32, 8, 32, 8).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(-1, 64)
mu = P.mean(0)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(P - mu, full_matrices=False)
for k in [1, 3, 6]:
    rec = (P - mu) @ Vt[:k].T @ Vt[:k] + mu
    rec = rec.reshape(32, 32, 8, 8).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(H, W)
    print(f"PCA, k = {k} ({k / 64:4.1%}): PSNR = {psnr(img, rec):5.2f} dB")

# uniform quantisation (Proposition 2.5): measured vs predicted MSE
for bits in [4, 6, 8]:
    step = 1.0 / 2 ** bits
    q = (np.floor(img / step) + 0.5) * step
    print(f"{bits} bits: MSE = {np.mean((img - q) ** 2):.2e}, step^2/12 = {step ** 2 / 12:.2e}")

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Rate is bits per sample, distortion is mean squared error, PSNR is distortion on a log scale.
  • With an orthonormal transform, keeping the largest coefficients is optimal for a given number of terms.
  • PCA is the optimal linear code; the DCT approximates it without transmitting a basis.
  • A uniform quantiser costs \Delta^2 / 12; for a Gaussian source each bit buys about 6 dB.
  • Learned codecs train encoder, entropy model and decoder on a rate–distortion loss, with noise in place of rounding.

3. Super-resolution

Logs are averaged over decimetres, seismic over tens of metres, and some imaging tools are coarser than the features interpreters need. Super-resolution estimates a finer signal from a coarser one. This chapter shows that the problem has infinitely many exact solutions, and that what a trained network returns depends on its loss.

3.1 The degradation model

Definition 3.1 — Degradation model A fine signal x \in \mathbb{R}^N is observed through a blur h (the tool response, Proposition 1.3), a decimation S_s that keeps one sample in s, and noise: y = S_s(h * x) + n = A x + n, \qquad A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times N}, \quad m = N / s .

Proposition 3.2 — Super-resolution is not unique The kernel of A has dimension at least N - m = N(1 - 1/s). Every x + v with v \in \ker A produces the same noise-free data as x.

Proof. By the rank–nullity theorem, \dim \ker A = N - \operatorname{rank} A \ge N - m. For v \in \ker A, A(x + v) = Ax. ∎

Super-resolution is therefore a choice inside an affine space of dimension at least N(1 - 1/s), and only prior knowledge can make it. In Fourier terms (Theorem 0.5), frequencies above the coarse Nyquist limit are folded onto lower ones and cannot be told apart from them without a prior.

3.2 Classical estimators

  • Interpolation (nearest, linear, cubic, sinc) assumes the signal is smooth or band-limited; it never restores what the blur removed.
  • Regularised inversion chooses the smoothest consistent signal, with D a finite-difference matrix: \hat x = \arg\min_x \|y - Ax\|^2 + \lambda \|Dx\|^2 = (A^\top A + \lambda D^\top D)^{-1} A^\top y . The matrix is invertible when \ker A \cap \ker D = \{0\}, since the quadratic form then vanishes only at zero.
  • Example-based regression learns the map from coarse to fine patches on pairs where both are known.

3.3 What a trained estimator converges to

Theorem 3.3 — The MMSE estimator is the posterior mean Among all measurable functions f with \mathbb{E}\|f(Y)\|^2 < \infty, \arg\min_f \; \mathbb{E}\, \|X - f(Y)\|^2 = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y].

Proof. Write X - f(Y) = (X - \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]) + (\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] - f(Y)). The second term is a function of Y, and the first is orthogonal to every square-integrable function of Y by the defining property of conditional expectation. The cross term therefore vanishes, and \mathbb{E}\|X - f(Y)\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\|X - \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] - f(Y)\|^2 , which is minimal exactly when f(Y) = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] almost surely. ∎

Example 3.4 — Why MSE networks blur Suppose that, given a coarse log, a sharp boundary between values 0 and 1 is equally likely at fine positions p and p + 1.

  • The posterior mean is 0 before p, 0.5 between p and p + 1, and 1 after.
  • That two-step ramp is a blurred edge that no real log contains.

A network trained with squared error converges towards this average (Theorem 3.3), hence the familiar smooth look of MSE super-resolution.

Proposition 3.5 — The absolute loss gives the median For a real random variable X with a continuous distribution, \arg\min_{c \in \mathbb{R}} \mathbb{E}\, |X - c| = \operatorname{med}(X). Trained per sample with the absolute loss, a network estimates the conditional median.

Proof. The function \varphi(c) = \mathbb{E}|X - c| is convex with derivative \varphi'(c) = \mathbb{P}(X < c) - \mathbb{P}(X > c). It vanishes exactly where half of the mass lies on each side. ∎

In Example 3.4 any value in [0, 1] is a median at the ambiguous sample, so the absolute loss allows a sharp edge, although it does not choose where. The L1 loss is the default of many super-resolution networks for this reason.

Blau and Michaeli (2018) proved a perception–distortion trade-off: at a given level of realism, measured as a divergence between the distribution of outputs and that of real signals, the lowest achievable distortion increases as realism is pushed further. Adversarial networks such as SRGAN (Ledig et al., 2017) gain realism by giving up PSNR.

In geoscience, realism without fidelity is dangerous: a sharp but invented fracture is worse than a blurred real one. Two safeguards follow. First, check data consistency: the residual \|A\hat x - y\| should match the noise level. Second, prefer methods that report uncertainty, such as posterior sampling (ch. 10).

3.4 Deep super-resolution networks

  • SRCNN (Dong et al., 2016) upsamples by bicubic interpolation, then applies three convolutional layers read as patch extraction, nonlinear mapping and reconstruction.
  • Residual learning (VDSR, Kim, Lee and Lee, 2016) predicts the difference between the fine signal and the interpolated input, which is mostly high-frequency and easier to learn with a deep network.
  • Sub-pixel convolution (Shi et al., 2016) computes in the coarse grid and rearranges s^2 channels into an image s times larger, which is cheaper.

The training pairs are the weak point. Real high-resolution geology is rarely observed, so pairs are usually synthesised by applying a model of the tool response to high-resolution data. The network then learns that model, not the real tool, and the gap between them is a distribution shift (ch. 9.2). When two tools of different resolution log the same interval, their depth-aligned data (ch. 5) give real pairs.

Example 3.6 — Four-fold super-resolution of a blocky log Code 3.7 degrades piecewise-constant logs by an 8-sample box blur, decimation by 4 and noise of standard deviation 0.05, then compares three estimators on a test log (root mean squared error, RMSE).

  • Linear interpolation: 0.408.
  • Tikhonov with a first-difference penalty: 0.349.
  • A three-layer 1D CNN trained with the absolute loss on 32 synthetic logs: 0.323.

The gains are modest, as Proposition 3.2 predicts: three quarters of the fine signal lies in directions the data do not see, and only the learned prior of blocky layers recovers part of it, near bed boundaries.

Code 3.7 — super-resolution of a synthetic log (about 10 s on a laptop CPU).

import numpy as np, torch
from scipy.sparse import diags, csr_matrix
from scipy.sparse.linalg import spsolve

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
N, s, width, sigma = 512, 4, 8, 0.05      # fine samples, factor, tool width, noise

def blocky_log():                          # piecewise-constant "true" log
    edges = np.cumsum(rng.geometric(1 / 12, size=N))
    vals = rng.standard_normal(len(edges) + 1)
    return vals[np.searchsorted(edges, np.arange(N), side="right")]

A = np.zeros((N // s, N))                  # decimation o box blur (Def. 3.1)
for i in range(N // s):
    c = i * s + s // 2
    A[i, max(0, c - width // 2):c + width // 2] = 1 / width
def measure(x):
    return A @ x + sigma * rng.standard_normal(N // s)

train = [blocky_log() for _ in range(32)]
x_true = blocky_log()
y = measure(x_true)
rmse = lambda u: np.sqrt(np.mean((u - x_true) ** 2))

# (a) linear interpolation of the coarse samples
centres = np.arange(N // s) * s + s // 2
print("linear interpolation:", round(rmse(np.interp(np.arange(N), centres, y)), 3))

# (b) Tikhonov with a first-difference penalty (closed form, see ch. 8)
As = csr_matrix(A)
D = diags([-1.0, 1.0], [0, 1], shape=(N - 1, N))
x_tik = spsolve((As.T @ As + 0.02 * D.T @ D).tocsc(), As.T @ y)
print("Tikhonov            :", round(rmse(x_tik), 3))

# (c) a small 1D CNN trained on synthetic pairs (x, A x + noise)
net = torch.nn.Sequential(
    torch.nn.Upsample(scale_factor=s, mode="linear"),
    torch.nn.Conv1d(1, 16, 9, padding=4), torch.nn.ReLU(),
    torch.nn.Conv1d(16, 16, 9, padding=4), torch.nn.ReLU(),
    torch.nn.Conv1d(16, 1, 9, padding=4))
X = torch.tensor(np.stack(train), dtype=torch.float32)[:, None]
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for it in range(400):
    Y = torch.tensor(np.stack([measure(x) for x in train]), dtype=torch.float32)[:, None]
    loss = torch.mean(torch.abs(net(Y) - X))          # L1 loss (Prop. 3.5)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    x_cnn = net(torch.tensor(y, dtype=torch.float32)[None, None])[0, 0].numpy()
print("1D CNN (L1 loss)    :", round(rmse(x_cnn), 3))

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Super-resolution inverts blur plus decimation; the kernel of the operator has dimension at least N(1 - 1/s).
  • Only a prior selects a solution: smoothness, sparsity, or a prior learned from examples.
  • Squared error yields the posterior mean, hence blur; absolute error yields the median and allows sharp edges.
  • Realism and fidelity trade off; in geoscience, check data consistency before trusting a sharp output.
  • Deep networks (SRCNN, residual, sub-pixel) are only as good as the tool model that generated their training pairs.

4. Quality measures and ranking

Every later decision rests on comparisons: is this reconstruction better than that one, is this interval of log reliable, which of ten models should be deployed? This chapter defines measures of quality, with and without a reference, and the tools that turn comparisons into rankings.

4.1 Full-reference measures

MSE and PSNR (Definition 2.1) compare a reconstruction with a reference pixel by pixel. They ignore where the error is and what it looks like, which motivated the structural similarity index.

Definition 4.1 — Structural similarity (SSIM) On a window of two images a and b, with local means \mu_a, \mu_b, variances \sigma_a^2, \sigma_b^2 and covariance \sigma_{ab}: \mathrm{SSIM}(a, b) = \frac{2\mu_a\mu_b + C_1}{\mu_a^2 + \mu_b^2 + C_1} \cdot \frac{2\sigma_{ab} + C_2}{\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2 + C_2}, with small constants C_1, C_2 > 0. The first factor compares luminance, the second contrast and structure; the index of an image is the average over sliding windows (Wang et al., 2004).

Proposition 4.2 — SSIM is at most 1 On every window, for non-negative images, \mathrm{SSIM}(a, b) \le 1, with equality if and only if a = b on the window.

Proof.

  1. 2\mu_a\mu_b \le \mu_a^2 + \mu_b^2, so the first factor lies in (0, 1], with equality if and only if \mu_a = \mu_b.
  2. By Cauchy–Schwarz, \sigma_{ab} \le \sigma_a \sigma_b, and 2\sigma_a\sigma_b \le \sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2. The second factor is therefore at most 1, with equality if and only if \sigma_a = \sigma_b and the correlation is 1.
  3. The product is at most 1, with equality only when both factors equal 1: equal means, equal variances and correlation 1, that is a - \mu_a = b - \mu_b and thus a = b. ∎

Example 4.3 — Same PSNR, different damage Code 4.8 degrades a striped test image in three ways, each scaled to an MSE of 0.01 (PSNR = 20 dB).

Degradation PSNR SSIM
uniform brightness offset 20.00 dB 0.964
white noise 20.00 dB 0.900
Gaussian blur 20.00 dB 0.904

The best quality measure is the one tied to the decision. For a super-resolved image, it may be the error on dips picked from it (Theorem 1.5); for an enhanced log, the facies accuracy downstream (ch. 7); for an inversion, the error on impedance at a blind well (ch. 8). Optimising a proxy too hard can degrade the task it stands for.

4.2 Quality without a reference

In the field there is no ground truth, yet each log and image must be checked before use. Quality control relies on indicators computed from the data alone.

Proposition 4.4 — Robust estimation of the noise level Let d_1, \dots, d_m be fine-scale coefficients that are pure Gaussian noise \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), except for a small fraction affected by the signal. The estimator \hat \sigma = \frac{\operatorname{med}(|d_1|, \dots, |d_m|)}{0.6745} is consistent when that fraction tends to zero (Donoho and Johnstone, 1994).

Proof. For d \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), \mathbb{P}(|d| \le c) = 2\Phi(c/\sigma) - 1, which equals \frac12 for c = \sigma\, \Phi^{-1}(3/4) = 0.6745\, \sigma. The empirical median converges to that population median, and a vanishing fraction of outliers moves the median by a vanishing amount. ∎

For a log, the fine-scale coefficients can simply be first differences divided by \sqrt 2. Other no-reference indicators include the coverage fraction of an image, the fraction of saturated values, a sharpness score such as gradient energy, the agreement between redundant sensors, and physical plausibility (a density outside the range of minerals flags a bad interval).

Experts can also grade quality directly. Absolute grades are noisy and drift between experts, whereas a preference between two candidates is easier to give and more consistent. This turns quality assessment into a ranking problem.

4.3 Ranking

Definition 4.5 — Ranking metrics For two rankings of n items, a pair is concordant when both rankings order it the same way. Kendall’s tau is \tau = \frac{\#\text{concordant} - \#\text{discordant}}{\binom{n}{2}} \in [-1, 1]. For a ranked list with graded relevances \mathrm{rel}_i, the discounted cumulative gain is \mathrm{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\mathrm{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)}, and NDCG divides it by the DCG of the ideal order (Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002).

Example 4.6 — Does the metric agree with the experts? Six reconstruction methods have PSNR 31.2, 29.8, 30.5, 27.9, 28.4 and 32.0 dB; experts rank them 2, 4, 1, 6, 5, 3.

  • The metric puts method 6 first, then 1 and 3; the experts put 3, then 1 and 6.
  • The pairs (6, 1), (6, 3) and (1, 3) are discordant; the other 12 of the 15 pairs agree.
  • Hence \tau = (12 - 3)/15 = 0.6.

For a search engine that returns intervals with relevances (3, 2, 3, 0, 1), \mathrm{DCG}@5 = 12.78 and the ideal order (3, 3, 2, 1, 0) gives 13.35, so NDCG = 0.957.

Theorem 4.7 — Bradley–Terry scores are a convex problem Model the probability that item i is preferred to item j as \mathbb{P}(i \succ j) = \sigma(s_i - s_j), \qquad \sigma(u) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-u}} (Bradley and Terry, 1952). The negative log-likelihood of observed preferences is convex in s \in \mathbb{R}^n and invariant under s \mapsto s + c\mathbf{1}. If the graph of compared pairs is connected, it is strictly convex on the hyperplane s_1 = 0.

Proof.

  1. Each observation “i beats j” contributes \ell(s_i - s_j) with \ell(u) = -\log \sigma(u). Since \ell''(u) = \sigma(u)(1 - \sigma(u)) > 0, each term is a convex function of a linear form, and so is the sum.
  2. The Hessian is a weighted graph Laplacian on the compared pairs: \nabla^2 = \sum_{\text{obs}} \ell''(s_i - s_j)\,(e_i - e_j)(e_i - e_j)^\top .
  3. Its kernel is spanned by the constant vectors exactly when the comparison graph is connected, so it is positive definite on \{s_1 = 0\}. ∎

Strict convexity gives uniqueness, but a minimiser exists only if no group of items wins all its comparisons against the rest; otherwise their scores run to infinity (Ford, 1957).

RankNet (Burges et al., 2005) replaces free scores by a network s_i = f_\theta(x_i) computed from the image or log x_i, trained with the same pairwise loss. The network then generalises: it scores new data with the preferences learned from experts.

Code 4.8 — SSIM, Kendall’s tau and Bradley–Terry scores.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import uniform_filter, gaussian_filter
from scipy.stats import kendalltau
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
j = np.arange(128)
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * (j[:, None] + 8 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * j[None, :] / 128)) / 16)
x = (x + 1) / 2                                    # reference image in [0, 1]

def ssim(a, b, L=1.0, win=7):                      # Definition 4.1, local windows
    C1, C2 = (0.01 * L) ** 2, (0.03 * L) ** 2
    mu_a, mu_b = uniform_filter(a, win), uniform_filter(b, win)
    va = uniform_filter(a * a, win) - mu_a ** 2
    vb = uniform_filter(b * b, win) - mu_b ** 2
    cov = uniform_filter(a * b, win) - mu_a * mu_b
    m = ((2 * mu_a * mu_b + C1) * (2 * cov + C2)) / ((mu_a**2 + mu_b**2 + C1) * (va + vb + C2))
    return m.mean()

psnr = lambda a, b: 10 * np.log10(1 / np.mean((a - b) ** 2))
target_mse = 0.01                                  # three degradations, same MSE
shift = x + np.sqrt(target_mse)                    # brightness offset
noise = rng.standard_normal(x.shape)
noisy = x + noise * np.sqrt(target_mse / np.mean(noise ** 2))
blur = gaussian_filter(x, 1.0)
blur = x + (blur - x) * np.sqrt(target_mse / np.mean((blur - x) ** 2))
for name, im in [("offset", shift), ("noise", noisy), ("blur", blur)]:
    print(f"{name:6s}  PSNR = {psnr(x, im):5.2f} dB   SSIM = {ssim(x, im):.3f}")

# Kendall tau between a metric ranking and an expert ranking of 6 methods
metric_scores = [31.2, 29.8, 30.5, 27.9, 28.4, 32.0]
expert_rank = [2, 4, 1, 6, 5, 3]                   # 1 = best
tau, _ = kendalltau(metric_scores, [-r for r in expert_rank])
print(f"Kendall tau(metric, expert) = {tau:.3f}")

# Bradley-Terry scores from pairwise expert votes (Theorem 4.7)
true_s = np.array([0.0, 0.8, 1.5, -0.5])
pairs, wins = [], []
for _ in range(400):
    i, k = rng.choice(4, 2, replace=False)
    d = np.zeros(4); d[i], d[k] = 1, -1
    pairs.append(d)
    wins.append(rng.random() < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(true_s[i] - true_s[k]))))
bt = LogisticRegression(fit_intercept=False, C=10.0).fit(np.array(pairs), wins)
s_hat = bt.coef_[0] - bt.coef_[0][0]               # fix the gauge s_0 = 0
print("Bradley-Terry scores:", s_hat.round(2), " true:", true_s)

With 400 simulated votes on four items, the fitted scores are (0, 0.78, 1.56, −0.37) against (0, 0.8, 1.5, −0.5) in truth: logistic regression on difference vectors is exactly Bradley–Terry maximum likelihood, with a mild \ell_2 penalty.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • PSNR measures energy of the error; SSIM compares local means, contrasts and correlations, and equals 1 only for identical images.
  • The right quality measure is the one tied to the decision the data will support.
  • Without a reference, robust statistics (the median absolute deviation) and physical checks estimate quality.
  • Kendall’s tau compares rankings, NDCG scores ranked lists with graded relevance.
  • Pairwise preferences give scores through the convex Bradley–Terry likelihood; RankNet learns them from the data.

5. Data alignment

Two logging runs in the same well rarely agree in depth: the cable stretches, the tool sticks and slips, and depth references differ. Cores carry their own depths, and seismic lives in time rather than depth. Alignment comes before any learning on several sources, because a misaligned label is a wrong label.

5.1 Constant shifts: cross-correlation and phase correlation

Proposition 5.1 — Least squares and correlation select the same shift Let S_\tau be the circular shift by \tau samples. For signals x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n, \arg\min_\tau \|x - S_\tau y\|^2 = \arg\max_\tau \langle x, S_\tau y \rangle .

Proof. Expand the square: \|x - S_\tau y\|^2 = \|x\|^2 + \|S_\tau y\|^2 - 2\langle x, S_\tau y\rangle . A shift permutes the samples, so \|S_\tau y\| = \|y\|, and only the last term depends on \tau. ∎

All n correlations are computed at once with the FFT, in \mathcal{O}(n \log n), since a correlation is a convolution with a reversed signal (Proposition 0.4). For non-circular logs, the overlap changes with \tau, so the correlation is computed on a common interior window or normalised.

Proposition 5.2 — Phase correlation If y = S_\tau x, then \hat y_\ell = e^{-2i\pi \ell\tau/n}\, \hat x_\ell. Wherever \hat x_\ell \neq 0, the normalised cross-power spectrum is a pure phase, and its inverse DFT is a spike at \tau: \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[\frac{\hat y_\ell\, \overline{\hat x_\ell}}{|\hat y_\ell\, \overline{\hat x_\ell}|}\right] = \delta_\tau .

Proof. The shift theorem follows from Definition 0.3 by the change of index k \mapsto k - \tau. The ratio then equals e^{-2i\pi\ell\tau/n} for every \ell, and the inverse DFT of this sequence is the Kronecker delta at \tau. ∎

Phase correlation ignores gains and slow trends, extends to 2D translations of images, and handles rotation and scaling after a log-polar resampling. On a borehole image, a rotation of the tool around its axis is a circular shift in azimuth, so it is estimated this way.

5.2 Non-linear alignment: dynamic time warping

When the shift varies with depth, a single lag is not enough.

Definition 5.3 — Warping path For two sequences a = (a_1, \dots, a_n) and b = (b_1, \dots, b_m), a warping path is a sequence of index pairs (i_k, j_k).

It starts at (1, 1), ends at (n, m), and at each step advances in one sequence or in both.

Its cost, for a local distance d such as the squared difference, is \sum_k d(a_{i_k}, b_{j_k}).

Theorem 5.4 — Dynamic programming computes the optimal warping Set C(0, 0) = 0 and C(i, 0) = C(0, j) = +\infty otherwise, and C(i, j) = d(a_i, b_j) + \min\{C(i-1, j-1),\; C(i-1, j),\; C(i, j-1)\}. Then C(n, m) is the minimal cost over all warping paths, computed in \mathcal{O}(nm) operations; a band |i - j| \le w reduces this to \mathcal{O}(nw) (Sakoe and Chiba, 1978).

Proof. By induction on i + j, C(i, j) is the minimal cost of a path from (1, 1) to (i, j). An optimal path to (i, j) arrives from one of its three predecessors. Its prefix is then a path to that predecessor, and it must be optimal: otherwise replacing it by a cheaper one would lower the total. The minimum over the three predecessors is therefore the optimal cost. Backtracking the minimising choices recovers the path. ∎

Example 5.5 — A warping by hand Align a = (0, 2, 1) with b = (0, 1, 2, 1) using the squared difference. The table C(i, j) is \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 5 & 6 \\ 4 & 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 5 & 1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, so the optimal cost is 1. One optimal path matches 0 \leftrightarrow 0, 0 \leftrightarrow 1, 2 \leftrightarrow 2, 1 \leftrightarrow 1. The value 1 in b has no counterpart in a, so one unit of cost is unavoidable.

The constraints carry geology. Monotone steps preserve the stratigraphic order (layers cannot swap), the band encodes the largest plausible depth error, and slope limits forbid absurd stretching. Soft-DTW (Cuturi and Blondel, 2017) replaces the minimum by a smooth minimum, which makes the alignment cost differentiable and usable as a training loss.

5.3 Rigid registration

Theorem 5.6 — Orthogonal Procrustes (Kabsch) Let p_i, q_i \in \mathbb{R}^d be paired points with zero centroids, and let H = \sum_i p_i q_i^\top = U \Sigma V^\top (SVD). Over rotations R \in SO(d), the sum \sum_i \|R p_i - q_i\|^2 is minimised by R^\star = V \operatorname{diag}(1, \dots, 1, \det(V U^\top))\, U^\top .

Proof.

  1. Expanding, and using \sum_i q_i^\top R p_i = \operatorname{tr}(R H): \sum_i \|Rp_i - q_i\|^2 = \sum_i (\|p_i\|^2 + \|q_i\|^2) - 2\operatorname{tr}(R H).
  2. With M = V^\top R U, which is orthogonal, so that M_{kk} \le 1: \operatorname{tr}(RH) = \operatorname{tr}(V^\top R U \Sigma) = \sum_k M_{kk}\sigma_k \le \sum_k \sigma_k .
  3. Equality holds for M = I, that is R = VU^\top, which is a rotation when \det(VU^\top) = 1.
  4. Otherwise \det M = -1 is imposed, and the best choice flips the sign attached to the smallest \sigma_d, which gives the stated formula (Kabsch, 1976). ∎

With non-centred data, first translate both sets to their centroids; the optimal translation then maps one centroid onto the other.

Rigid registration aligns repeated passes of a tool, matches 3D surveys, and registers images from different tools once a few common features are identified. Learned registration replaces hand-picked features by networks: spatial transformer networks (Jaderberg et al., 2015) predict a transformation inside a network, and VoxelMorph (Balakrishnan et al., 2019) learns deformable registration fields from pairs of images.

5.4 The well-to-seismic tie

The tie aligns a well, measured in depth, with seismic, measured in time. It chains the tools of ch. 1 and of this chapter.

  1. Compute the impedance log Z = \rho v from the density and sonic logs.
  2. Convert depth to two-way time with the sonic velocities, t(z) = 2\int_0^z dz'/v(z'), corrected by check-shot measurements when they exist.
  3. Compute the reflectivity (Definition 1.7) and convolve it with a wavelet to get a synthetic trace.
  4. Match the synthetic and the real trace: a bulk shift by cross-correlation (5.1), then a gentle stretch-and-squeeze with a narrow-band DTW (5.2), while estimating the wavelet.

The tie produces the time–depth relation that transfers well labels (facies, impedance) onto the seismic, and the low-frequency model that inversion needs (ch. 8.4).

Example 5.7 — Depth matching two logging runs Code 5.8 simulates two gamma-ray runs whose depth offset varies smoothly from 5 to 20 samples.

  • The best constant lag, found by cross-correlation, is 16 samples; it leaves a mean depth error of 2.43 samples in the interior.
  • Banded DTW follows the varying offset and reduces the mean error to 0.76 samples.

Code 5.8 — constant shift versus dynamic time warping.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter1d

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 400
gr = gaussian_filter1d(rng.standard_normal(n + 80), 3)       # "true" gamma-ray log
ref = gr[40:40 + n] + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal(n)          # reference run

# second run: depth shift of 5 samples plus a slow stretch (cable effects)
u = np.arange(n)
warp = u + 5 + 15 * np.sin(np.pi * u / n)                     # true depth map
run2 = np.interp(warp + 40, np.arange(n + 80), gr) + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal(n)

# (1) constant shift by cross-correlation (Proposition 5.1)
lags = np.arange(-30, 31)
cc = [np.dot(ref[40:-40], run2[40 + l:n - 40 + l]) for l in lags]
best = lags[int(np.argmax(cc))]
print("cross-correlation lag:", best, "samples")

# (2) dynamic time warping with a Sakoe-Chiba band (Theorem 5.4)
def dtw(a, b, band=25):
    na, nb = len(a), len(b)
    C = np.full((na + 1, nb + 1), np.inf)
    C[0, 0] = 0.0
    for i in range(1, na + 1):
        for j in range(max(1, i - band), min(nb, i + band) + 1):
            d = (a[i - 1] - b[j - 1]) ** 2
            C[i, j] = d + min(C[i - 1, j - 1], C[i - 1, j], C[i, j - 1])
    i, j, path = na, nb, []                                  # backtracking
    while i > 0 and j > 0:
        path.append((i - 1, j - 1))
        k = np.argmin([C[i - 1, j - 1], C[i - 1, j], C[i, j - 1]])
        i, j = (i - 1, j - 1) if k == 0 else ((i - 1, j) if k == 1 else (i, j - 1))
    return C[na, nb], path[::-1]

cost, path = dtw(ref, run2)
# ref sample i matches run2 sample j  <=>  i = warp[j]; compare in the interior
est = {}
for i, j in path:
    est.setdefault(j, []).append(i)
js = np.array([j for j in est if 60 < j < n - 60])
err_dtw = np.mean([abs(np.mean(est[j]) - warp[j]) for j in js])
err_cc = np.mean(np.abs((js - best) - warp[js]))   # run2[j] ~ ref[j - lag]
print(f"mean depth error: constant shift {err_cc:.2f}, DTW {err_dtw:.2f} samples")

The printed lag is −16 in the code’s convention (sample j of the second run matches sample j + 16 of the reference).

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • For a constant shift, least squares and cross-correlation agree, and the FFT computes all lags at once.
  • Phase correlation turns a translation into a spike and ignores gains and trends.
  • Dynamic time warping finds the optimal monotone alignment by dynamic programming; bands and slopes encode geological limits.
  • The best rotation between paired point sets comes from an SVD (Procrustes, Kabsch).
  • The well-to-seismic tie chains impedance, time conversion, synthetic traces and alignment.

6. Contrast enhancement and inpainting

Raw images are often hard to read: a few extreme values crush the contrast, and parts of the wall are not measured at all. This chapter treats both problems: remapping values so that structure becomes visible, and filling gaps with values consistent with their surroundings.

6.1 Contrast enhancement

Definition 6.1 — Point transform A point transform replaces every value u of an image by T(u) for a non-decreasing function T. Examples are windowing (clip to [u_{\min}, u_{\max}] and rescale), the logarithm (natural for resistivity, ch. 1.2) and the gamma correction T(u) = u^\gamma on [0, 1].

Theorem 6.2 — Histogram equalisation Let U be a real random variable with a continuous distribution function F. Then F(U) \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 1).

Proof. Let F^{-}(t) = \inf\{u : F(u) \ge t\} for t \in (0, 1). By continuity of F, F(F^{-}(t)) = t.

Moreover F(U) \le t holds exactly when U \le F^{-}(t), up to an event of probability zero. Hence \mathbb{P}(F(U) \le t) = F(F^{-}(t)) = t. \qquad ∎

On an image, F is the empirical distribution function, and the equalised value of a pixel is its rank divided by the number of pixels.

Borehole images are distributed in two renderings. Static normalisation uses one mapping for the whole well, which keeps values comparable between depths and shows large-scale changes. Dynamic normalisation recomputes the mapping in a sliding depth window, which reveals fractures and fine bedding everywhere but destroys absolute levels. Interpreters need both, for different questions.

Proposition 6.3 — Equalisation amplifies where the histogram peaks If U has density p, the equalising map T = F has slope T'(u) = p(u). A small perturbation \delta of a value u becomes, after equalisation, T(u + \delta) - T(u) \approx p(u)\, \delta . Clipping the density at a level c before integrating bounds the slope, hence the amplification, by c.

Proof. F is differentiable with F' = p, and the first-order Taylor expansion gives the approximation. If the clipped density \min(p, c) is renormalised by its mass \kappa \le 1, the new map has slope at most c/\kappa, which is bounded. ∎

Flat, noisy regions have a peaked histogram, so plain equalisation turns their noise into strong texture. CLAHE (Zuiderveld, 1994) clips the histogram, redistributes the excess, computes the map in tiles, and blends neighbouring tiles bilinearly. Learned enhancement networks can mimic the renderings interpreters prefer, at the risk of learning the taste of a single expert (ch. 9.1).

6.2 Inpainting

Definition 6.4 — Inpainting An image f is known on a domain \Omega except on a set D (the mask). Inpainting computes values on D that are consistent with f on \Omega \setminus D. Typical masks are the vertical gaps between the pads of a resistivity imager, dead seismic traces, and log intervals lost in washed-out borehole sections.

Theorem 6.5 — Harmonic inpainting On a pixel grid, let E(u) = \sum_{p \sim q} (u_p - u_q)^2 over pairs of neighbouring pixels, with u = f fixed outside D. If every connected component of D has a neighbour outside D, then E has a unique minimiser. In it, every unknown pixel is the average of its neighbours (a discrete harmonic function), and its values stay between the minimum and the maximum of the known values.

Proof.

  1. E is a quadratic function of the unknowns u_D. Setting its partial derivative at p \in D to zero gives u_p = \frac{1}{\deg p}\sum_{q \sim p} u_q.
  2. The quadratic part is u_D^\top L_{DD}\, u_D, where L_{DD} is the graph Laplacian restricted to D. It equals the sum of (u_p - u_q)^2 over edges inside the mask, plus u_p^2 over edges leaving it.
  3. If this sum vanishes, u is constant on each component of D and zero next to its boundary, hence zero. So L_{DD} is positive definite and the minimiser is unique.
  4. At a maximum of u inside D, the average of the neighbours equals the maximum, so all neighbours share it. Walking to the boundary shows that it is reached on known pixels; the minimum is similar. ∎

In the continuous limit, the minimiser of \int_D |\nabla u|^2 with boundary data f solves the Laplace equation \Delta u = 0 in D.

Example 6.6 — Filling pad gaps Code 6.7 builds an equalised image of dipping beds and removes 25 % of the azimuths in four vertical gaps, as a four-pad tool would.

  • Filling each gap with the mean of the known pixels leaves an RMSE of 0.290 in the gaps.
  • Harmonic inpainting, periodic in azimuth, reduces it to 0.139.
  • The equalised image has exactly flat histogram bins (2457 or 2458 pixels in each fifth).

Harmonic filling is smooth, so it cannot continue thin sinusoids across wide gaps: it averages them away. Other models keep more structure:

  • Total variation inpainting (Shen and Chan, 2002) minimises \int_D |\nabla u| and preserves edges, although it joins them by straight segments.
  • Exemplar-based methods (Criminisi, Pérez and Toyama, 2004) copy patches from the known region along the isophotes.
  • Multiple-point statistics fill borehole images with patterns learned from the measured part (Hurley and Zhang, 2011).
  • Geometric models fit the sinusoids of Theorem 1.5 on the visible pieces and extend them across the gaps: physics as a prior.

Deep inpainting has two families. Partial convolutions (Liu et al., 2018) renormalise each convolution over the valid pixels of its window and update the mask from layer to layer; they are trained on large image collections with random masks. Deep Image Prior (Ulyanov, Vedaldi and Lempitsky, 2018) needs no training data. It fits a randomly initialised network g_\theta(z) to the known pixels only, \min_\theta \|M \odot (g_\theta(z) - f)\|^2 , where M is the indicator of known pixels, and stops early. The architecture itself acts as the prior.

To measure inpainting error honestly, hide known pixels with an artificial mask shaped like the real gaps and compare. No other measurement is possible, since the true values in the gaps are never observed.

Code 6.7 — histogram equalisation and harmonic inpainting.

import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import lil_matrix
from scipy.sparse.linalg import spsolve

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
H, W = 96, 128
z, j = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W].astype(float)
phi = 2 * np.pi * j / W
img = np.sin(2 * np.pi * (z + 10 * np.cos(phi - 1.0)) / 20)       # dipping beds
img = np.exp(1.5 * img) + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal((H, W))     # skewed values

# histogram equalisation (Theorem 6.2): map each value through the empirical CDF
ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(img.ravel()))
eq = ((ranks + 0.5) / img.size).reshape(H, W)
print("histogram of equalised image (5 bins):", np.histogram(eq, 5, (0, 1))[0])

# pad tool: 4 pads, each covering 75% of its quarter of the circumference
mask = (j % 32) < 24                                              # True = measured
known = np.where(mask, eq, 0.0)

# harmonic inpainting (Theorem 6.5): discrete Laplace equation in the gaps,
# periodic in azimuth, known pixels as Dirichlet data
idx = -np.ones((H, W), dtype=int)
idx[~mask] = np.arange((~mask).sum())
M = lil_matrix(((~mask).sum(),) * 2)
b = np.zeros((~mask).sum())
for (r, c) in zip(*np.nonzero(~mask)):
    k = idx[r, c]
    nbrs = [(r, (c - 1) % W), (r, (c + 1) % W)] + [(rr, c) for rr in (r - 1, r + 1) if 0 <= rr < H]
    M[k, k] = len(nbrs)
    for rr, cc in nbrs:
        if mask[rr, cc]:
            b[k] += known[rr, cc]
        else:
            M[k, idx[rr, cc]] -= 1
filled = known.copy()
filled[~mask] = spsolve(M.tocsr(), b)
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((filled[~mask] - eq[~mask]) ** 2))
print(f"gap fraction {1 - mask.mean():.0%}, harmonic RMSE in the gaps = {rmse:.3f}")
print(f"RMSE of a constant fill with the mean = {np.sqrt(np.mean((eq[~mask] - eq[mask].mean()) ** 2)):.3f}")

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Point transforms remap values; equalisation through the distribution function makes the histogram uniform.
  • Static normalisation keeps values comparable along the well, dynamic normalisation reveals local detail.
  • Equalisation amplifies noise where the histogram peaks; CLAHE bounds that gain by clipping.
  • Harmonic inpainting solves a discrete Laplace equation, with a unique solution and no overshoot.
  • Edge-preserving, exemplar, geometric and deep methods keep more structure; measure them with artificial masks.

7. Classification and segmentation

Interpretation assigns classes: a facies to each depth of a well, a label to each pixel of an image. This chapter covers both tasks, with one theme throughout: the validation must imitate the deployment, or the scores will lie.

7.1 Facies classification from logs

A facies is a body of rock with distinctive characteristics (lithology, texture, depositional environment). The task is to predict a facies y_k \in \{1, \dots, K\} at each depth from the logs x(z_k), using a few wells where geologists have labelled facies from cores and logs.

  • Features: the logs, their depth derivatives, and windows of neighbouring samples that give context.
  • Models, in order of complexity: logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting such as XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016), and 1D convolutional or recurrent networks along depth.
  • Public benchmark: the tutorial and contest of Hall (2016) released labelled wells for this task.

Definition 7.1 — The risk that matters Let wells be drawn from a population, W \sim P_W, and let samples within a well follow P_{\cdot \mid W}. The risk of a classifier f on a new well is R_{\mathrm{new}}(f) = \mathbb{E}_{W}\, \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim P_{\cdot \mid W}}\, \mathbb{1}[f(x) \ne y].

Random K-fold cross-validation draws test samples from wells that also supply training samples, often from the next depth. Layers are thicker than the sampling interval and the tool smooths the logs (Proposition 1.3), so a test sample usually has a near copy in the training set. The estimate then targets accuracy at known wells, not R_{\mathrm{new}}. Group (well-wise) cross-validation holds out whole wells and estimates R_{\mathrm{new}}.

Example 7.2 — The gap, measured Code 7.3 simulates 8 wells with four facies, sticky layering, a well-specific offset on each log (calibration and regional trends) and tool smoothing. A random forest scores:

  • 0.969 under random 4-fold cross-validation;
  • 0.485 under well-wise 4-fold cross-validation;
  • 0.966 well-wise once each log is standardised within each well.

The last line is a remedy, not a free lunch: per-well standardisation assumes that each well contains similar proportions of facies, which fails for a well drilled mostly in one facies (Exercise 12).

Code 7.3 — random versus well-wise cross-validation (about 10 s).

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter1d
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, GroupKFold, cross_val_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
means = np.array([[0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0],          # 4 facies x 4 logs
                  [1.0, 0.6, -0.4, 0.2],
                  [1.6, -0.3, 0.6, -0.5],
                  [0.4, 1.2, 0.9, 0.8]])
T = np.full((4, 4), 0.01) + np.eye(4) * 0.96      # sticky Markov chain in depth

X, y, wells = [], [], []
for w in range(8):
    f = [rng.integers(4)]
    for _ in range(599):
        f.append(rng.choice(4, p=T[f[-1]] / T[f[-1]].sum()))
    f = np.array(f)
    offset = 0.7 * rng.standard_normal(4)          # well-specific calibration/trend
    logs = means[f] + offset + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((600, 4))
    logs = gaussian_filter1d(logs, 2, axis=0)      # tool vertical response
    X.append(logs); y.append(f); wells.append(np.full(600, w))
X, y, wells = np.vstack(X), np.concatenate(y), np.concatenate(wells)

clf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, random_state=0, n_jobs=1)
rand = cross_val_score(clf, X, y, cv=KFold(4, shuffle=True, random_state=0))
lowo = cross_val_score(clf, X, y, cv=GroupKFold(4), groups=wells)
print(f"random 4-fold accuracy      : {rand.mean():.3f}")
print(f"well-wise 4-fold accuracy   : {lowo.mean():.3f}")

# per-well standardisation, a simple remedy for well-to-well shift (ch. 9.2)
Xn = X.copy()
for w in range(8):
    Xn[wells == w] = (X[wells == w] - X[wells == w].mean(0)) / X[wells == w].std(0)
lowo_n = cross_val_score(clf, Xn, y, cv=GroupKFold(4), groups=wells)
print(f"well-wise, per-well scaling : {lowo_n.mean():.3f}")

Proposition 7.4 — Correcting for a change of class proportions Suppose p(x \mid y) is the same in training and deployment, while the class proportions change from \pi(y) to \pi'(y). Then the deployment posterior is p'(y \mid x) = \frac{p(y \mid x)\, \pi'(y)/\pi(y)}{\sum_{k} p(k \mid x)\, \pi'(k)/\pi(k)} .

Proof. By Bayes’ rule, p'(y \mid x) \propto p(x \mid y)\, \pi'(y) and p(y \mid x) \propto p(x \mid y)\, \pi(y), with normalisations that do not depend on y. Dividing, p'(y \mid x) \propto p(y \mid x)\, \pi'(y)/\pi(y), and normalising over y gives the formula. ∎

A classifier trained on balanced classes can thus be corrected to the proportions expected in a new field; when these are unknown, they can be estimated by an EM procedure (Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker, 2002).

Facies follow each other in a geological order: Walther’s law states that facies stacked vertically without a break were deposited side by side. A hidden Markov model with a transition matrix estimated on labelled wells smooths the sample-wise predictions along depth with the Viterbi algorithm. A useful geology-aware score is the accuracy that also accepts the neighbouring facies.

7.2 Semantic segmentation of images

Definition 7.5 — Semantic segmentation A segmentation network maps an image I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W} to class probabilities at every pixel. Fully convolutional networks (Long, Shelhamer and Darrell, 2015) contain no dense layer, so they accept images of any size. U-Net (Ronneberger, Fischer and Brox, 2015) adds a decoder that upsamples the coarse features and concatenates the encoder features of the same resolution through skip connections, which restores precise boundaries.

Proposition 7.6 — Dice and IoU rank segmentations identically For a predicted set A and a true set B of pixels, with A \cup B \ne \emptyset, \mathrm{IoU} = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}, \qquad \mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2|A \cap B|}{|A| + |B|} = \frac{2\, \mathrm{IoU}}{1 + \mathrm{IoU}} . Dice is an increasing function of IoU, and Dice ≥ IoU.

Proof. Inclusion–exclusion gives |A| + |B| = |A \cup B| + |A \cap B|. Divide numerator and denominator of Dice by |A \cup B| to obtain 2\,\mathrm{IoU}/(1 + \mathrm{IoU}). The map t \mapsto 2t/(1+t) is increasing on [0, 1] and satisfies 2t/(1+t) \ge t there. ∎

The soft Dice loss replaces the sets by predicted probabilities. It ignores the true negatives, which makes it suited to thin, rare objects: fractures cover 6 % of the pixels in code 7.8, and faults a few percent of a seismic section.

Applications follow the physics of ch. 1:

  • Seismic facies: Alaudah et al. (2019) published a benchmark with labelled facies on the public F3 block of the Dutch North Sea.
  • Faults: Wu et al. (2019) trained a 3D network on synthetic seismic volumes only (FaultSeg3D) and applied it to field data, an answer to the lack of labels (ch. 9.1).
  • Borehole images: fractures, bedding and breakouts. A classical baseline is the Hough transform in the parameter space (z_0, r\tan\theta, \varphi_d) of Theorem 1.5.
  • Corrosion: metal-loss regions on casing thickness images (ch. 1.5).

Data augmentation must respect the physics. A circular shift in azimuth (a rotated tool) or a small change of gain is legitimate. Flipping depth reverses the stratigraphic order and is legitimate only if the task is symmetric.

Example 7.7 — Segmenting fractures on synthetic borehole images Code 7.8 trains a three-layer fully convolutional network, with circular padding in azimuth, on synthetic 32 × 64 images of beds crossed by one sinusoidal fracture. The loss is binary cross-entropy plus soft Dice. After 150 steps of batches of 16 images, the network reaches IoU = 0.808 (Dice = 0.894) on 64 new images, for fractures that cover 6.2 % of the pixels.

Code 7.8 — a tiny segmentation network with a Dice loss (about 15 s).

import numpy as np, torch
import torch.nn as nn

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
H, W = 32, 64
z, j = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W].astype(float)
phi = 2 * np.pi * j / W

def sample():                       # beds + one conductive fracture (sinusoid)
    beds = np.sin(2 * np.pi * (z + rng.uniform(0, 6) * np.cos(phi - rng.uniform(0, 6))) / rng.uniform(6, 12))
    z0, a, p = rng.uniform(8, 24), rng.uniform(2, 8), rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi)
    frac = np.abs(z - z0 - a * np.cos(phi - p)) < 1.0      # Theorem 1.5
    img = 0.6 * beds + 1.0 * frac + 0.4 * rng.standard_normal((H, W))
    return img, frac

def batch(n):
    xs, ys = zip(*[sample() for _ in range(n)])
    return (torch.tensor(np.array(xs), dtype=torch.float32)[:, None],
            torch.tensor(np.array(ys), dtype=torch.float32)[:, None])

def soft_dice_loss(logits, target, eps=1.0):           # Proposition 7.6
    p = torch.sigmoid(logits)
    inter = (p * target).sum((1, 2, 3))
    return 1 - ((2 * inter + eps) / (p.sum((1, 2, 3)) + target.sum((1, 2, 3)) + eps)).mean()

net = nn.Sequential(                                   # tiny fully convolutional net
    nn.Conv2d(1, 16, 5, padding=2, padding_mode="circular"), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Conv2d(16, 16, 5, padding=2, padding_mode="circular"), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Conv2d(16, 1, 5, padding=2, padding_mode="circular"))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for it in range(150):
    x, t = batch(16)
    out = net(x)
    loss = nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(out, t) + soft_dice_loss(out, t)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

x, t = batch(64)                                       # held-out synthetic images
with torch.no_grad():
    pred = (net(x) > 0).float()
inter, union = (pred * t).sum(), ((pred + t) > 0).sum()
iou = (inter / union).item()
print(f"fracture pixels: {t.mean().item():.1%} of the image")
print(f"test IoU = {iou:.3f}, Dice = {2 * iou / (1 + iou):.3f}")

Circular padding in azimuth matters here: the unrolled image is a cylinder, so its left and right edges are neighbours. Note also that the padding wraps in depth, a harmless simplification for these small synthetic patches.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Facies classification maps logs to classes; samples of a well are strongly dependent.
  • Validate by holding out whole wells: random folds measure memory of known wells, not generalisation.
  • A change of class proportions is corrected by reweighting the posterior with the ratio of priors.
  • U-Nets segment images; Dice and IoU rank results identically, and soft Dice suits thin, rare objects.
  • Synthetic training data and physics-respecting augmentations answer the lack of labels, within limits.

8. Inversion

Inversion goes from measurements back to the property that produced them: reflectivity and impedance from seismic traces, resistivity of the formation from tool readings. Its difficulty is not computing a solution but choosing one that the data cannot contradict and that noise does not destroy. This chapter builds the classical tools and shows where learning and physics enter.

8.1 Ill-posed problems

Definition 8.1 — Well-posedness (Hadamard) The problem of recovering x from y = Ax is well-posed if a solution exists for every y, if it is unique, and if it depends continuously on y. Otherwise it is ill-posed.

In finite dimension, the third condition fails gradually rather than absolutely: the ratio \sigma_1 / \sigma_n (the condition number) measures how much noise is amplified. For deconvolution by a band-limited wavelet, the smallest singular values are numerically zero; in code 8.7 they range from 8.3 down to 3 \times 10^{-17}. Dividing by them, as A^+ does, amplifies noise without limit.

8.2 Tikhonov regularisation

Theorem 8.2 — Tikhonov in the SVD basis With A = \sum_i \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top and \lambda > 0, the regularised solution of Proposition 0.6 is

Key result — filter factors x_\lambda = \sum_i f_i\, \frac{u_i^\top y}{\sigma_i}\, v_i, \qquad f_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda} \in (0, 1).

If y = Ax^\star + \varepsilon with \mathbb{E}\varepsilon = 0 and \operatorname{Cov}\varepsilon = \sigma^2 I, the mean squared error splits into a bias and a variance: \mathbb{E}\, \|x_\lambda - x^\star\|^2 = \sum_i (1 - f_i)^2 (v_i^\top x^\star)^2 + \sigma^2 \sum_i \frac{f_i^2}{\sigma_i^2}.

Proof.

  1. In the basis (v_i), A^\top A + \lambda I has eigenvalues \sigma_i^2 + \lambda and A^\top y has coordinates \sigma_i u_i^\top y. Dividing gives the formula: v_i^\top x_\lambda = \frac{\sigma_i\, u_i^\top y}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}.
  2. Substitute u_i^\top y = \sigma_i v_i^\top x^\star + \epsilon_i, where \epsilon_i = u_i^\top \varepsilon. The error along v_i is v_i^\top (x_\lambda - x^\star) = -(1 - f_i)\, v_i^\top x^\star + f_i\, \frac{\epsilon_i}{\sigma_i}.
  3. The noise terms have zero mean and variance \sigma^2, and are uncorrelated across i. Squaring and taking expectations gives bias squared plus variance. ∎

Small \lambda keeps the bias low and lets the variance explode through the small \sigma_i; large \lambda does the opposite. Three classical rules choose \lambda:

  • the discrepancy principle picks \lambda so that \|Ax_\lambda - y\| \approx \sigma\sqrt{m}, the expected noise norm;
  • the L-curve plots \log\|x_\lambda\| against \log\|Ax_\lambda - y\| and takes the corner;
  • generalised cross-validation minimises a leave-one-out proxy that needs no knowledge of \sigma (Golub, Heath and Wahba, 1979).

The Bayesian reading of Proposition 0.6 also gives uncertainty. With the Gaussian prior and noise of that proposition, the posterior covariance is \Big(\frac{A^\top A}{\sigma^2} + \frac{I}{\tau^2}\Big)^{-1}, which is large exactly along the directions v_i where the data are weak.

8.3 Sparsity: sparse-spike deconvolution

Reflectivity is sparse: a few interfaces in a long trace. Smooth regularisers spread each spike over many samples, so seismic deconvolution uses the \ell_1 norm instead: \min_r \; F(r) = \frac{1}{2}\|y - Wr\|_2^2 + \lambda \|r\|_1 , where W is the convolution matrix of the wavelet.

Proposition 8.3 — Soft-thresholding is the proximal map of the \ell_1 norm For v \in \mathbb{R} and \lambda > 0, \arg\min_u \tfrac{1}{2}(u - v)^2 + \lambda |u| = \operatorname{sign}(v)\, \max(|v| - \lambda, 0).

Proof. The objective is strictly convex, and its minimiser u satisfies 0 \in u - v + \lambda\, \partial|u|.

  • If u > 0, then u = v - \lambda, which requires v > \lambda; symmetrically for u < 0.
  • Otherwise u = 0, which requires v \in [-\lambda, \lambda]. ∎

Theorem 8.4 — Convergence of ISTA Let L = \sigma_{\max}(W)^2. The iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm r_{k+1} = \operatorname{soft}_{\lambda/L}\!\Big(r_k + \frac{1}{L} W^\top (y - W r_k)\Big) satisfies, for every minimiser r^\star (Daubechies, Defrise and De Mol, 2004; Beck and Teboulle, 2009), F(r_k) - F(r^\star) \le \frac{L\, \|r_0 - r^\star\|^2}{2k}.

Proof. Sketch. The smooth part g(r) = \frac12\|y - Wr\|^2 has an L-Lipschitz gradient, so it lies below the quadratic g(r_k) + \langle \nabla g(r_k), r - r_k\rangle + \frac{L}{2}\|r - r_k\|^2 . Adding \lambda\|r\|_1 and minimising this upper bound gives exactly the ISTA step, by Proposition 8.3 applied coordinate by coordinate. The same telescoping argument as in Proposition 0.7 then yields the rate. ∎

The accelerated version FISTA (Beck and Teboulle, 2009) improves the rate to \mathcal{O}(1/k^2) at the same cost per iteration.

8.4 From reflectivity to impedance

Proposition 8.5 — Reflectivity is a hyperbolic tangent of the log-impedance jump With R as in Definition 1.7, R = \tanh\Big(\frac{1}{2} \ln \frac{Z_2}{Z_1}\Big), \qquad \text{hence} \qquad \ln Z_k = \ln Z_0 + 2 \sum_{j \le k} \operatorname{artanh} R_j .

Proof. Let a = \ln(Z_2 / Z_1). Dividing numerator and denominator by Z_1, \frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1} = \frac{e^a - 1}{e^a + 1} = \frac{e^{a/2} - e^{-a/2}}{e^{a/2} + e^{-a/2}} = \tanh\frac{a}{2}. Inverting and summing the jumps from the top gives the recursion. ∎

For small contrasts, R \approx \frac12 \Delta \ln Z: reflectivity is half the derivative of log-impedance.

The recursion needs the starting value Z_0. More deeply, the wavelet has no energy at 0 Hz, so the low frequencies of \ln Z (the slow trends) are not in the seismic at all. Practical impedance inversion adds them from a low-frequency model interpolated between tied wells (ch. 5.4). Data and physics are combined here by construction, not by choice.

Example 8.6 — Deconvolution and impedance on a synthetic trace Code 8.7 creates a 300-sample trace from 10 reflectors and a 25 Hz Ricker wavelet, with noise of standard deviation 0.01. The true reflectivity has norm 0.281, which is also the error of the trivial estimate r = 0.

  • Tikhonov: errors 0.489, 0.293, 0.245 and 0.256 for \lambda = 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, 0.3, 10; even the best value barely beats zero, because a smooth estimate spreads each spike.
  • ISTA with \lambda = 0.03: error 0.151, with 22 non-zero samples for 10 true spikes.
  • Proposition 8.5 applied to the ISTA output, with Z_0 known, gives the impedance within 2.7 % on average.

Code 8.7 — Tikhonov, ISTA and impedance recovery.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import toeplitz

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, dt, f = 300, 0.002, 25.0
t = np.arange(-40, 41) * dt
w = (1 - 2 * (np.pi * f * t) ** 2) * np.exp(-(np.pi * f * t) ** 2)   # Ricker
col = np.zeros(n); col[:41] = w[40:]
row = np.zeros(n); row[:41] = w[40::-1]
Wm = toeplitz(col, row)                         # convolution matrix, y = W r + noise

# sparse reflectivity from a blocky log-impedance (Proposition 8.5: R = tanh(d lnZ / 2))
lnZ = np.log(6e6) + np.cumsum(np.where(rng.random(n) < 0.04, 0.25 * rng.standard_normal(n), 0))
r = np.tanh(np.diff(lnZ, prepend=lnZ[0]) / 2)
y = Wm @ r + 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(n)

# Tikhonov through the SVD filter factors (Theorem 8.2)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Wm)
def tikhonov(lam):
    return Vt.T @ (s / (s ** 2 + lam) * (U.T @ y))
print(f"||r|| = {np.linalg.norm(r):.3f}  (error of the zero estimate)")
for lam in [1e-3, 1e-2, 0.3, 10.0]:
    print(f"Tikhonov, lambda = {lam:g}: error {np.linalg.norm(tikhonov(lam) - r):.3f}")

# ISTA for 1/2 ||y - W r||^2 + lam ||r||_1 (Theorem 8.4)
L = s[0] ** 2
lam, x = 0.03, np.zeros(n)
for k in range(2000):
    g = x + Wm.T @ (y - Wm @ x) / L
    x = np.sign(g) * np.maximum(np.abs(g) - lam / L, 0)          # soft threshold
print(f"ISTA, lambda = {lam}: error {np.linalg.norm(x - r):.3f}, "
      f"{(np.abs(r) > 1e-3).sum()} true spikes, {(np.abs(x) > 1e-3).sum()} non-zeros")

# back to impedance: ln Z_k = ln Z_0 + 2 sum artanh(r_j), needs Z_0 from a well
lnZ_hat = lnZ[0] + 2 * np.cumsum(np.arctanh(np.clip(x, -0.99, 0.99)))
print(f"mean relative impedance error: {np.abs(np.exp(lnZ_hat - lnZ) - 1).mean():.3f}")

8.5 Nonlinear and learned inversion

Many forward operators are nonlinear: induction and laterolog resistivity tools, or wave propagation in full-waveform inversion. Writing J for the Jacobian of \mathcal{M} at the current estimate, the Levenberg–Marquardt step is \delta = (J^\top J + \mu I)^{-1} J^\top \big(y - \mathcal{M}(x)\big), \qquad x \leftarrow x + \delta . It is a Tikhonov problem (Theorem 8.2) for the linearised model, with \mu adapted at each step: decreased after a successful step, increased otherwise. Each iteration needs forward simulations and a Jacobian, usually by the adjoint method, which is why inversion is expensive.

Learning enters in four ways, each with its own failure mode:

  1. Direct regression. A network f_\theta : y \mapsto x is trained on simulated pairs (x_i, \mathcal{M}(x_i) + \varepsilon_i). Inference is instantaneous, but the network knows only its training prior, and nothing guarantees that its output explains the data.
  2. Unrolled optimisation. LISTA (Gregor and LeCun, 2010) unrolls K ISTA iterations into K layers with learned matrices and thresholds, and reaches a given accuracy with far fewer iterations than ISTA.
  3. Learned priors in a variational scheme. Plug-and-play methods (Venkatakrishnan, Bouman and Wohlberg, 2013) replace the proximal step of the regulariser by a trained denoiser, while the data term keeps the physics.
  4. Physics-consistent training. A data-consistency loss \|\mathcal{M}(f_\theta(y)) - y\|^2 trains on field data without labels and penalises outputs that contradict the measurement.

The survey of Arridge et al. (2019) gives the mathematical landscape. Whatever the method, two checks remain mandatory: the data residual must sit at the noise level, and predictions must match a blind well that played no part in training or tying.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Inversion is ill-posed: small singular values amplify noise without bound.
  • Tikhonov damps each SVD component by \sigma_i^2 / (\sigma_i^2 + \lambda), trading bias for variance.
  • Sparse reflectivity calls for the \ell_1 norm; ISTA solves it by gradient steps and soft-thresholding at rate 1/k.
  • Reflectivity is \tanh of half the log-impedance jump; low frequencies must come from wells.
  • Learned inversion (regression, unrolling, plug-and-play, data consistency) must still pass the residual and blind-well checks.

9. From prototype to deployment: the industrial challenges

A method that works in a notebook on one data set can still fail on the next well, on the next tool, or on the hardware where it must run. This chapter treats the obstacles named by the course description: imperfect labels, distribution shift, robustness and interpretability, compute and memory budgets, and the path to a deployed tool.

9.1 Limited and imperfect labels

Proposition 9.1 — Symmetric label noise keeps the Bayes classifier In binary classification, let \eta(x) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid x), and suppose each observed label is flipped with probability \rho < \frac{1}{2}, independently of x. The noisy posterior is \tilde\eta(x) = (1 - 2\rho)\, \eta(x) + \rho , so \tilde\eta(x) > \frac12 if and only if \eta(x) > \frac12: the Bayes classifier is unchanged.

Proof. A label is observed as 1 if it is 1 and kept, or 0 and flipped: \mathbb{P}(\tilde Y = 1 \mid x) = (1 - \rho)\, \eta(x) + \rho\, (1 - \eta(x)) = (1 - 2\rho)\,\eta(x) + \rho . Since 1 - 2\rho > 0, \tilde\eta(x) > \frac12 is equivalent to (1 - 2\rho)\eta(x) > \frac12 - \rho, that is \eta(x) > \frac12. ∎

The good news is limited. The noisy probabilities are compressed towards \frac12, so they are miscalibrated. Noise that depends on the class or on the sample, which is typical of interpreters who disagree near boundaries, does change the classifier; it needs explicit corrections when flip rates are known (Natarajan et al., 2013).

Practical answers to scarce or imperfect labels:

  • collect labels from several interpreters and train on consensus or soft labels;
  • use metrics that tolerate small boundary shifts or neighbouring facies (ch. 7.1);
  • pre-train on large unlabelled archives with self-supervised objectives, then fine-tune on the few labels;
  • generate labelled synthetic data with forward models (ch. 1, ch. 7.2), then treat the synthetic-to-field gap as a distribution shift (9.2).

9.2 Distribution shift

Definition 9.2 — Kinds of shift Between a source distribution p_s (training) and a target p_t (deployment):

  • covariate shift: p_t(y \mid x) = p_s(y \mid x) but p_t(x) \ne p_s(x) (a new field with other rocks, the same physics);
  • label shift: p(x \mid y) is fixed but class proportions change (Proposition 7.4);
  • concept shift: p(y \mid x) itself changes, for instance with a new tool whose readings mean something else.

Proposition 9.3 — Importance weighting and the domain classifier Under covariate shift, with p_t absolutely continuous with respect to p_s and w = p_t / p_s, for every loss \ell: \mathbb{E}_{t}\, \ell(f(X), Y) = \mathbb{E}_{s}\, \big[w(X)\, \ell(f(X), Y)\big]. If c(x) is the probability that x comes from the target, for a classifier trained on n_s source and n_t target samples, then w(x) = \frac{n_s}{n_t} \cdot \frac{c(x)}{1 - c(x)} .

Proof.

  1. Since p_t(y \mid x) = p_s(y \mid x), the joint densities satisfy p_t(x, y) = w(x)\, p_s(x, y), and integrating \ell against both sides gives the identity (Shimodaira, 2000).
  2. In the pooled sample, Bayes’ rule gives c(x) = n_t p_t(x) / (n_t p_t(x) + n_s p_s(x)). Hence c/(1 - c) = n_t p_t(x) / (n_s p_s(x)), which rearranges to the formula. ∎

The price is variance. With weights w_i, the effective sample size is (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i w_i^2, and it collapses when the target lies where the source has few samples.

Example 9.4 — Weighting a misspecified model Code 9.7 fits a straight line to y = \sin x plus noise, on a source x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), for a target x \sim \mathcal{N}(1.5, 0.6^2).

  • Unweighted least squares: target MSE 0.172.
  • Weights from a logistic domain classifier: target MSE 0.055, with an effective sample size of 347 out of 2000.

Weighting helps because the model is misspecified: it then fits the line where the target lives.

Other answers to shift are standardising each well or tool (code 7.3), domain-adversarial training that makes features indistinguishable across domains (Ganin et al., 2016), and monitoring input statistics in production (9.5).

9.3 Uncertainty, robustness and interpretability

Theorem 9.5 — Split conformal prediction Let f be trained on one data set, and let S_i = |Y_i - f(X_i)| be the scores of n calibration pairs. Let \hat q be the \lceil (1 - \alpha)(n + 1) \rceil-th smallest score. If the calibration pairs and a new pair (X, Y) are exchangeable, then

Key result — distribution-free coverage \mathbb{P}\big(|Y - f(X)| \le \hat q\big) \;\ge\; 1 - \alpha .

Proof. Let S_{n+1} = |Y - f(X)|. By exchangeability, the rank of S_{n+1} among S_1, \dots, S_{n+1} is uniform on \{1, \dots, n+1\} when there are no ties (ties only help).

Let k = \lceil (1 - \alpha)(n+1) \rceil. The event S_{n+1} \le \hat q contains the event that this rank is at most k, whose probability is k / (n+1) \ge 1 - \alpha. ∎

The interval [f(x) - \hat q, f(x) + \hat q] thus covers the truth with probability at least 1 - \alpha, whatever the model and the distribution (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer, 2005; Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023).

The guarantee rests entirely on exchangeability. In code 9.7, calibrated on the source, the 90 % intervals cover 90.2 % of new source samples but only 76.9 % of the shifted target. Weighted conformal prediction (Tibshirani et al., 2019) restores coverage under covariate shift with the weights of Proposition 9.3.

  • Ensembles. Several networks trained from different initialisations (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017) disagree where data are scarce; their spread is a practical estimate of model uncertainty.
  • Robustness. Test against realistic perturbations (noise levels, gaps, gains, a different tool) before worrying about adversarial ones.
  • Interpretability. Permutation importance measures the drop in score when one log is shuffled; check that the model relies on inputs that make physical sense. When a simple model is almost as good, it is often the better product.

9.4 Compute and memory constraints

Proposition 9.6 — Error of weight quantisation Round every weight of a linear unit u = w^\top x to the nearest multiple of \Delta, giving \tilde w. Then |\tilde w^\top x - w^\top x| \le \frac{\Delta}{2}\, \|x\|_1 . If the rounding errors are independent and uniform, the output error has variance \frac{\Delta^2}{12}\, \|x\|_2^2.

Proof. Each error e_i = \tilde w_i - w_i satisfies |e_i| \le \Delta/2, so |\sum_i e_i x_i| \le \frac{\Delta}{2}\sum_i |x_i|. Under independence, the variance of \sum_i e_i x_i is \sum_i x_i^2\, \mathbb{E} e_i^2, and \mathbb{E} e_i^2 = \Delta^2/12 by Proposition 2.5. ∎

Storing weights in 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats divides memory by 4 and enables integer arithmetic (Jacob et al., 2018); the step is set per tensor or per channel from the range of the weights. Distillation trains a small student network on the soft outputs of a large teacher (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015). Pruning removes weights or channels of small importance.

Activations, not weights, often dominate memory. A seismic cube of 1000^3 samples in 32-bit floats occupies 4 GB, and one 32-channel feature map of the same size would need 128 GB. Large volumes are therefore processed in overlapping tiles. The overlap must exceed the receptive field (Definition 0.8), and predictions are blended with weights that vanish at tile borders to avoid seams.

9.5 From prototype to a deployable solution

  • Frame the decision. Agree with the users on what the output will change and on the acceptable error, in their units.
  • Own the data pipeline. Units, depth references, versions of tools and of processing; every training set is a versioned artefact.
  • Validate like you deploy. Blind wells or blind fields (ch. 7.1), a classical baseline from each chapter, and task-based metrics (ch. 4.1).
  • Ship uncertainty and quality flags. Conformal intervals or ensemble spread (9.3), and the quality-control indicators of ch. 4.2.
  • Keep a human in the loop. Interpreters validate or correct, and their corrections become new labels.
  • Monitor and retrain. Track input statistics against the training distribution (9.2), and retrain when they drift.
  • Document. A model card states intended use, data, metrics and known limits (Mitchell et al., 2019); reproducibility practices are the subject of Fundamentals of Reproducible Research.

Code 9.7 — importance weighting and conformal coverage under shift.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def draw(n, mean, sd):
    x = rng.normal(mean, sd, n)
    return x[:, None], np.sin(x) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(n)

Xs, ys = draw(2000, 0.0, 1.0)            # source: training field
Xt, yt = draw(2000, 1.5, 0.6)            # target: new field (covariate shift)

# domain classifier -> density ratio w(x) = p_t(x) / p_s(x)  (Proposition 9.3)
feats = lambda X: np.hstack([X, X ** 2])
dom = LogisticRegression().fit(feats(np.vstack([Xs, Xt])), np.r_[np.zeros(2000), np.ones(2000)])
p = dom.predict_proba(feats(Xs))[:, 1]
w = p / (1 - p)                            # equal sample sizes
print(f"effective sample size: {w.sum() ** 2 / (w ** 2).sum():.0f} of 2000")

for name, sw in [("unweighted", None), ("importance-weighted", w)]:
    m = LinearRegression().fit(Xs, ys, sample_weight=sw)     # misspecified model
    print(f"{name:20s} target MSE = {np.mean((m.predict(Xt) - yt) ** 2):.3f}")

# split conformal prediction (Theorem 9.5), alpha = 0.1
m = LinearRegression().fit(Xs[:1000], ys[:1000])
scores = np.abs(ys[1000:] - m.predict(Xs[1000:]))            # calibration residuals
q = np.sort(scores)[int(np.ceil(0.9 * 1001)) - 1]
Xs2, ys2 = draw(2000, 0.0, 1.0)
cover = lambda X, y: np.mean(np.abs(y - m.predict(X)) <= q)
print(f"coverage, same distribution: {cover(Xs2, ys2):.3f}")
print(f"coverage, shifted field    : {cover(Xt, yt):.3f}")

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Symmetric label noise keeps the Bayes classifier but ruins calibration; structured noise needs corrections.
  • Under covariate shift, importance weights from a domain classifier correct the risk, at a cost in effective sample size.
  • Split conformal intervals cover with probability 1 - \alpha under exchangeability, and lose it under shift.
  • Quantisation, distillation, pruning and tiling fit models to hardware; quantisation error is bounded by \frac{\Delta}{2}\|x\|_1.
  • Deployment is a process: framing, versioned data, blind validation, uncertainty, human review, monitoring, documentation.

10. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It points to active research lines that extend the course, with surveys and founding papers to start from.

Surveys. Bergen et al. (2019) review machine learning for data-driven discovery in solid Earth geoscience, and Reichstein et al. (2019) argue for combining deep learning with process understanding in Earth system science. Yu and Ma (2021) survey deep learning for geophysics, and Mousavi and Beroza (2022) do the same for seismology. Karpatne et al. (2017) set out the programme of theory-guided data science, close to the “integration with physical models” of this course.

Physics-informed networks and neural operators. Physics-informed neural networks (Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis, 2019) add the residual of a differential equation to the training loss. Neural operators learn the solution map of a family of equations: the Fourier neural operator (Li et al., 2021) and DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021). As fast surrogates of forward models, they can make nonlinear inversion (ch. 8.5) and uncertainty quantification affordable. Their accuracy outside the training family remains an open question.

Generative priors and posterior sampling. Score-based and diffusion models trained on images can serve as priors for inverse problems, and their samples approximate the posterior instead of its mean (Song et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2023). This answers the blur of Theorem 3.3 and the hallucination risk of ch. 3.3 at once, provided the samples are calibrated. Checking that calibration on field data, where the truth is unknown, is open.

Full-waveform inversion. Full-waveform inversion fits entire seismic waveforms by solving the wave equation, and it suffers from local minima when the starting model is too far from the truth (Virieux and Operto, 2009). Adler, Araya-Polo and Poggio (2021) review how deep learning accelerates or replaces parts of seismic inversion workflows.

Data-driven inverse problems. Arridge et al. (2019) give a mathematical overview of learned regularisers, unrolled schemes and their theory. Guarantees of stability and data consistency for learned reconstructions are still partial.

Open problems close to this course:

  • generalisation to a new basin or tool with an uncertainty that remains valid under shift;
  • detecting hallucinated structures in super-resolved or inpainted images without ground truth;
  • learning from interpreter disagreement rather than averaging it away;
  • benchmarks with blind wells and fields, so that published scores measure R_{\mathrm{new}} (Definition 7.1);
  • hybrid physics–learning models whose outputs provably fit the data at the noise level;
  • self-supervised pre-training on large archives of logs, images and seismic, and its evaluation across fields.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • Surveys of machine learning in geoscience stress the combination of data-driven models with physical understanding.
  • Physics-informed networks and neural operators offer fast surrogates of forward models, with open questions on extrapolation.
  • Generative priors turn super-resolution and inversion into posterior sampling, if their calibration can be checked.
  • Full-waveform and learned inversion inherit the ill-posedness of ch. 8; data consistency remains the test.
  • Generalisation to new fields with valid uncertainty is the central open problem.

Exercises

  1. ★ A thin coal bed on a density log. A density tool with a 40 cm box response (Proposition 1.3) logs a 10 cm coal bed of density 1.40 g/cm³ inside a sandstone of density 2.45 g/cm³. What is the lowest density the log can show? How thick must a bed be for the log to show at least 90 % of the true contrast?

    Solution: \Delta = -1.05 g/cm³ and the apparent contrast is at most \frac{10}{40}\Delta = -0.26 g/cm³, so the log reads about 2.19 g/cm³ at best.

    With a box response the apparent contrast is \frac{h}{w}\Delta for h < w and \Delta for h \ge w; 90 % therefore requires h \ge 0.9\, w = 36 cm.

  2. ★ Dip from a sinusoid. On the image of an 8.5-inch borehole (r = 10.8 cm), a fracture reaches its shallowest point at azimuth 200° and depth 1012.40 m, and its deepest point at azimuth 20° and depth 1012.90 m. Give its dip, dip azimuth and the parameter z_0 of Theorem 1.5.

    Solution: the amplitude is half the span, r\tan\theta = 0.25 m, so \tan\theta = 2.315 and \theta = 66.6°. The dip azimuth is the azimuth of the deepest point, \varphi_d = 20°, and z_0 = 1012.65 m is the mid-depth.

  3. ★ PSNR of a uniform quantiser. An image with values in [0, 1] is quantised with b bits, that is with step \Delta = 2^{-b}. Using Proposition 2.5, show that \mathrm{PSNR} \approx 6.02\, b + 10.79 \text{ dB}. Check the formula against the 8-bit line of code 2.8.

    Solution: with \mathrm{MSE} = \Delta^2/12 and peak 1, \mathrm{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}(12 \cdot 4^b) = 10\log_{10}12 + 20\, b \log_{10}2 = 10.79 + 6.02\, b . For b = 8 this gives 58.95 dB, and the measured MSE 1.27 \times 10^{-6} gives 58.96 dB.

  4. ★ Reflectivity and back. Three layers have impedances 6.0, 7.5 and 5.0 (in 10^6 SI units). Compute the two reflection coefficients, then recover the impedance ratios with Proposition 8.5, and compare with the small-contrast rule \Delta \ln Z \approx 2R.

    Solution: R_1 = 1.5/13.5 = 0.111 and R_2 = -2.5/12.5 = -0.2. Then 2\operatorname{artanh}(0.111) = 0.2231 = \ln(7.5/6) and 2\operatorname{artanh}(-0.2) = -0.4055 = \ln(5/7.5), exactly. The approximation gives 0.222 and −0.400, errors of 0.4 % and 1.3 %.

  5. ★ Dynamic time warping by hand. Align a = (1, 3, 2) with b = (1, 1, 3, 2) with the squared difference (Theorem 5.4). Give the table, the optimal cost and an optimal path. Why is the cost zero although the sequences have different lengths?

    Solution: the table is \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 4 & 4 & 0 & 1 \\ 5 & 5 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, so the cost is 0, with path (1,1), (1,2), (2,3), (3,4). The sequence b is a with its first sample repeated, a pure stretch, which warping absorbs at no cost.

  6. ★ Ranking metrics. (a) Two rankings of five items are (A, B, C, D, E) and (B, A, C, E, D); compute Kendall’s tau. (b) A retrieval system returns four intervals with relevances (2, 3, 0, 1); compute DCG@4 and NDCG@4.

    Solution: (a) two discordant pairs, (A, B) and (D, E), out of 10, so \tau = (8 - 2)/10 = 0.6. (b) \mathrm{DCG} = 3 + 7/\log_2 3 + 0 + 1/\log_2 5 = 7.85; the ideal order (3, 2, 1, 0) gives 9.39, so NDCG = 0.835.

  7. ★★ PCA with one component. For zero-mean x with covariance C, show that \max_{\|q\| = 1} q^\top C q = \lambda_1 and that \mathbb{E}\|x - qq^\top x\|^2 = \operatorname{tr} C - q^\top C q . Apply to the covariance with rows (4, 2) and (2, 1).

    Solution: in the eigenbasis, q^\top C q = \sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2 with \sum_i c_i^2 = 1, at most \lambda_1; the error formula is step 1 of the proof of Theorem 2.4 with k = 1. Here the eigenvalues are 5 and 0, with first direction (2, 1)/\sqrt 5: the data lie on a line and one component codes them without error.

  8. ★★ Three estimators for one ambiguous sample. Given the coarse data, a fine sample equals 0 with probability 0.7 and 1 with probability 0.3. Compute the MMSE, median and MAP estimates, and the expected squared and absolute errors of the first two.

    Solution: the mean is 0.3; the median and the mode are 0. For c = 0.3: squared error 0.21, absolute error 0.42. For c = 0: squared error 0.30, absolute error 0.30. Each estimate wins on its own loss (Theorem 3.3, Proposition 3.5), and the MMSE value 0.3 never occurs in reality.

  9. ★★ Harmonic inpainting on a line. Values u_0 = a and u_{m+1} = b are known, and u_1, \dots, u_m minimise \sum_{i=0}^{m} (u_{i+1} - u_i)^2. Show that the minimiser is the linear interpolation between a and b, and deduce the maximum principle in this case. What does this predict for a thin layer crossing a wide gap?

    Solution: the optimality condition u_i = \frac12(u_{i-1} + u_{i+1}) says the second difference vanishes, so u_i = a + (b - a)\frac{i}{m+1}, which lies between a and b. A thin layer seen on one side only is not continued: the fill joins the two edges linearly, which motivates the geometric priors of ch. 6.2.

  10. ★★ Dice, IoU and empty masks. For |A| = 30, |B| = 40 and |A \cap B| = 20, compute IoU and Dice and check Proposition 7.6. What happens when both masks are empty, and why does the soft Dice loss of code 7.8 add \varepsilon?

    Solution: IoU = 20/50 = 0.4 and Dice = 40/70 = 0.571 = 0.8/1.4. With two empty masks both ratios are 0/0; a convention (score 1) is needed. The \varepsilon makes the loss defined and equal to 0 in that case, and smooths the gradient on images with few positive pixels.

  11. ★★ The best Tikhonov parameter in one dimension. Let y = s\, x^\star + \varepsilon with \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), and x_\lambda = s y / (s^2 + \lambda). Using Theorem 8.2, find the \lambda that minimises the mean squared error, and compare with Proposition 0.6.

    Solution: with f = s^2/(s^2 + \lambda), the error is (1 - f)^2 x^{\star 2} + f^2 \sigma^2 / s^2, minimal at f = s^2 x^{\star 2} / (s^2 x^{\star 2} + \sigma^2). Hence \lambda^\star = \frac{\sigma^2}{x^{\star 2}}, the MAP weight of Proposition 0.6 with prior variance \tau^2 = x^{\star 2}.

  12. ★★ Well-wise validation, computed. Starting from code 7.3: (a) vary the standard deviation of the well offsets from 0 to 1.2 and plot random and well-wise accuracies; (b) make well 0 contain 80 % of facies 0 and measure the effect of per-well standardisation on that well; (c) propose a normalisation that survives (b).

    Hints and expected result: (a) the two curves coincide near 0 and separate as the offset grows. (b) Standardisation subtracts a mean dominated by facies 0, so that well’s accuracy drops. (c) Normalise on a reference interval or on a facies that every well contains (for example a regional shale), as interpreters do.

  13. ★★ How much data do importance weights leave? Let the source be \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and the target \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1). Compute the weight w and its first two moments under the source, and deduce the limit of the effective sample size divided by n. Evaluate for \mu = 1.5 and n = 2000.

    Solution: w(x) = e^{\mu x - \mu^2/2}, \mathbb{E}_s[w] = 1 and \mathbb{E}_s[w^2] = e^{\mu^2}. So \frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}{n} \to e^{-\mu^2}, about 211 samples out of 2000 for \mu = 1.5, the same order as the 347 of code 9.7 (whose target is narrower).

  14. ★★ Conformal coverage is also bounded above. With continuous scores (no ties), show that \mathbb{P}(|Y - f(X)| \le \hat q) \le 1 - \alpha + \frac{1}{n + 1}. Is the coverage of 0.902 printed by code 9.7 surprising?

    Solution: the event is exactly “rank \le k”, of probability k/(n+1), and k \le (1 - \alpha)(n + 1) + 1. For n = 1000 and \alpha = 0.1 the marginal coverage lies in [0.9, 0.901].

    With one fixed calibration set, the realised coverage fluctuates with a standard deviation near \sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)/n} \approx 0.009, so 0.902 is expected.

  15. ★★★ Losses for super-resolution. From code 3.7, train the CNN with the squared loss and with the absolute loss. Compare the RMSE, the sharpness at bed boundaries (mean absolute jump over the true boundaries) and the data residual \|A\hat x - y\| relative to the noise level. Then add a small adversarial term and discuss the perception–distortion trade-off.

    Hints: expect similar RMSE, sharper boundaries with the absolute loss (Proposition 3.5), and sharper but less faithful outputs with the adversarial term. A data residual far above \sigma\sqrt{m} reveals invented structure.

  16. ★★★ Physics-guided gap filling. Combine Theorem 1.5 with ch. 6.2: on the image of code 6.7 with added fractures, detect sinusoids on the measured pads (least squares on picked points, or a Hough transform), extend them across the gaps, and inpaint only the residual harmonically. Evaluate with hold-out masks.

    Hints: the target is an RMSE in the gaps below the 0.139 of harmonic filling, and a dip error below 2° for fractures that cross a gap. Discuss the failure cases: several crossing fractures, non-planar features, and breakouts.
