  • The written exam in Projects and assessment is a proposal for study. The official assessment remains the individual mini-projects, with both stored counts kept. ## 0. Review: gradients and a short history of deep learning {#ch0}

Three M1 tools open the course: the gradient of a squared loss, the chain rule, and a short map of where the models came from. The official first topic is the history of deep learning and its relation to cognitive science, so that history shares the hour with the calculus.

Definition 0.1 — Affine predictor For a vector x, an affine predictor is f(x) = Wx + b, with a weight matrix W and a bias b. A linear classifier reads the sign of one coordinate of f(x). Later networks stack several affine maps and put a nonlinearity between them.

Proposition 0.2 — Gradient of the squared loss Let X have n rows, let y be the vector of targets, and define L(w) = \frac{1}{2n}\|Xw - y\|^2 . The gradient is \nabla L(w) = \frac{1}{n} X^\top (Xw - y) .

Proof.

  • Expand the square. The terms that depend on w are the quadratic form built from X^\top X and a linear term in w.
  • The gradient of the quadratic piece, after the factor 1/(2n), equals (1/n) X^\top X w.
  • The linear piece contributes -(1/n) X^\top y. Their sum is the displayed gradient. ∎

Keep the matrix gradient. Back-propagation in ch. 1 is that identity together with the chain rule. \nabla L(w) = \frac{1}{n} X^\top (Xw - y) .

A frequent slip is to minimize the mean of squares while using the gradient of the half-mean. The two gradients differ by a factor of two, so the step is twice as long as intended. Exercise 2 descends on the half-mean and then prints the mean of squares.

If a scalar loss depends on w only through an intermediate vector u, the chain rule multiplies the upstream gradient by the Jacobian of the map that builds u. Proposition 0.2 is the case where u is the vector of residuals of a linear map. Every layer in ch. 1 applies the same pattern once.

McCulloch and Pitts (1943) treated a neuron as a threshold on a weighted sum. Rosenblatt (1958) fitted such a threshold from labelled examples. Minsky and Papert (1969) showed that a single threshold cannot compute XOR. Exercise 1 is that argument in a few lines. Hidden layers are the practical reply, and they are useless without a way to pass a gradient through them.

Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986) made back-propagation that way. LeCun, Bottou, Bengio and Haffner (1998) trained a convolutional stack on handwritten digits. The stack follows a physiological picture. Hubel and Wiesel (1962) described simple and complex cells in visual cortex, and Fukushima (1980) built the neocognitron in that image. The engineering models borrow the hierarchy. They do not claim to be a cortex.

Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton (2012) won ImageNet with a deep convolutional net. The top-5 error was 15.3%, against 26.2% for the next entry in the same contest. LeCun, Bengio and Hinton (2015) review the work that followed. People still learn many visual tasks from far fewer labelled examples (Lake, Ullman, Tenenbaum and Gershman, 2017). That gap is an open question in ch. 8.

Example 0.3 — One explicit step Take two samples, let X be the identity, let the targets be 1 and -1, and start at w = 0. Then L(0) = 1/2, and the gradient equals (-1/2,\, 1/2).

A step of size 1 lands on (1/2,\, -1/2). The loss there is 1/8.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The half-mean squared loss has gradient (1/n)\, X^\top (Xw - y).
  • The chain rule pushes that gradient through one intermediate map at a time.
  • A single threshold cannot solve XOR. Hidden layers and back-propagation are the historical reply.
  • ImageNet in 2012 marks the modern empirical period. The cortical story is a source of architectures, not a claim that a trained net is a brain.

1. Feedforward networks, regularisation and optimization

A feedforward net is a composition of affine maps and simple nonlinearities, trained by gradient descent on a scalar loss. This chapter writes the composition, the cross-entropy gradient, and the two-layer chain rule, then names the regularisers and the step used in practice.

Definition 1.1 — Feedforward network Fix an input h_0 = x. For layers \ell = 1,\ldots,L, z_\ell = W_\ell h_{\ell-1} + b_\ell, \qquad h_\ell = \varphi(z_\ell) . The map \varphi acts coordinatewise. The ReLU is \varphi(t) = \max(t, 0) (Nair and Hinton, 2010). The network output is h_L, or z_L when the loss already contains a softmax.

Definition 1.2 — Softmax cross-entropy For logits z \in \mathbb{R}^K and a class index y, let p be the softmax of z. The loss on one sample is -\log p_y.

Proposition 1.3 — Softmax gradient With p the softmax of z, the derivatives of the cross-entropy are \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_k} = p_k - \mathbf{1}\{k = y\} .

Proof.

  • Write \log p_y = z_y - \log \sum_j e^{z_j}. The loss is the negative of this expression.
  • The derivative of the log-sum-exp in coordinate k equals p_k.
  • The derivative of z_y equals 1 only in coordinate y. Subtracting these two contributions gives the display. ∎

Theorem 1.4 — Two-layer back-propagation Let z_1 = W_1 x and write h = \varphi(z_1). Set z_2 = W_2 h.

Let \delta_2 be the gradient of the scalar loss in z_2. Then \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_2} = \delta_2 h^\top, \qquad \delta_1 = \big(W_2^\top \delta_2\big) \odot \varphi'(z_1), \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_1} = \delta_1 x^\top . Where the ReLU is off, that coordinate of \varphi' is 0. Where it is on, the coordinate is 1.

Proof.

  • The differential of z_2 = W_2 h splits into a piece linear in dW_2 and a piece linear in dh. Pairing with \delta_2 produces the outer product \delta_2 h^\top.
  • The chain rule through \varphi multiplies by \varphi'(z_1) coordinatewise, which builds \delta_1 from W_2^\top \delta_2.
  • The same outer-product step, now from z_1 = W_1 x, produces \delta_1 x^\top. A minibatch sums these outer products. ∎

Weight decay adds (\lambda/2)\|W\|^2 to the loss. The extra gradient is \lambda W, so each step shrinks the weights as well as fitting the data. Dropout drops hidden units at random during training and rescales so that the expected activation matches test time (Srivastava, Hinton, Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Salakhutdinov, 2014). Batch normalisation recentres each coordinate on the minibatch and then applies a learned scale and shift (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015).

Stochastic gradient descent replaces the full gradient by a minibatch average (Robbins and Monro, 1951). Adam keeps running averages of the gradient and of its square, and Kingma and Ba (2015) give 0.001 as the default step size. For ReLU layers, He, Zhang, Ren and Sun (2015) draw the weights with variance 2/n_{\mathrm{in}}.

A dead ReLU is a practical failure of the same derivative. If a pre-activation is negative, \varphi' is zero along that coordinate and the local gradient does not revive it. A smaller step, a different initial bias, or a leaky variant is the usual repair. Exercise 5 computes a case where the unit is active, so the derivative is 1.

Example 1.5 — An exact XOR network Label inputs (0, 0) and (1, 1) with output 0, and label (1, 0) and (0, 1) with output 1. The hidden units are the ReLU of x_1+x_2 and the ReLU of x_1+x_2-1. The output is the first hidden unit minus twice the second.

The four outputs are 0, 1, 1 and 0. A linear classifier cannot match this labelling. Exercise 1 proves that claim.

Cybenko (1989) showed that one hidden layer of sigmoids can approximate continuous functions on a compact set. The argument does not choose the number of units, and it does not say that gradient descent will find them.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • A feedforward net alternates affine maps and a coordinatewise nonlinearity such as the ReLU.
  • The softmax cross-entropy has gradient p - e_y in the logits.
  • Two-layer back-propagation is two outer products and one coordinatewise product with \varphi'.
  • Weight decay, dropout and batch normalisation change the update. Adam is a default adaptive step, not a different model.

2. Representation learning and Siamese networks

Classification asks for a label. Representation learning asks for a vector whose distances mean something: same object close, different objects far. A Siamese net answers that request with one embedding function applied to every input.

Definition 2.1 — Siamese embedding An embedding is a map f_\theta from inputs to a Euclidean space. A Siamese network evaluates the same f_\theta on two or three inputs and trains \theta from the distances between the outputs. The weights are shared, so the notion of distance does not depend on which branch saw the input (Bromley, Guyon, LeCun, Säckinger and Shah, 1993).

Proposition 2.2 — Triplet hinge For an anchor a, a positive p and a negative n, and a margin m > 0, the FaceNet loss uses squared Euclidean distances (Schroff, Kalenichenko and Philbin, 2015): L = \max\big(0,\; \|f(a)-f(p)\|^2 - \|f(a)-f(n)\|^2 + m\big) . If the positive is already closer than the negative by at least m, then L = 0 and a subgradient with respect to the embeddings is 0.

Proof.

  • The expression inside the maximum is an affine function of the two squared distances.
  • On the open set where that expression is negative, the hinge is locally constant.
  • A constant has derivative 0, so the zero vector is a subgradient there. When the expression is positive, a subgradient puts weight +1 on the positive squared distance and weight -1 on the negative one. ∎

Train until positives beat negatives by the margin, and stop pushing a triplet once that happens. L = \max\big(0,\; d(a,p) - d(a,n) + m\big) .

Easy negatives never violate the margin, so they contribute a zero gradient and waste the batch. Hard negatives violate it and move the embedding. In practice one mines negatives that sit close to the margin, rather than negatives that are already far away. The loss above does not, by itself, stop every embedding from collapsing to one point if you only sample easy pairs.

Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun (2006) wrote an earlier contrastive loss on pairs. A pair labelled similar is pulled together. A pair labelled dissimilar is pushed apart until a margin. The triplet form is the same idea with an explicit comparison inside the anchor. FaceNet then used the triplet loss to embed face images so that a threshold on distance verifies identity.

Example 2.3 — Two numerical triplets Let f(a) = (1, 0) and f(p) = (1, 0.5), and take the margin m = 0.2. The squared distance from anchor to positive is 0.25.

If f(n) = (0, 0), the negative squared distance is 1 and the hinge is negative, so the loss is 0.

If f(n) = (1, 0.1), the negative squared distance is 0.01 and the loss equals 0.44.

The 1993 signature network was already Siamese: two branches, shared weights, a distance, a verification decision. The modern name “representation learning” adds the habit of reusing f_\theta on a task that was not the training task. ch. 3 freezes such a vector and trains a new head. ch. 6 learns the vector without class labels.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • A Siamese net applies one shared map to every branch, then trains from distances.
  • The triplet hinge is zero when the positive wins by at least the margin.
  • Easy negatives do not move the parameters. Mining matters as much as the formula.
  • The same embedding can be reused for verification or as a frozen feature for a new head.

3. Generative adversarial networks and transfer learning

A generator maps noise z to a sample. A discriminator scores how much a sample looks like the training data. Transfer learning starts from the opposite end: a net trained on a large labelled set, reused on a smaller one. Both topics are on the official list for this session.

Definition 3.1 — Adversarial value Write p_{\mathrm{data}} for the data distribution and p_g for the distribution of G(z). Goodfellow, Pouget-Abadie, Mirza, Xu, Warde-Farley, Ozair, Courville and Bengio (2014) study V(D,G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))] . The discriminator maximizes V. The generator minimizes it.

Theorem 3.2 — Optimal discriminator Fix the generator, and assume the two densities are positive. The discriminator that maximizes V is D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)}{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} . At this choice, the value equals 2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_g) - \log 4. The minimum over generators is -\log 4, about -1.386, and it is reached when p_g = p_{\mathrm{data}}.

Proof.

  • For a fixed x, maximize a \log y + b \log(1-y) over y \in (0,1), with a = p_{\mathrm{data}}(x) and b = p_g(x). The critical point is y = a/(a+b).
  • The second derivative -a/y^2 - b/(1-y)^2 is negative, so the critical point is a maximum. That is D^*.
  • Substitute D^* and set m = (p_{\mathrm{data}}+p_g)/2. The value becomes the sum of two Kullback–Leibler divergences to m, minus \log 4.
  • That sum is twice the Jensen–Shannon divergence. It vanishes only when p_g = p_{\mathrm{data}}, and the value is then -\log 4. ∎

The best discriminator at a point is the data density divided by the sum of the two densities. D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)}{p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)+p_g(x)} .

When the discriminator rejects the generator, \log(1-D(G(z))) is flat and the generator gradient vanishes. Goodfellow and coauthors (2014) therefore train the generator to maximize \log D(G(z)) instead. The two games have the same equilibrium and different early gradients. A second failure is mode collapse: G emits one sharp mode that fools D and ignores the rest of the data. The value in Theorem 3.2 still equals -\log 4 only when the full distributions match, so a collapsed generator has not won the theorem.

Example 3.3 — One point, two densities Suppose at a point the data density is 0.8 and the generator density is 0.2. Theorem 3.2 gives D^* = 0.8.

If the two densities agree everywhere, then D^* = 1/2 and the value is -\log 4. Exercise 8 asks for both calculations.

Transfer starts from a net already trained on a large visual set. Early layers respond to edges and textures. Later layers respond to the classes of that set. Yosinski, Clune, Bengio and Lipson (2014) measured that the early features transfer across visual tasks more readily than the last layer, and that fine-tuning the whole net recovers part of the gap.

A practical protocol has three steps. Train a head on top of a frozen trunk. Unfreeze the trunk. Continue with a smaller step size than the one used on the original set, so the transferred features move only a little. ch. 6 removes the need for the original labels by learning the trunk from a pretext task. The official list treats that case separately.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • A GAN is a game between a generator and a discriminator, with the value V above.
  • The optimal discriminator is the normalized data density, and the best value is -\log 4.
  • The non-saturating generator loss keeps a gradient when D is confident. Collapse is still possible.
  • Transfer reuses early features. Fine-tuning with a small step adapts them to the new task.

4. Recurrent networks and LSTMs for language

Language arrives as a sequence. A recurrent net keeps a state, updates it with the next token, and predicts the token after that. The official topic for this session is that model, including the LSTM, aimed at natural language processing.

Definition 4.1 — Recurrent state Let x_t be the input at time t and let h_t be the state. A simple recurrent layer sets h_t = \varphi(W h_{t-1} + U x_t + b) . The same W, U and b are used at every time. A language model reads h_t through a softmax and scores the next token.

Proposition 4.2 — Product of Jacobians Let J_k be the Jacobian of the state at one time with respect to the previous state.

The derivative of a loss at a later time contains the product of those Jacobians. If every factor has operator norm at most \rho, a product of m factors has norm at most \rho^m.

Proof.

  • The chain rule writes the derivative through h_{t+1},\ldots,h_T as a product of the successive Jacobians.
  • The operator norm of a product is at most the product of the norms, so each bound \rho contributes one factor.
  • If \rho < 1, the factor \rho^{T-t} goes to 0 as T-t grows, and early tokens stop receiving a gradient. If \rho > 1, the same product grows and the gradient explodes. ∎

Early tokens are trained through a product of Jacobians. A factor below 1 vanishes. A factor above 1 explodes. \Big\|\frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t}\Big\| \le \rho^{T-t} .

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) introduced the long short-term memory cell so that a path through time can have derivative near 1. The cell keeps a vector c_t. A forget gate multiplies c_{t-1}, an input gate writes a candidate, and an output gate exposes the updated cell. If the forget gate stays near 1, the product along the cell does not shrink, and a signal from an early token can reach the loss.

Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio (2013) document the explosion in the other direction and use gradient clipping: if the gradient norm exceeds a threshold, rescale it. Clipping does not fix a vanishing product. It only stops a single step from diverging. The LSTM is the architectural answer to vanishing. Clipping is the numerical answer to explosion.

The training loss is the average next-token cross-entropy. On a sequence y_1,\ldots,y_T, the model defines p(y_t \mid y_{<t}) from the state, and the loss is the mean of -\log p(y_t \mid y_{<t}). A unigram model, which ignores the state, is the baseline a recurrent net has to beat. Exercise 9’s chance calculation is the same idea in a contrastive disguise. The language version is the unigram.

Sutskever, Vinyals and Le (2014) encode a source sentence with one LSTM and decode the target with another. Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2015) let the decoder look at every encoder state, not only the last one. That attention step is the bridge to the transformers in ch. 8. This chapter stops at the recurrent cell, which is the official topic.

Example 4.3 — A scalar product Take a linear state h_t = 0.5\, h_{t-1} and a loss that depends on h_{10}. The derivative of h_{10} with respect to h_0 equals 0.5^{10} = 1/1024, about 9.77 \times 10^{-4}.

Replace 0.5 by 1.1. Ten steps multiply the gradient by about 2.59. Exercise 7 asks for twenty steps at 0.8 and at 1.2.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • A recurrent layer reuses one map at every time and keeps a state.
  • Back-propagation through time multiplies Jacobians. Norms below 1 vanish, and norms above 1 explode.
  • The LSTM cell adds a path whose multiplier is a forget gate, which can stay near 1.
  • Next-token cross-entropy is the language-model loss. Attention, later, looks beyond the last state.

5. Object detection

Classification names the object. Detection names it and localizes it with a box. The official topic is that second task. The numbers that decide a correct box are the intersection over union and the average precision built from it.

Definition 5.1 — Intersection over union For two regions A and B of positive area, \mathrm{IoU}(A,B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} . A detection is a true positive at threshold \tau when its IoU with an unmatched ground-truth box of the same class is at least \tau. Matching walks down the list of detections in score order, which is the Pascal VOC protocol (Everingham, Van Gool, Williams, Winn and Zisserman, 2010).

Proposition 5.2 — The ratio is at most one For any two regions, \mathrm{IoU}(A,B) \le 1 . Equality holds when A and B have the same area as their intersection. For axis-aligned boxes of positive area, that forces the boxes to coincide.

Proof.

  • The intersection is contained in each region, so its area is at most |A| and at most |B|.
  • Adding those two inequalities yields 2|A \cap B| \le |A| + |B|, hence |A \cap B| \le |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|.
  • The right-hand side is |A \cup B|. The ratio of the two sides is at most 1. Equality needs |A \cap B| = |A| = |B|. ∎

A box is a hit only when the overlap ratio clears the threshold. The ratio never exceeds 1. \mathrm{IoU}(A,B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} .

Average precision summarizes a ranked list. Sort detections of one class by score. Precision at a rank is the fraction of those predictions that are true positives. Recall is the fraction of ground-truth objects recovered. Average precision is the area under the precision-recall curve. Mean average precision averages that area over classes.

The threshold \tau changes the curve. Pascal VOC uses \tau = 0.5. The COCO challenge averages average precision over thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05 (Lin, Maire, Belongie, Hays, Perona, Ramanan, Dollár and Zitnick, 2014). A method can lead at 0.5 and fall behind once the boxes must be tight.

Two families dominate the modern baselines. The two-stage line proposes regions, then classifies them. Girshick, Donahue, Darrell and Malik (2014) classified regions from a convolutional trunk. Girshick (2015) shared the trunk across regions. Ren, He, Girshick and Sun (2015) learned the proposals with a region network. The one-stage line predicts boxes on a grid in one pass. Redmon, Divvala, Girshick and Farhadi (2016) did this with YOLO. Liu, Anguelov, Erhan, Szegedy, Reed, Fu and Berg (2016) did it with SSD, at several scales.

Without a suppression step, one object produces many overlapping boxes. Non-maximum suppression keeps the highest score and deletes boxes whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold, then repeats. Omitting it inflates the false-positive count and collapses precision.

Example 5.3 — Two squares Let A be the square with corners (0, 0) and (2, 2), and let B have corners (1, 1) and (3, 3). Each area is 4. The intersection has area 1, and the union has area 7.

The IoU equals 1/7, about 0.143. At the VOC threshold 0.5 this detection is not a true positive. Exercise 4 is this calculation.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Intersection over union is the overlap area divided by the union area, and it is at most 1.
  • A true positive needs that ratio to clear a threshold, with greedy matching by score.
  • Average precision is the area under the precision-recall curve. VOC and COCO use different thresholds.
  • Two-stage detectors propose then classify. One-stage detectors predict boxes in one pass. Suppression removes duplicates.

6. Self-supervised methods

Labels are expensive. A self-supervised method builds a training signal from the input alone, then reuses the resulting network on a labelled task. The official topic is that family. The loss that organizes the contrastive half of it is InfoNCE.

Definition 6.1 — InfoNCE Let q be a query vector and let one positive key k_+ sit among N-1 negative keys. With a temperature \tau > 0, van den Oord, Li and Vinyals (2018) use L = -\log \frac{\exp(q \cdot k_+ / \tau)}{\sum_{k} \exp(q \cdot k / \tau)} , where the sum runs over the positive and the negatives. The loss is the cross-entropy of picking the positive out of N candidates.

Proposition 6.2 — Chance baseline If every score q \cdot k is equal, then L = \log N. Any embedding that ranks the positive strictly above the negatives gets a strictly smaller loss.

Proof.

  • Equal scores make the softmax uniform, so each candidate has weight 1/N.
  • The cross-entropy of the correct class under the uniform distribution is -\log(1/N), which equals \log N.
  • Raising the positive score above the others increases its softmax weight above 1/N and therefore lowers -\log of that weight. ∎

A constant embedding scores \log N. Anything useful has to beat that number. L_{\mathrm{chance}} = \log N .

At the optimal scores, the same loss also lower-bounds a mutual information by \log N - L (van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, 2018). The proof is in that paper. The chance baseline above is the piece used in the exercises.

Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton (2020) turn the idea into SimCLR. Two random augmentations of one image are the query and the positive. The other images in the minibatch are the negatives. The score is a cosine similarity, divided by a temperature. A very small temperature makes the softmax nearly a hard maximum and starves the gradient. Chen and coauthors use a temperature near 0.1.

He, Fan, Wu, Xie and Girshick (2020) keep a queue of negatives from past minibatches, so the contrast set can be larger than the current batch. The query encoder is trained by gradient descent. The key encoder is a slow average of the query encoder, which stabilizes the queue. Grill and coauthors (2020) later drop the negatives entirely and rely on a stop-gradient to avoid collapse. Exercise 9 asks why the contrastive form already prices a constant embedding at \log N, and what changes when the negatives disappear.

Masked prediction is the other official strand. Devlin, Chang, Lee and Toutanova (2019) hide tokens and ask a transformer to recover them. He, Chen, Xie, Li, Dollár and Girshick (2022) hide image patches and ask the net to reconstruct pixels. In both cases the input is its own label. The learned trunk then transfers with the protocol of ch. 3.

Example 6.3 — Three scores Take temperature 1, a positive score 2, and negative scores 0 and 1. The normalizer is e^{2} + e^{0} + e^{1}.

The loss equals 0.408 to three decimal places. A constant embedding on the same three candidates would score \log 3, about 1.099, which is worse.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • InfoNCE is the cross-entropy of finding the positive key among N candidates.
  • Equal scores give loss \log N. A useful embedding must do better.
  • SimCLR uses two views and in-batch negatives. A queue, as in MoCo, enlarges the contrast set.
  • Masked tokens and masked patches are self-supervised targets that transfer like a labelled trunk.

7. Deep reinforcement learning

The last official topic replaces a fixed dataset with an agent that changes the data by acting. The mathematical object is a Markov decision process. The algorithms that made the topic practical are a deep Q-network and a policy gradient.

Definition 7.1 — Markov decision process A process has states, actions, a transition kernel, a reward, and a discount \gamma \in (0,1). A policy chooses the action.

The return is the random sum of discounted rewards from a given time onward. The action-value Q^\pi(s,a) is the expected return from a chosen state and action, after which the policy continues.

Theorem 7.2 — Bellman equation For every state s and action a, Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\big[r + \gamma\, Q^\pi(s', a')\big] , where the reward and the next state follow the transition, and the next action follows \pi.

Proof.

  • Split the return into the immediate reward plus \gamma times the return from the next time.
  • Take the expectation conditional on s and a. The inner return, given the next state and the next action, has conditional expectation Q^\pi(s', a').
  • The Markov property makes that conditional expectation depend on the past only through (s', a'). Taking the remaining expectation yields the display. ∎

A deep Q-network represents Q by a neural net with weights \theta (Mnih and coauthors, 2015). One step of Q-learning replaces the right-hand side by a target. The target uses a frozen copy \theta^- of the weights: y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta^-) . The online net is trained to regress Q(s,a;\theta) toward y, on minibatches drawn from a replay buffer of past transitions. The buffer weakens the correlation between successive updates. The frozen copy keeps the target from chasing the online net at every step. Exercise 10 shows why the maximum inside y is biased high when the values are noisy.

Proposition 7.3 — Score-function gradient Let the action set be finite, and let J(\theta) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a)\, r(a) be the expected reward of one draw. Then \nabla J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[r(a)\, \nabla \log \pi_\theta(a)\big] .

Proof.

  • Differentiate under the finite sum. Each term contributes r(a)\, \nabla \pi_\theta(a).
  • Use \nabla \pi = \pi\, \nabla \log \pi on each action, which is the derivative of a logarithm.
  • The sum is then the expectation of r(a)\, \nabla \log \pi_\theta(a) under \pi_\theta. ∎

The policy gradient is an expectation of reward times the score. No derivative of the reward is required. \nabla J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[r(a)\, \nabla \log \pi_\theta(a)\big] .

Williams (1992) extends the same identity from one draw to a trajectory. The reward r(a) is replaced by a return, and the score is summed along the visited states. Subtracting a baseline that does not depend on the action leaves the expectation unchanged and can cut the variance. Sutton and Barto (2018) give the book form of this argument. The one-step proof above is the piece to recompute by hand.

Example 7.4 — One softmax step Two actions start with equal logits, so each probability is 1/2. Action A pays reward 1 and action B pays 0. A sample of action A has score (1/2,\, -1/2) in the two logits.

A step of size 1 adds that score to the logits. The new probability of action A is 1/(1+e^{-1}), about 0.731. The expected reward moves from 0.5 to about 0.731.

Function approximation, bootstrapping, and off-policy replay can diverge together (Sutton and Barto, 2018). The target network and the replay buffer are engineering controls, not a convergence theorem. The grid-world project keeps the grid small enough that the greedy policy can be checked.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Q^\pi(s,a) is the expected return, and it satisfies the Bellman equation.
  • DQN regresses a neural net onto a target that maximizes a frozen copy, using a replay buffer.
  • The maximum in that target is biased high under noise. Exercise 10 computes a toy case.
  • The score-function identity gives a policy gradient without differentiating the reward.

8. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is self-study, outside the 24 hours. It collects directions that start from the official list and then leave it. The voice is that of the papers. Nothing here is a claim about the instructors’ current research.

Attention and scale

Vaswani and coauthors (2017) replaced most of the recurrent stacks of ch. 4 with self-attention. A token mixes with every other token in one step, so the vanishing product of Jacobians is no longer the bottleneck. The official content list stops at LSTMs. Foundations of Large Language Models and ALTEGRAD take up the transformer as a semester 1 neighbour, not as a prerequisite of this page.

Kaplan and coauthors (2020) fitted power laws: in the range they studied, the loss of a language model falls as a power of the parameter count, the data, and the compute. Hoffmann and coauthors (2022) then argued that many large models had been under-trained, and that a compute-optimal recipe spends more of the budget on data. The exponents are empirical fits. They are not theorems of ch. 1.

Generative models after the original GAN

Theorem 3.2 characterizes the equilibrium of the original adversarial game. Later practice often left that game. Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020) generate images by reversing a noising process, one small Gaussian step at a time. The training loss is a denoising objective, not a discriminator. Generative Models for Images develops that line. Mode collapse, as described in ch. 3, is less typical there, and a different set of failure modes appears, including a sample that is sharp and still wrong in its fine geometry.

Radford and coauthors (2021) train an image encoder and a text encoder with a contrastive loss of the same family as ch. 6, on pairs collected from the web. The embedding of ch. 2 becomes a joint space. How much of that alignment survives on a laptop-sized corpus is open.

Generalization and deep control

Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht and Vinyals (2017) showed that standard nets can fit random labels and still drive the training loss to zero. A uniform bound that treats every labelling as equal then says nothing about the real test error. The theoretical course linked from the introduction is the place for the partial answers. What a practitioner can measure on a mini-project is a gap between training and held-out loss, not a proof.

Henderson and coauthors (2018) showed that reported scores in deep reinforcement learning move with the seed, the codebase and the length of training. A single lucky run is not a result. The grid-world project in the assessment section therefore asks for 100 evaluation episodes, not for one screenshot. Sample efficiency on that grid, once the net is larger than the table, is still open. Mnih and coauthors (2015) needed millions of frames on Atari. A method that keeps a neural net and solves the 5 by 5 grid in a few thousand transitions would be a real finding.

Lake, Ullman, Tenenbaum and Gershman (2017) return to the cognitive question of ch. 0. People learn new visual concepts from a handful of examples. The nets in these notes do not. Closing that gap is not a homework. It is a research problem with no agreed formalization.

Questions to explore

  • On a toy corpus, does a one-layer attention model beat the LSTM of ch. 4 at the same parameter count, and by how much in nats per token?
  • For the mixture of Gaussians in the group project, which failure appears first as the step size grows: collapse to one mode, or a discriminator that saturates?
  • Can you predict the sign of the Q-learning bias in Exercise 10 before you train, from the noise in the target alone?
  • What annotation would you add to a detector so that a box carries a calibrated uncertainty, not only a score and an IoU?
  • Which piece of ch. 7 would you change first to cut the number of transitions needed on the 5 by 5 grid: the replay buffer, the target copy, or the network width?

Exercises

Levels: ★ applies one result, ★★ combines two results or a short proof with a calculation, ★★★ is open-ended. Exercises 2 and 6 each contain a Python fence and quote a number that fence prints.

  1. ★ A linear classifier cannot fit XOR. The points (0,0) and (1,1) are labelled negative. The points (1,0) and (0,1) are labelled positive. Show that no weights and bias satisfy the four strict inequalities of a linear separator.

    Solution. Suppose w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b > 0 on the positive points and w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b < 0 on the negative points. Adding the two positive inequalities yields w_1 + w_2 + 2b > 0. Adding the two negative inequalities yields w_1 + w_2 + 2b < 0. The same quantity cannot be both positive and negative.

  2. ★ A seeded half-mean step. The code below draws 32 inputs in dimension 4, builds targets from the weight (1.5, -0.5, 0.25, 2) plus noise of scale 0.1, and takes 20 steps of size 0.1 on the half-mean squared loss of Proposition 0.2, starting from w = 0. It then prints the mean of squares.

    import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(32, 4))
true_w = numpy.array([1.5, -0.5, 0.25, 2.0])
y = X @ true_w + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=32)
w = numpy.zeros(4)
for _ in range(20):
    residual = X @ w - y
    grad = X.T @ residual / 32.0
    w = w - 0.1 * grad
mse = float(numpy.mean((X @ w - y) ** 2))
print(f"{mse:.6f}")

    Solution. The printed mean squared error is 0.301251. The update uses the gradient of L in Proposition 0.2, which is half the gradient of the mean of squares, and the printed number is that mean of squares after 20 steps. Noise of scale 0.1 remains, so the error does not reach zero.

  3. ★ Counting parameters. A dense net maps 784 inputs to 128 hidden units and then to 10 logits, with a bias on every layer. A convolution has 3 input channels, 8 filters of size 3 \times 3, and a bias on each filter. How many parameters does each map have?

    Solution. The dense map has 784 \times 128 + 128 + 128 \times 10 + 10 = 101770 parameters. Each filter has 3 \times 3 \times 3 weights and one bias, so eight filters have 8 \times 28 = 224 parameters.

  4. ★ Intersection over union of two squares. Let A = [0,2] \times [0,2] and B = [1,3] \times [1,3]. Compute the IoU. Is the detection a true positive at the Pascal VOC threshold 0.5?

    Solution. Each square has area 4. The intersection is [1,2] \times [1,2], of area 1. The union has area 4 + 4 - 1 = 7. The IoU equals 1/7, about 0.143, which is below 0.5, so the detection is not a true positive. Proposition 5.2 only says the ratio cannot exceed 1. It does not make this pair a hit.

  5. ★★ Back-propagation through one active ReLU. Take a scalar net with input x = 2, first weight 0.5, first bias -0.5, second weight -4 and second bias 1. The loss is the square of the output, and the target is 0. Compute the output and the derivative of the loss with respect to the first weight.

    Solution. The pre-activation is 0.5, so the ReLU stays on and outputs 0.5. The network output is -1, and the loss is 1.

    The upstream derivative is -2. The hidden derivative is 8, so the derivative in the first weight is 16. Theorem 1.4 gives the same outer-product step.

  6. ★★ A finite-difference check of a ReLU gradient. The fence draws a weight and an input with the seeded generator, sets the target to 1.7, and compares the analytic gradient of (\max(0, w \cdot x) - y)^2 with a central difference of width 10^{-6}. It prints the pre-activation and the largest absolute gap.

    import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
w = rng.normal(size=3)
x = rng.normal(size=3)
y = 1.7
z = float(numpy.dot(w, x))
analytic = 2.0 * (z - y) * x
eps = 1e-6
fd = numpy.zeros(3)
for i in range(3):
    e = numpy.zeros(3)
    e[i] = eps
    lp = (max(0.0, float(numpy.dot(w + e, x))) - y) ** 2
    lm = (max(0.0, float(numpy.dot(w - e, x))) - y) ** 2
    fd[i] = (lp - lm) / (2 * eps)
gap = float(numpy.max(numpy.abs(analytic - fd)))
print(f"{z:.4f}")
print(f"{gap:.3e}")

    Solution. The fence prints 0.3155 and then 5.399e-11. The pre-activation is positive, so the ReLU is locally linear and the analytic gradient 2(z-y)x is valid. The gap is rounding error, not a bug in Theorem 1.4.

  7. ★★ A product of scalar Jacobians. A scalar recurrence multiplies the state by \rho at every step. Compute \rho^{20} for \rho = 0.8 and for \rho = 1.2. Which product is the vanishing regime of Proposition 4.2, and which is the exploding regime?

    Solution. 0.8^{20} equals 0.01153 to five decimals. 1.2^{20} equals 38.34 to two decimals. The factor 0.8 has norm below 1, so twenty steps vanish. The factor 1.2 has norm above 1, so the same horizon explodes. Clipping would rescale the large gradient. It would not restore the small one.

  8. ★★ The optimal discriminator at one point. At a point, the data density is 0.6 and the generator density is 0.4. Compute D^*. Then suppose the two densities agree everywhere. What is D^*, and what is the adversarial value?

    Solution. Theorem 3.2 gives D^* = 0.6 / (0.6 + 0.4) = 0.6. When the densities agree, D^* = 1/2 and the value equals -\log 4, about -1.386. That number is the minimum of the game. A generator that matches the data has reached it.

  9. ★★★ The chance baseline, and what happens without negatives. Prove Proposition 6.2. For N = 4, evaluate the chance loss. Then explain, in a short paragraph, why Grill and coauthors (2020) need an extra device once negatives are removed, while SimCLR still has a number to beat.

    Hints and expected result. Equal scores give a uniform softmax, so the loss is \log N. For N = 4 that is \log 4, about 1.386. SimCLR still pays \log N for a constant embedding, so collapse is an expensive stationary point, not a minimum. Bootstrap Your Own Latent drops the negatives. A constant embedding is then no longer punished by a contrastive denominator, and the stop-gradient between the two branches is what blocks the trivial solution. A full-mark note names that asymmetry and does not claim that SimCLR is immune to every other degeneracy.

  10. ★★★ Upward bias of the maximum in a Q-target. Two actions have true action-value 0. Independent noise equals +1 or -1 with equal probability on each action. Let the backed-up target be the maximum of the two noisy values. Compute its expectation. Then say what van Hasselt, Guez and Silver (2016) change in DQN to fight this bias.

    Hints and expected result. The four noise pairs are (1, 1), (1, -1), (-1, 1) and (-1, -1), equally likely. Their maxima are 1, 1, 1 and -1, so the expectation is 1/2.

    The true maximum is 0, so the target is biased upward by 1/2. Double DQN selects the action with the online network and scores it with the target network. A full-mark note states both the expectation and this split.
