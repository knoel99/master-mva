Audience: a student with a good M1 in mathematics, statistics or signal processing who wants to know why classical estimators fail when the dimension is comparable to the number of samples, and how to correct them.

Empirical first: draw 1000 samples of a 500-dimensional standard Gaussian vector. The true covariance is the identity, so every eigenvalue equals 1. Yet the eigenvalues of the sample covariance spread from 0.08 to 2.87, and its condition number is about 35 (code in ch. 0). This spread is not bad luck: the Marchenko–Pastur law predicts both ends within 0.05, and it biases every method that plugs the sample covariance in.

This course studies the spectra of large random matrices when both dimensions grow at the same rate. It builds the tools (Stieltjes transform, Gaussian calculus, deterministic equivalents), proves the two founding limit theorems, and then uses them to design and analyse estimators and detectors for wireless communications, array processing, graphs and kernel methods.

It shares tools with Kernel Methods for Machine Learning, Graphs in Machine Learning, Detection Theory and Industrial Applications and Theoretical Foundations of Deep Learning.

What you will be able to do

  • Apply Gaussian integration by parts and the Poincaré–Nash inequality to compute expectations and bound variances of traces of resolvents.
  • State and prove the Marchenko–Pastur theorem for Gaussian data, and compute its support, density and moments.
  • Write the deterministic equivalents of sample covariance, variance-profile and signal-plus-noise models, and solve their fixed-point equations numerically.
  • Predict the ergodic capacity of a large multi-antenna channel and explain why it grows linearly with the number of antennas.
  • Locate the outlier eigenvalues of a spiked model, compute the alignment of its eigenvectors, and explain the phase transition.
  • Use the Tracy–Widom law to set the threshold of a test based on the largest eigenvalue.
  • Derive the generalized likelihood ratio test for source detection and predict its power in the high-dimensional regime.
  • Build consistent G-estimators of population eigenvalues and powers, and use them to locate a transmitter.
  • Correct the MUSIC algorithm for large arrays, detect communities in a dense graph, and analyse kernel spectral clustering with the same spiked-model tools.

0. Review: eigenvalues, resolvents and Gaussian vectors

Every chapter manipulates the eigenvalues of large Hermitian matrices through their resolvents, and averages quadratic forms of random vectors. This chapter collects the M1-level facts used later, with short proofs.

The Linear Algebra, Analysis and Optimization refresher and the Probability and Statistics refresher cover the same ground at a slower pace.

The phenomenon that motivates the course takes five lines of NumPy: white data, a sample covariance, and eigenvalues that are far from 1.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, n = 500, 1000                      # dimension, number of samples
X = rng.standard_normal((N, n))       # white data: true covariance = I
S = X @ X.T / n                       # sample covariance
lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(S)
c = N / n
print(f"smallest / largest eigenvalue: {lam.min():.3f} / {lam.max():.3f}")
print(f"Marchenko-Pastur edges       : {(1 - c**0.5)**2:.3f} / {(1 + c**0.5)**2:.3f}")
print(f"condition number of S        : {lam.max() / lam.min():.1f}")

The output gives eigenvalues between 0.082 and 2.868, against the predicted edges 0.086 and 2.914 of ch. 3, and a condition number of 34.9 instead of 1.

Eigenvalue inequalities

Throughout, A and B are Hermitian N \times N matrices, with eigenvalues in decreasing order \lambda_1(A) \geq \dots \geq \lambda_N(A). The spectral norm is \|A\| = \max_k |\lambda_k(A)|.

Theorem 0.1 — Courant–Fischer min-max For every k, \lambda_k(A) = \max_{\dim V = k}\; \min_{x \in V,\, \|x\| = 1} x^* A x .

Proof. Let (e_i) be an orthonormal eigenbasis. On V_0 = \mathrm{span}(e_1, \dots, e_k), every unit x has x^* A x \geq \lambda_k, so the maximum is at least \lambda_k.

Conversely, any V of dimension k meets \mathrm{span}(e_k, \dots, e_N), of dimension N - k + 1, in a non-zero vector. On that vector x^* A x \leq \lambda_k \|x\|^2. ∎

Proposition 0.2 — Weyl and interlacing inequalities (a) Weyl: for every k, |\lambda_k(A) - \lambda_k(B)| \leq \|A - B\| .

(b) Interlacing: if \mathrm{rank}(A - B) \leq r, then, for every k \leq N - r and symmetrically in A and B, \lambda_{k + r}(A) \leq \lambda_k(B) . The numbers of eigenvalues of A and of B above any level t differ by at most r.

Proof. (a) x^* A x \leq x^* B x + \|A - B\| for unit x; take the max-min of Theorem 0.1 on both sides, then exchange A and B.

(b) Let V realize \lambda_{k+r}(A) in Theorem 0.1. The space V' = V \cap \ker(A - B) has dimension at least k, and A = B on it. Hence \lambda_{k+r}(A) \leq \min_{x \in V',\, \|x\| = 1} x^* B x \leq \lambda_k(B) . The counting statement follows. ∎

Resolvents, Sherman–Morrison and Schur complements

Definition 0.3 — Resolvent The resolvent of A at z \in \mathbb{C} outside its spectrum is Q_A(z) = (A - z I_N)^{-1} . We write \mathbb{C}^+ = \{z : \Im z > 0\} and, for z \in \mathbb{C}^+, v = \Im z.

Proposition 0.4 — Resolvent toolbox For z \in \mathbb{C}^+:

  1. \|Q_A(z)\| \leq 1 / v, and more generally \|Q_A(z)\| = 1/\mathrm{dist}(z, \mathrm{spec}\, A).
  2. Resolvent identity: Q_A - Q_B = Q_A (B - A) Q_B, and \frac{d}{dz} Q_A = Q_A^2.
  3. Sherman–Morrison: for invertible M and vectors u, w with 1 + w^* M^{-1} u \neq 0, (M + u w^*)^{-1} u = \frac{M^{-1} u}{1 + w^* M^{-1} u} .
  4. Determinant lemma: \det(M + u w^*) = \det(M)\,(1 + w^* M^{-1} u).

Proof. (1) Q_A is normal with eigenvalues 1/(\lambda_k - z), of modulus at most 1/v. (2) Multiply (A - z)^{-1} on the left and (B - z)^{-1} on the right of (B - z) - (A - z).

(3) Multiply both sides by M + u w^*. (4) Factor out M: \det(I + M^{-1} u w^*) = 1 + w^* M^{-1} u, since M^{-1} u w^* has rank one. ∎

The Schur complement formula inverts a matrix by blocks: if M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b^* \\ b & D \end{pmatrix} with a scalar and D invertible, the top-left entry of the inverse is (M^{-1})_{11} = \frac{1}{a - b^* D^{-1} b} . It gives the diagonal entries of a resolvent in terms of the resolvent of a smaller matrix, which is the starting point of the general-entries proofs of ch. 13.

Quadratic forms of random vectors

The workhorse of the course is the concentration of x^* A x around \mathrm{tr}\, A when x has independent standardized entries and A is independent of x.

Proposition 0.5 — Trace lemma Let x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_N) and A a deterministic real symmetric matrix. Then \mathbb{E}\, x^\top A x = \mathrm{tr}\, A, \qquad \mathrm{Var}\,(x^\top A x) = 2\, \mathrm{tr}\, A^2 \leq 2 N \|A\|^2 . Hence the normalized form satisfies \frac{1}{N} x^\top A x - \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, A = O_P\big(\|A\| N^{-1/2}\big) . Here Y_N = O_P(a_N) means that Y_N / a_N is bounded in probability.

Proof. Write A = O D O^\top with O orthogonal. The vector g = O^\top x is again standard Gaussian, and x^\top A x = \sum_k d_k g_k^2. The g_k^2 are independent with mean 1 and variance 2. ∎

For independent entries with mean 0, variance 1 and finite fourth moment, and complex A, the same bound holds up to a constant (Bai and Silverstein, 2010, Lemma B.26): \mathbb{E}\, |x^* A x - \mathrm{tr}\, A|^2 \leq C\, \mathrm{tr}(A A^*) .

Probability reminders A sequence of probability measures \mu_N converges weakly to \mu when \int f\, d\mu_N \to \int f\, d\mu for every bounded continuous f.

Borel–Cantelli: if \sum_N \mathbb{P}(|Y_N| > \varepsilon) < \infty for every \varepsilon > 0, then Y_N \to 0 almost surely. With Chebyshev’s inequality, a variance of order N^{-2} is enough.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Weyl: eigenvalues move by at most the norm of the perturbation. Interlacing: a rank-r perturbation moves the eigenvalue counts by at most r.
  • The resolvent is bounded by 1/\Im z off the real axis. Sherman–Morrison and the determinant lemma handle rank-one updates.
  • A quadratic form x^* A x concentrates around \mathrm{tr}\, A at relative order N^{-1/2}.

1. Spectral measures and the Stieltjes transform

The object of the course is the distribution of the eigenvalues of a large matrix. The Stieltjes transform turns it into an analytic function that equals the normalized trace of the resolvent, and that is what the proofs compute.

Definition 1.1 — Empirical spectral distribution The empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of a Hermitian N \times N matrix A and its distribution function are \mu_A = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N \delta_{\lambda_k(A)}, \qquad F^A(t) = \frac{1}{N}\, \#\{k : \lambda_k(A) \leq t\} . When A is random, \mu_A is a random probability measure.

Definition 1.2 — Stieltjes transform The Stieltjes transform of a probability measure \mu on \mathbb{R} is the function on \mathbb{C}^+ m_\mu(z) = \int \frac{\mu(d\lambda)}{\lambda - z} . For an ESD it is a normalized trace of the resolvent: m_{\mu_A}(z) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^N \frac{1}{\lambda_k(A) - z} = \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, Q_A(z) .

Proposition 1.3 — Basic properties Let m = m_\mu and z = x + iv \in \mathbb{C}^+.

  1. m is analytic on \mathbb{C}^+ and \Im m(z) > 0.
  2. |m(z)| \leq 1/v.
  3. -iy\, m(iy) \to 1 as y \to +\infty.
  4. If \mu is carried by [0, \infty), then \Im\big(z\, m(z)\big) \geq 0.

Proof. (1) Differentiate under the integral; the imaginary part of 1/(\lambda - z) is v/|\lambda - z|^2 > 0. (2) |\lambda - z| \geq v.

(3) -iy/(\lambda - iy) \to 1 pointwise, with modulus at most 1: dominated convergence.

(4) The imaginary part of z/(\lambda - z) equals \lambda v / |\lambda - z|^2 \geq 0 when \lambda \geq 0. ∎

Conversely, an analytic map from \mathbb{C}^+ to \mathbb{C}^+ with property 3 is the Stieltjes transform of a probability measure (Anderson, Guionnet and Zeitouni, 2010, §2.4). This is how a solution of a fixed-point equation is recognized as a Stieltjes transform.

Theorem 1.4 — Inversion formula If a < b are not atoms of \mu, then \mu\big((a, b)\big) = \lim_{\varepsilon \downarrow 0} \frac{1}{\pi} \int_a^b \Im\, m_\mu(x + i\varepsilon)\, dx . If \mu has a density f continuous at x, then f(x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \downarrow 0} \frac{1}{\pi} \Im\, m_\mu(x + i\varepsilon) .

Proof. The imaginary part is a convolution with the Cauchy density P_\varepsilon of scale \varepsilon: \frac{1}{\pi} \Im\, m_\mu(x + i\varepsilon) = \int \frac{1}{\pi} \frac{\varepsilon}{(\lambda - x)^2 + \varepsilon^2}\, \mu(d\lambda) = (P_\varepsilon * \mu)(x) .

By Fubini, the integral over (a, b) equals \int \frac{1}{\pi} \Big[ \arctan \frac{b - \lambda}{\varepsilon} - \arctan \frac{a - \lambda}{\varepsilon} \Big]\, \mu(d\lambda) .

The integrand is bounded by 1 and tends to 1 on (a, b), to 1/2 at a and b, and to 0 outside [a, b].

Dominated convergence and \mu(\{a\}) = \mu(\{b\}) = 0 give the first claim. The second is the convergence of P_\varepsilon * \mu at a continuity point of the density. ∎

Theorem 1.5 — Continuity theorem Let \mu_N and \mu be probability measures on \mathbb{R}. Then \mu_N \to \mu weakly if and only if m_{\mu_N}(z) \to m_\mu(z) for every z \in \mathbb{C}^+.

It suffices that the convergence holds on a countable set with an accumulation point in \mathbb{C}^+. For random measures, convergence almost surely on such a set implies weak convergence almost surely.

Proof. (\Rightarrow) For fixed z, \lambda \mapsto 1/(\lambda - z) is bounded and continuous.

(\Leftarrow) By Helly’s selection theorem, every subsequence has a further subsequence converging vaguely to a sub-probability measure \nu. The function 1/(\lambda - z) vanishes at infinity, so m_\nu = m_\mu on the countable set. Both are analytic on \mathbb{C}^+, so they agree everywhere by the identity theorem.

Theorem 1.4 then gives \nu = \mu. As \mu has mass 1, no mass escapes, the convergence is weak, and the limit does not depend on the subsequence. For random measures, apply this on the event of probability one where the countable convergence holds. ∎

The Stieltjes transform also controls finite-rank perturbations, which is the first hint of the spiked models of ch. 6.

Proposition 1.6 — Rank perturbations do not move the ESD If \mathrm{rank}(A - B) \leq r, then \sup_t |F^A(t) - F^B(t)| \leq \frac{r}{N}, \qquad |m_{\mu_A}(z) - m_{\mu_B}(z)| \leq \frac{\pi r}{N v} .

Proof. The first bound is the counting statement of Proposition 0.2. For the second, integrate by parts: m_{\mu_A}(z) - m_{\mu_B}(z) = \int \frac{d(F^A - F^B)(t)}{t - z} = \int \frac{F^A(t) - F^B(t)}{(t - z)^2}\, dt . Its modulus is at most \frac{r}{N} \int \frac{dt}{(t - x)^2 + v^2} = \frac{\pi r}{N v} . ∎

So the ESD cannot see a perturbation of fixed rank. Centring the data, for instance, subtracts a rank-one matrix from the sample covariance and does not change its limit (Exercise 2). Individual eigenvalues, however, can move far away: that is the subject of ch. 6.

Example 1.7 — Two atoms For A = \mathrm{diag}(1, 3), m_{\mu_A}(z) = \frac{1}{2} \Big( \frac{1}{1 - z} + \frac{1}{3 - z} \Big) . At z = 2 + 0.1 i, this gives m = 0.099\, i: the real parts cancel at the midpoint, and \frac{1}{\pi}\Im\, m = 0.03 says that there is little mass near 2.

At z = 1 + 0.1 i, \frac{1}{\pi}\Im\, m = 1.60. This is a Cauchy bump of height \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon} \approx 1.59 above the atom at 1, which carries mass 1/2, plus 0.004 from the atom at 3.

As \varepsilon \to 0 the bump concentrates on the atom, as Theorem 1.4 says.

The code recovers the density of a sample covariance spectrum from its Stieltjes transform, at three values of \varepsilon:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N, n = 400, 1600
c = N / n
X = rng.standard_normal((N, n))
lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X @ X.T / n)

def stieltjes(z):                     # m_N(z) = (1/N) tr (S - z I)^{-1}
    return np.mean(1.0 / (lam - z))

a, b = (1 - c**0.5) ** 2, (1 + c**0.5) ** 2
def mp_density(x):
    return np.sqrt(max((b - x) * (x - a), 0.0)) / (2 * np.pi * c * x)

print("x     eps=0.1  eps=0.03  eps=0.01  MP density")
for x in [0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0]:
    approx = [stieltjes(x + 1j * eps).imag / np.pi for eps in (0.1, 0.03, 0.01)]
    print(f"{x:<5} " + "  ".join(f"{v:7.3f}" for v in approx) + f"  {mp_density(x):9.3f}")

With \varepsilon = 0.1 the estimate is smoothed (0.669 against 0.842 at x = 0.5, near the left edge). With \varepsilon = 0.01, close to the eigenvalue spacing, it starts to follow individual eigenvalues (0.231 against 0.211 at x = 2). The useful range is between the spacing 1/N and the scale of the density.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • The Stieltjes transform of an ESD is \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, Q(z): spectra become traces of resolvents.
  • Inversion: the density is \frac{1}{\pi} \Im\, m(x + i0). Continuity: weak convergence is pointwise convergence of m on \mathbb{C}^+.
  • A rank-r perturbation changes the ESD by at most r/N: limit spectra ignore low-rank signals, individual eigenvalues do not.

2. Gaussian matrix calculus and the Poincaré–Nash inequality

For matrices with Gaussian entries, two tools replace most of the combinatorics of random matrix theory: integration by parts computes expectations of traces of resolvents, and the Poincaré–Nash inequality shows that their variance is negligible. This approach goes back to Pastur, and Hachem, Loubaton, Najim and coauthors used it systematically for wireless channels.

Integration by parts and the Poincaré–Nash inequality

Lemma 2.1 — Gaussian integration by parts Let x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Gamma) in \mathbb{R}^d, and f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{C} of class C^1.

If f and \nabla f have polynomial growth, then, for every i, \mathbb{E}\big[x_i\, f(x)\big] = \sum_{j=1}^d \Gamma_{ij}\, \mathbb{E}\big[\partial_j f(x)\big] .

Proof. For d = 1 and \Gamma = 1, the standard density \varphi satisfies \varphi'(t) = -t \varphi(t), so an integration by parts gives \int t f(t) \varphi(t)\, dt = -\int f(t) \varphi'(t)\, dt = \int f'(t) \varphi(t)\, dt . For independent standard coordinates, apply this to one coordinate with the others fixed. In general write x = \Gamma^{1/2} g with g standard and use the chain rule. ∎

For a complex Gaussian z \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, 1) the same computation gives \mathbb{E}[z f] = \mathbb{E}[\partial f / \partial \bar z], which is the form used for complex channels.

Theorem 2.2 — Poincaré–Nash inequality With x and f as in Lemma 2.1 and f real, \mathrm{Var}\, f(x) \leq \mathbb{E}\big[ \nabla f(x)^\top\, \Gamma\, \nabla f(x) \big] . For complex f, applying it to \Re f and \Im f gives \mathbb{E}\, |f(x) - \mathbb{E} f(x)|^2 \leq \mathbb{E}\big[ \overline{\nabla f(x)}^\top\, \Gamma\, \nabla f(x) \big] .

Proof. By the change of variables x = \Gamma^{1/2} g, assume \Gamma = I. Let y be an independent copy of x, and for t \in [0, 1] let Z_t = \sqrt{t}\, x + \sqrt{1 - t}\, y, \qquad \phi(t) = \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\, f(Z_t)\big] .

  • Endpoints: Z_1 = x and Z_0 = y is independent of x, so \phi(1) - \phi(0) = \mathrm{Var}\, f(x).
  • Derivative: by the chain rule, \phi'(t) = \mathbb{E}\Big[f(x)\, \nabla f(Z_t) \cdot \Big(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{t}} - \frac{y}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\Big)\Big] .
  • Lemma 2.1 in y gives \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\, \partial_i f(Z_t)\, y_i\big] = \sqrt{1 - t}\; \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\, \partial_{ii} f(Z_t)\big] .
  • Lemma 2.1 in x gives \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\, \partial_i f(Z_t)\, x_i\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[\partial_i f(x)\, \partial_i f(Z_t)\big] + \sqrt{t}\; \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\, \partial_{ii} f(Z_t)\big] .

The second-derivative terms cancel, which leaves \phi'(t) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\, \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla f(x) \cdot \nabla f(Z_t)\big] .

Since Z_t has the same law as x, Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the expectation by \mathbb{E} |\nabla f(x)|^2. Integrating, \int_0^1 \frac{dt}{2\sqrt{t}} = 1 gives the claim. Functions that are only C^1 are handled by smoothing. ∎

Derivatives of resolvents

In the rest of the chapter and in ch. 3, X is an N \times n matrix with independent \mathcal{N}(0, 1) entries and columns x_1, \dots, x_n. We set \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X X^\top, \qquad Q(z) = (\Sigma - z I_N)^{-1}, \qquad m_N(z) = \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, Q(z) .

Proposition 2.3 — Derivatives of the resolvent Let e_i be the i-th canonical vector of \mathbb{R}^N. Then \frac{\partial Q}{\partial X_{ij}} = -\frac{1}{n}\, Q \big(e_i x_j^\top + x_j e_i^\top\big) Q, \qquad \frac{\partial\, \mathrm{tr}\, Q}{\partial X_{ij}} = -\frac{2}{n}\, \big(Q^2 X\big)_{ij} .

Proof. Differentiate Q (\Sigma - z) = I: \partial Q = -Q\, (\partial \Sigma)\, Q, and \frac{\partial \Sigma}{\partial X_{ij}} = \frac{1}{n}\big(e_i x_j^\top + x_j e_i^\top\big) . For the trace, use \mathrm{tr}(Q A Q) = \mathrm{tr}(Q^2 A) and the symmetry Q^\top = Q. ∎

Proposition 2.4 — The trace of the resolvent barely fluctuates For z \in \mathbb{C}^+ with v = \Im z, \mathbb{E}\, |m_N(z) - \mathbb{E}\, m_N(z)|^2 \leq \frac{4}{N n v^4} . Consequently, if N/n \to c > 0, then m_N(z) - \mathbb{E}\, m_N(z) \to 0 almost surely, for every z \in \mathbb{C}^+.

Proof. By Proposition 2.3 and \|Q\| \leq 1/v, \sum_{i,j} \Big| \frac{\partial m_N}{\partial X_{ij}} \Big|^2 = \frac{4}{N^2 n^2}\, \|Q^2 X\|_F^2 \leq \frac{4}{N^2 n^2 v^4}\, \|X\|_F^2 . Theorem 2.2 with \mathbb{E} \|X\|_F^2 = N n gives the bound. It is of order N^{-2}, which is summable: Chebyshev and Borel–Cantelli give the almost sure convergence. ∎

The variance is of order N^{-2}, not N^{-1}: \mathrm{tr}\, Q is a sum of N terms but fluctuates by O(1) only. This rigidity of spectra is quantified by the central limit theorem of ch. 13.

First application: the semicircle law

A Wigner matrix is the simplest model. Wigner (1955, 1958) found its limit law by the moment method (ch. 13); the Gaussian method proves it in a few lines.

Theorem 2.5 — Semicircle law for the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble Let G be N \times N with independent \mathcal{N}(0, 1) entries and W = (G + G^\top)/\sqrt{2N}. Almost surely, \mu_W converges weakly to the semicircle law \sigma(dx) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{4 - x^2}\; \mathbf{1}_{[-2, 2]}(x)\, dx , whose Stieltjes transform is the root with positive imaginary part of m^2 + z m + 1 = 0: m_{\mathrm{sc}}(z) = \frac{-z + \sqrt{z^2 - 4}}{2} . The branch of the square root behaves like z at infinity.

Proof. Let Q = (W - z)^{-1} and m_N = \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, Q.

  1. Resolvent identity. W Q = I + z Q, so that \mathbb{E}\, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}(W Q) = 1 + z\, \mathbb{E}\, m_N .
  2. Integration by parts. W is linear in G, so Lemma 2.1 in the variables G_{ab}, with \partial Q = -Q\, (\partial W)\, Q, gives for all i, j \mathbb{E}\big[W_{ij} Q_{ji}\big] = -\frac{1}{N}\, \mathbb{E}\big[Q_{ji}^2 + Q_{ii} Q_{jj}\big] .
  3. Summation. Summing over i, j and dividing by N: 1 + z\, \mathbb{E}\, m_N = -\mathbb{E}\, m_N^2 - \frac{1}{N^2}\, \mathbb{E}\, \mathrm{tr}\, Q^2 .
  4. Closing the equation. The last term is at most 1/(N v^2). Theorem 2.2 gives a variance of order N^{-2} (Exercise 9), so \mathbb{E}\, m_N^2 = (\mathbb{E}\, m_N)^2 + O(N^{-2}), and (\mathbb{E}\, m_N)^2 + z\, \mathbb{E}\, m_N + 1 = O\big(N^{-1}\big) .
  5. Identification. The functions \mathbb{E}\, m_N are analytic and bounded by 1/v, so by Montel’s theorem every subsequence has a further subsequence converging locally uniformly on \mathbb{C}^+ to an analytic m. This limit solves m^2 + zm + 1 = 0 and \Im m \geq 0.
  6. The right root. The two roots have product 1, so their imaginary parts have opposite signs, and neither is real. Exactly one root lies in \mathbb{C}^+, so m = m_{\mathrm{sc}}, and the whole sequence converges.
  7. Conclusion. Proposition 2.4 (in its Wigner form) and Theorem 1.5 give the almost sure weak convergence; Theorem 1.4 turns m_{\mathrm{sc}} into the density, since \Im \sqrt{x^2 - 4} = \sqrt{4 - x^2} on (-2, 2). ∎

Example 2.6 — The golden ratio At z = i, z^2 - 4 = -5 and the branch gives \sqrt{z^2 - 4} = i\sqrt{5}. Hence m_{\mathrm{sc}}(i) = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2}\, i \approx 0.618\, i . Directly, \Im\, m_{\mathrm{sc}}(i) is the average of 1/(1 + x^2) under the semicircle law, since \Im \frac{1}{x - i} = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}.

The code checks the two orders of magnitude of the proof: the bias of \mathbb{E}\, m_N is of order 1/N and the variance of order 1/N^2.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)

def goe(N):
    G = rng.standard_normal((N, N))
    return (G + G.T) / np.sqrt(2 * N)          # off-diagonal variance 1/N

z = 1.0 + 0.5j
m_sc = (-z + np.sqrt(z - 2) * np.sqrt(z + 2)) / 2   # branch with Im m > 0
print(f"semicircle m(z) = {m_sc:.4f}")
for N in [50, 100, 200, 400]:
    ms = np.array([np.mean(1 / (np.linalg.eigvalsh(goe(N)) - z)) for _ in range(200)])
    print(f"N={N:3d}: mean m_N = {ms.mean():.4f}, "
          f"|bias| = {abs(ms.mean() - m_sc):.1e}, N^2 Var = {N**2 * ms.var():.3f}")

The bias falls from 3.9 \times 10^{-3} to 3.2 \times 10^{-4} as N goes from 50 to 400, and N^2\, \mathrm{Var}\, m_N stays near 1.7.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Integration by parts moves x_i onto a derivative of f. With \partial Q = -Q\, (\partial A)\, Q, it turns \mathbb{E}\, \mathrm{tr}(A Q) into products of traces.
  • Poincaré–Nash bounds \mathrm{Var} f by \mathbb{E}\, \nabla f^\top \Gamma \nabla f. For \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, Q it gives O(N^{-2}), hence almost sure convergence.
  • The scheme: identity, integration by parts, variance bound, limit equation, choice of the root. It gives the semicircle law here and the Marchenko–Pastur law in ch. 3.

3. The Marchenko–Pastur theorem

The sample covariance matrix is the basic object of multivariate statistics. When the dimension N and the sample size n grow together, its spectrum does not concentrate at the population eigenvalues: it spreads over an interval whose shape the Marchenko–Pastur theorem gives.

Statement

The data are n independent vectors x_1, \dots, x_n of \mathbb{R}^N with independent entries of mean 0 and variance 1, so that the population covariance is I_N.

As in ch. 2, X = [x_1, \dots, x_n] and \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X X^\top. We write c_n = N/n and assume N, n \to \infty, \qquad c_n \to c \in (0, \infty) .

Theorem 3.1 — Marchenko–Pastur (1967) Almost surely, \mu_\Sigma converges weakly to the Marchenko–Pastur law \mu_c, whose Stieltjes transform m_c is, for each z \in \mathbb{C}^+, the unique solution in \mathbb{C}^+ of c z\, m^2 + (z - 1 + c)\, m + 1 = 0 . Equivalently, m = 1/(1 - c - z - c z m).

Proof. We prove it for Gaussian entries; ch. 13 gives the general argument. Let Q = (\Sigma - z)^{-1}, m_N = \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, Q and \bar m_N = \mathbb{E}\, m_N.

  1. Identity. Q \Sigma = I + z Q, so \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma) = 1 + z\, m_N.

  2. Integration by parts. (Q\Sigma)_{ii} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_j (Q X)_{ij} X_{ij}. Lemma 2.1 and Proposition 2.3 give \mathbb{E}\big[X_{ij} (QX)_{ij}\big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[ Q_{ii} - \frac{1}{n} Q_{ii}\, x_j^\top Q x_j - \frac{1}{n} (Q x_j)_i^2 \Big] .

  3. Averaging. Sum over j, divide by n, then average over i. Using \frac{1}{n} \sum_j x_j^\top Q x_j = \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma): \mathbb{E}\, \tfrac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}(Q\Sigma) = \bar m_N - c_n\, \mathbb{E}\big[m_N\, \tfrac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}(Q\Sigma)\big] - \tfrac{1}{n}\, \mathbb{E}\, \tfrac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma Q) .

  4. Closing. Insert step 1. The last term is O(1/n), because Q \Sigma Q = Q + z Q^2 has norm at most 1/v + |z|/v^2. Proposition 2.4 replaces \mathbb{E}\, m_N^2 by \bar m_N^2 up to O(n^{-2}): c_n z\, \bar m_N^2 + (z - 1 + c_n)\, \bar m_N + 1 = O\big(n^{-1}\big) .

  5. Identification. By Montel’s theorem, every limit point m of \bar m_N is analytic, solves the limit equation, and satisfies \Im m \geq 0 and |m| \leq 1/v.

  6. Uniqueness. Two distinct solutions m_1, m_2 of the equation m = 1/(1 - c - z - czm) satisfy c z\, m_1 m_2 = 1.

    On z = iy with y > c, the bound |m_k| \leq 1/y gives |c z\, m_1 m_2| \leq c/y < 1. So the limit point is unique there, and everywhere by analyticity.

  7. Conclusion. \bar m_N \to m_c, Proposition 2.4 transfers the convergence to m_N almost surely, and Theorem 1.5 concludes. ∎

Key formula — the Marchenko–Pastur equation m_c(z) = \frac{1}{1 - c - z - c z\, m_c(z)}

The law itself

Proposition 3.2 — Density, edges and moments Let a = (1 - \sqrt{c})^2 and b = (1 + \sqrt{c})^2. Then \mu_c(dx) = \Big(1 - \frac{1}{c}\Big)^+ \delta_0 + \frac{\sqrt{(b - x)(x - a)}}{2\pi c\, x}\, \mathbf{1}_{[a, b]}(x)\, dx . Its mean is 1 and its second moment is 1 + c. More generally, its k-th moment is a Narayana polynomial: \int x^k\, \mu_c(dx) = \sum_{r=0}^{k-1} \frac{1}{r + 1} \binom{k}{r} \binom{k - 1}{r}\, c^r .

Proof. The root with positive imaginary part is m_c(z) = \frac{1 - c - z + \sqrt{(z - 1 - c)^2 - 4c}}{2 c z} , with the branch that makes m_c(z) \sim -1/z at infinity. Note that (z - 1 - c)^2 - 4c = (z - a)(z - b).

  • Density. For x \in (a, b), (x - a)(x - b) < 0, and \frac{1}{\pi} \Im\, m_c(x + i0) is the announced density (Theorem 1.4).
  • Atom. At z \to 0, z\, m_c(z) \to -(1 - 1/c)^+, which is minus the mass at 0. When c > 1, \Sigma has rank n < N and N - n zero eigenvalues.
  • Moments. For large |z|, m_c(z) = -\sum_k z^{-k-1} \int x^k d\mu_c. Inserting this series into the equation gives a recursion for the moments, whose solution is the Narayana formula. The moment method of ch. 13 gives a combinatorial proof. ∎

The bulk is not centred at 1 in a symmetric way: the density is larger on the left, and for c > 1 a fraction 1 - 1/c of the eigenvalues is exactly 0. The data-dependent spread has a simple first consequence: \Sigma is badly conditioned even when the population covariance is the identity.

Example 3.3 — Numbers for c = 0.5 With N = 500 and n = 1000:

  • edges a = (1 - 0.707)^2 = 0.086 and b = (1 + 0.707)^2 = 2.914;
  • density at x = 1: (b - 1)(1 - a) = 1.914 \times 0.914 = 1.749, so the density is \sqrt{1.749}/\pi = 0.421;
  • asymptotic condition number b/a = 34.0, against 34.9 in the simulation of ch. 0;
  • second moment 1 + c = 1.5: the average squared eigenvalue exceeds the squared average by c.

Extreme eigenvalues

Weak convergence says nothing about a vanishing fraction of eigenvalues. A separate theorem controls the extreme ones, and ch. 6 and ch. 7 rely on it.

Theorem 3.4 — No eigenvalue outside the bulk If the entries are independent and identically distributed, with mean 0, variance 1 and finite fourth moment, then almost surely \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma) \to (1 + \sqrt{c})^2 , and, if c < 1, \lambda_{\min}(\Sigma) \to (1 - \sqrt{c})^2.

The first statement is due to Geman (1980) under stronger moment conditions and to Yin, Bai and Krishnaiah (1988); the second to Bai and Yin (1993). The proofs use the moment method with moments of order k \sim \log N, as in ch. 13. Bai and Silverstein (1998) extend it: no eigenvalue lies outside the support of the limit law of ch. 4.

Ch. 13 shows how the finite fourth moment enters, and why without it the largest eigenvalue can escape to infinity while the ESD still converges.

The code checks the law, its first two moments and its universality: Gaussian and \pm 1 entries give the same limit.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

def mp_cdf_factory(c):
    a, b = (1 - c**0.5) ** 2, (1 + c**0.5) ** 2
    dens = lambda x: np.sqrt(max((b - x) * (x - a), 0)) / (2 * np.pi * c * x)
    atom = max(1 - 1 / c, 0)
    def cdf(x):
        if x < a:
            return atom if x >= 0 else 0.0
        return atom + quad(dens, a, min(x, b))[0]
    return cdf

for N, n in [(300, 1200), (600, 1200), (1200, 600)]:
    c = N / n
    X = rng.standard_normal((N, n))            # Gaussian entries
    B = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(N, n))   # Rademacher entries
    cdf = mp_cdf_factory(c)
    for name, Z in [("gauss", X), ("rademacher", B)]:
        lam = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(Z @ Z.T / n))
        grid = np.linspace(1e-9, (1 + c**0.5) ** 2 + 0.2, 200)
        emp = np.searchsorted(lam, grid, side="right") / N
        ks = max(abs(e - cdf(g)) for e, g in zip(emp, grid))
        print(f"c={c:.2f} {name:10s}: mean {lam.mean():.3f} (1), "
              f"2nd moment {np.mean(lam**2):.3f} ({1 + c:.2f}), sup|F_N - F| = {ks:.3f}")

In all six cases the Kolmogorov distance to \mu_c is below 0.01, and the second moments match 1 + c within 0.005, including c = 2 with its atom at 0.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • For white data with N/n \to c, the ESD of the sample covariance tends to \mu_c, carried by [(1 - \sqrt{c})^2, (1 + \sqrt{c})^2], plus an atom at 0 when c > 1.
  • Its Stieltjes transform solves c z m^2 + (z - 1 + c) m + 1 = 0; the Gaussian proof is integration by parts plus a variance bound.
  • The extreme eigenvalues converge to the edges under a fourth moment, so the sample covariance is ill-conditioned even for white data.

4. Structured models and deterministic equivalents

Real data are not white. This chapter treats the three structured models of the official syllabus: a population covariance matrix, a variance profile, and a deterministic signal plus noise. In each case the limit spectrum is described by a fixed-point equation, and a deterministic equivalent replaces the random resolvent by a deterministic matrix.

Population covariance matrix

The observations are y_j = C^{1/2} x_j, where the x_j are as in ch. 3 and C is a deterministic positive semidefinite N \times N matrix with bounded norm. The sample covariance is \hat C = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n y_j y_j^* = \frac{1}{n}\, C^{1/2} X X^* C^{1/2} .

Theorem 4.1 — Deterministic equivalent for the sample covariance For z \in \mathbb{C}^+, let \delta(z) be the unique solution in \mathbb{C}^+ of the fixed-point equation below, and \bar Q(z) the matrix it defines: \delta(z) = \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C\, \bar Q(z), \qquad \bar Q(z) = \Big( \frac{C}{1 + \delta(z)} - z I_N \Big)^{-1} . Then Q(z) = (\hat C - z)^{-1} is close to \bar Q(z) in the following sense: for deterministic A of bounded norm and unit vectors a, b, almost surely, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, A\, \big(Q(z) - \bar Q(z)\big) \to 0, \qquad a^* \big(Q(z) - \bar Q(z)\big)\, b \to 0 .

Proof. Sketch of the key step. Write Q_{-j} for the resolvent without the j-th sample. By Sherman–Morrison (Proposition 0.4), Q\, y_j = \frac{Q_{-j}\, y_j}{1 + \frac{1}{n} y_j^* Q_{-j}\, y_j} .

  • y_j is independent of Q_{-j}, so the trace lemma (Proposition 0.5) replaces \frac{1}{n} y_j^* Q_{-j} y_j by \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C Q_{-j}.
  • A rank-one update changes \mathrm{tr}\, C Q by at most \|C\| / v, as in Proposition 1.6, so the index can be removed. The variance bound of ch. 2 then replaces the trace by its mean: \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C Q_{-j} \approx \delta_n = \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C\, \mathbb{E}[Q] , uniformly in j.
  • Then I + z Q = Q \hat C = \frac{1}{n} \sum_j Q y_j y_j^*, and taking expectations gives I + z\, \mathbb{E} Q \approx \frac{\mathbb{E}[Q]\, C}{1 + \delta_n} .

So \mathbb{E}[Q] \approx (\frac{C}{1 + \delta_n} - z)^{-1}. Multiplying by C and taking \frac{1}{n}\mathrm{tr} shows that \delta_n solves the fixed-point equation up to a vanishing error. The stability of that equation then gives \delta_n - \delta \to 0, hence \mathbb{E}[Q] \approx \bar Q.

The error terms are controlled in Couillet and Liao (2022, ch. 2); for Gaussian data the method of ch. 3 applies verbatim. ∎

With C = I, \bar Q = m I with \delta = c m, and the equation becomes the Marchenko–Pastur equation of Theorem 3.1. The word “equivalent” matters: when the spectrum of C has no limit, \bar Q has no limit either, but Q - \bar Q still vanishes in the sense of the theorem.

When the ESD of C converges to a law H, the limit is described by the companion transform. Let \underline{m} be the Stieltjes transform of the limit ESD of the n \times n matrix \frac{1}{n} Y^* Y, which has the same non-zero eigenvalues as \hat C.

Proposition 4.2 — Silverstein’s equation The limit ESD F of \hat C and its companion satisfy \underline{m}(z) = c\, m_F(z) - \frac{1 - c}{z}, \qquad z = -\frac{1}{\underline{m}} + c \int \frac{t\, H(dt)}{1 + t\, \underline{m}} . The second relation gives z as an explicit function of \underline m.

Proof. The deterministic equivalent of the co-resolvent (\frac{1}{n} Y^* Y - z)^{-1} is -\frac{1}{z(1 + \delta)} I_n, so \underline m = -1/(z(1 + \delta)). Substitute \frac{C}{1 + \delta} = -z \underline m\, C in the definition of \delta: \delta = -\frac{1}{z}\, \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C (I + \underline m\, C)^{-1} . Then replace \delta by -1/(z \underline m) - 1 and pass to the limit. ∎

This is the equation of Marchenko and Pastur (1967) in the form of Silverstein and Bai (1995). Its explicit inverse gives the support.

Proposition 4.3 — The support (Silverstein and Choi, 1995) On the real set where \underline m \neq 0 and -1/\underline m is outside the support of H, define x(u) = -\frac{1}{u} + c \int \frac{t\, H(dt)}{1 + t u} . A real x \neq 0 lies outside the support of F if and only if x = x(u) for some u with x'(u) > 0.

The edges of the support are therefore the values of x(u) at the critical points x'(u) = 0.

When H has several atoms and c is small, the support splits into clusters, one per population eigenvalue. Bai and Silverstein (1999) show that the sample eigenvalues then split exactly as the population ones do. This separation condition is what ch. 9 needs to estimate each population eigenvalue.

Example 4.4 — Two population eigenvalues Take H = \frac{1}{2}\delta_1 + \frac{1}{2}\delta_4 and c = 0.1. The critical points of x(u) give the support [0.555,\ 1.408] \cup [2.485,\ 6.052] . The two clusters are separated, half of the sample eigenvalues lie below 2, and each cluster is wider than its population value: the eigenvalue 4 spreads over an interval of length 3.6.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
N, n = 1000, 10000                    # c = N/n = 0.1
c = N / n
t, w = np.array([1.0, 4.0]), np.array([0.5, 0.5])   # population spectrum H
pop = np.repeat(t, N // 2)

def det_equiv(z, iters=500):
    """Fixed point delta = (1/n) tr C (C/(1+delta) - z)^{-1}; returns (1/N) tr Qbar(z)."""
    delta = 1.0 + 0j
    for _ in range(iters):
        delta = 0.5 * delta + 0.5 * np.sum(pop / (pop / (1 + delta) - z)) / n
    return np.mean(1 / (pop / (1 + delta) - z))

def support_edges(c, t, w):
    """Edges of the limit support: x(u) = -1/u + c sum w t/(1+tu) is increasing off the support."""
    u = -np.logspace(-3, 3, 200000)
    x = -1 / u + c * np.sum(w * t / (1 + np.outer(u, t)), axis=1)
    dx = 1 / u**2 - c * np.sum(w * t**2 / (1 + np.outer(u, t)) ** 2, axis=1)
    flips = np.nonzero(np.diff(dx > 0))[0]
    return np.sort(x[flips])

Y = np.sqrt(pop)[:, None] * rng.standard_normal((N, n))   # columns ~ N(0, C)
lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Y @ Y.T / n)
for z in [0.5 + 0.1j, 2.0 + 0.1j, 4.0 + 0.1j]:
    print(f"z={z}: m_N(z) = {np.mean(1 / (lam - z)):.4f}, deterministic = {det_equiv(z):.4f}")
print("support edges (theory):", np.round(support_edges(c, t, w), 3))
print(f"sample eigenvalue range: [{lam.min():.3f}, {lam.max():.3f}], "
      f"fraction below 2: {np.mean(lam < 2):.3f}")

The empirical and deterministic Stieltjes transforms agree to 5 \times 10^{-4} at z = 2 + 0.1i, in the gap, and to within 10^{-2} inside the clusters, where \Im z = 0.1 resolves fewer eigenvalues. The sample eigenvalues range over [0.552, 5.997], inside the predicted support up to the edge fluctuations of ch. 7.

Variance profile

In a wireless channel or a bipartite network, each entry may have its own variance. Let Y_{ij} = \frac{\sigma_{ij}}{\sqrt{n}} X_{ij} with bounded deterministic \sigma_{ij} and standardized independent X_{ij}.

Theorem 4.5 — Variance profile (Girko; Hachem, Loubaton and Najim, 2007) For z \in \mathbb{C}^+, the system below has a unique solution (t_i(z))_{i \leq N}, (\tilde t_j(z))_{j \leq n} of Stieltjes transforms of probability measures on \mathbb{R}^+: t_i(z) = \frac{-1}{z \big(1 + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j} \sigma_{ij}^2\, \tilde t_j(z)\big)}, \qquad \tilde t_j(z) = \frac{-1}{z \big(1 + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i} \sigma_{ij}^2\, t_i(z)\big)} . Almost surely, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\,(YY^* - z)^{-1} - \frac{1}{N} \sum_i t_i(z) \to 0 .

With \sigma_{ij} = 1, t_i = m_c and \tilde t_j = \underline m: the system collapses to the Marchenko–Pastur equation. When \sigma_{ij}^2 = d_i depends on the row only, it is the population model with C = \mathrm{diag}(d_i) (Exercise 11). The proof follows the scheme of Theorem 3.1, with one equation per row and one per column; Hachem, Loubaton and Najim (2007) also give the deterministic equivalents of the mutual information used in ch. 5.

Signal plus noise

In the information-plus-noise model, a deterministic signal matrix A is observed in white noise: \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} (A + \sigma X)(A + \sigma X)^* . Its columns are not centred, so it is not a sample covariance matrix, but it is the right model for deterministic sources in ch. 8–10.

Theorem 4.6 — Information plus noise (Dozier and Silverstein, 2007) If the ESD of \frac{1}{n} A A^* converges to H and N/n \to c, then the ESD of \Sigma converges almost surely to a law F whose Stieltjes transform is the unique solution in \mathbb{C}^+ of m = \int \frac{H(dt)}{\dfrac{t}{1 + \sigma^2 c\, m} - (1 + \sigma^2 c\, m)\, z + \sigma^2 (1 - c)} .

With A = 0 and \sigma = 1 the equation is again the Marchenko–Pastur equation. The three models share one structure: a self-consistent equation for a few scalar functions, solved numerically by damped fixed-point iterations, as in det_equiv above.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Population covariance: Q(z) \approx \bar Q(z) = (\frac{C}{1 + \delta} - z)^{-1} with \delta = \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C \bar Q; equivalently Silverstein’s equation for \underline m.
  • The inverse map x(\underline m) gives the support; the clusters separate when c is small enough.
  • Variance profiles and signal-plus-noise matrices have their own fixed-point systems, all reducing to Marchenko–Pastur in the white case.

5. Multi-antenna (MIMO) channels: mutual information and ergodic capacity

A link with n transmit and N receive antennas carries more information than a single-antenna link, and the question is how much more. The mutual information is a log-determinant of a random matrix, so its large-system limit is a functional of the spectra of ch. 3 and ch. 4.

Definition 5.1 — MIMO channel A narrow-band MIMO channel maps the transmitted vector x \in \mathbb{C}^n to y = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{n}}\, H x + w , where H is the N \times n channel matrix, known to the receiver, w \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, I_N) is the noise, and \rho is the signal-to-noise ratio.

In the Rayleigh model, H has independent \mathcal{CN}(0, 1) entries; in the correlated model, H = R^{1/2} X with a receive correlation matrix R.

Proposition 5.2 — Mutual information with Gaussian input With x \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, I_n) independent of w, the mutual information between x and y, given H, is, in nats per channel use, I_N(\rho) = \log \det\Big( I_N + \frac{\rho}{n} H H^* \Big) = \sum_{k=1}^N \log\big(1 + \rho\, \lambda_k\big) , where \lambda_k are the eigenvalues of \frac{1}{n} H H^*.

Proof. I(x; y) = h(y) - h(y \mid x) = h(y) - h(w). Given H, y is circular Gaussian with covariance I_N + \frac{\rho}{n} H H^*, and a circular Gaussian vector with covariance K has entropy \log \det(\pi e K). The difference is the log-determinant. ∎

When the transmitter does not know H, Telatar (1999) shows that for Rayleigh fading the isotropic Gaussian input is optimal. The ergodic capacity is then the average \mathbb{E}\, I_N(\rho) over the fading, the rate achievable by coding over many independent channel realizations.

The large-system limit

By Proposition 5.2, \frac{1}{N} I_N(\rho) is the integral of \log(1 + \rho \lambda) against the ESD of \frac{1}{n} HH^*, and the integrand is unbounded only logarithmically. Theorem 3.1 therefore gives the limit of the capacity per receive antenna, and the deterministic equivalent gives it in closed form.

Theorem 5.3 — Deterministic equivalent of the mutual information Let H = R^{1/2} X, with X standard complex Gaussian and the eigenvalues r_1, \dots, r_N of R bounded. Let \delta > 0 be the unique positive solution of \delta = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{\rho\, r_i\, (1 + \delta)}{\rho\, r_i + 1 + \delta} , and define the deterministic quantity \bar I_N(\rho) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log\Big(1 + \frac{\rho\, r_i}{1 + \delta}\Big) + n \log(1 + \delta) - \frac{n\, \delta}{1 + \delta} . Then \mathbb{E}\, I_N(\rho) - \bar I_N(\rho) \to 0, without normalization by N.

Proof. Sketch. Write s = 1/\rho; then I_N = \log\det(\hat C + s I) - N \log s with \hat C = \frac{1}{n} H H^*.

  1. Derivative. Differentiating in s, \frac{d}{ds} I_N = \mathrm{tr}\, (\hat C + s)^{-1} - \frac{N}{s} = \mathrm{tr}\, Q(-s) - \frac{N}{s} , and Theorem 4.1 replaces Q(-s) by \bar Q(-s).
  2. Envelope. Define \Phi(\delta, s) = \bar I_N as a function of both variables. A direct computation shows that \partial \Phi / \partial \delta = 0 exactly at the fixed point, and that \partial \Phi / \partial s = \mathrm{tr}\, \bar Q(-s) - N/s.
  3. Integration. Both sides tend to 0 as s \to \infty; integrating the derivatives from s to \infty gives the claim.

The rate O(1/N) of the error is proved by Hachem, Khorunzhiy, Loubaton, Najim and Pastur (2008) with integration by parts and the Poincaré–Nash inequality. ∎

For R = I and c = N/n the limit per receive antenna is the Shannon transform of the Marchenko–Pastur law: \frac{1}{N}\, \bar I_N(\rho) = \log\Big(1 + \frac{\rho}{1 + \delta}\Big) + \frac{1}{c} \log(1 + \delta) - \frac{\delta}{c\, (1 + \delta)} = \int \log(1 + \rho \lambda)\, \mu_c(d\lambda) .

Key result — linear growth of the capacity \mathbb{E}\, I_N(\rho) \approx N\, \mathcal{V}_c(\rho), \qquad \mathcal{V}_c(\rho) = \int \log(1 + \rho \lambda)\, \mu_c(d\lambda)

Example 5.4 — An 8 × 8 link at 10 dB For N = n (c = 1) and \rho = 10, the fixed point is \delta = 2.7016, and \mathcal{V}_1(10) = 1.888 \text{ nats} = 2.72 \text{ bits per receive antenna} . An 8 \times 8 link therefore carries about 21.8 bits per channel use, against 2.9 bits for a single Rayleigh antenna at the same total power: the capacity grows linearly in the number of antennas.

Foschini and Gans (1998) and Telatar (1999) observed this linear growth; the closed form of the Shannon transform of \mu_c appears in Verdú and Shamai (1999), for random spreading in CDMA.

At high SNR, \mathcal{V}_c(\rho) \approx \min(1, 1/c) \log \rho up to a constant: the slope \min(N, n) of I_N in \log \rho is the multiplexing gain. Receive correlation lowers the constant but not the slope as long as R is invertible.

The code compares Monte Carlo averages with Theorem 5.3, for independent and correlated antennas (R_{ij} = r^{|i - j|}), down to N = n = 2:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)

def det_equiv_mi(R, n, rho, iters=500):
    """Deterministic equivalent of E log det(I + rho/n R^{1/2} X X^* R^{1/2}), in nats."""
    N, s2 = R.shape[0], 1 / rho
    ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(R)
    d = 1.0
    for _ in range(iters):
        d = 0.5 * d + 0.5 * np.sum(ev / (ev / (1 + d) + s2)) / n
    return (np.sum(np.log(1 + ev / (s2 * (1 + d))))
            + n * np.log(1 + d) - n * d / (1 + d))

def monte_carlo_mi(R, n, rho, reps=4000):
    N = R.shape[0]
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(R)
    out = []
    for _ in range(reps):
        X = (rng.standard_normal((N, n)) + 1j * rng.standard_normal((N, n))) / np.sqrt(2)
        H = L @ X
        out.append(np.linalg.slogdet(np.eye(N) + rho / n * H @ H.conj().T)[1])
    return np.mean(out), np.std(out)

rho = 10.0                                   # SNR of 10 dB
for N in [2, 4, 8]:
    for r in [0.0, 0.8]:                     # receive correlation R_ij = r^|i-j|
        R = r ** np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(N), np.arange(N)))
        mc, sd = monte_carlo_mi(R, N, rho)
        de = det_equiv_mi(R, N, rho)
        print(f"N=n={N}, r={r}: Monte Carlo {mc / np.log(2):6.3f} bits "
              f"(sd {sd / np.log(2):.2f}), deterministic equivalent {de / np.log(2):6.3f} bits")

Even for N = n = 2 the error is below 0.1 bit, and for N = n = 8 it is about 0.015 bit. The standard deviation of I_N stays between 1.0 and 1.3 bits whatever N: the mutual information fluctuates by O(1), as Proposition 2.4 suggests, and ch. 13 gives the Gaussian limit that predicts outage probabilities.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • With Gaussian input, the mutual information of a MIMO channel is \log\det(I + \frac{\rho}{n} H H^*), a linear spectral statistic.
  • Its deterministic equivalent solves one scalar fixed-point equation, is accurate to O(1/N), and shows a capacity growing linearly with the number of antennas.
  • Correlation between antennas lowers the capacity through the eigenvalues of R only.

6. Spiked models: outliers and eigenvectors

A low-rank signal does not change the limit spectrum (Proposition 1.6), but it can push a few eigenvalues out of the bulk. Whether it does depends on its strength, with a sharp threshold. This phase transition decides whether principal component analysis, detection or clustering can see a signal at all.

Definition 6.1 — Spiked models Let r be fixed, \ell_1 > \dots > \ell_r > 0 and u_1, \dots, u_r orthonormal in \mathbb{R}^N.

  • Spiked covariance (Johnstone, 2001): the population covariance is C = I_N + \sum_{k} \ell_k u_k u_k^\top, and \hat C = \frac{1}{n} C^{1/2} X X^\top C^{1/2}.
  • Spiked Wigner: W + \theta\, v v^\top, with W as in Theorem 2.5, \theta > 0 and \|v\| = 1.

The \ell_k and \theta are the spikes. Their eigenvectors are the signal to be recovered.

The proofs reduce the location of an outlier to a scalar equation involving a quadratic form of the resolvent. The key input is that such forms behave like normalized traces.

Proposition 6.2 — Isotropic quadratic forms Let \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X X^\top with Gaussian X, and u a deterministic unit vector.

Let z be at a fixed positive distance from the support of \mu_c, that is [a, b], plus the point 0 when c > 1. Almost surely, u^\top Q(z)\, u - m_c(z) \to 0 . The same holds for W of Theorem 2.5, with m_{\mathrm{sc}} and [-2, 2].

Proof. For every orthogonal O, O X has the law of X, so O Q O^\top has the law of Q.

Hence u^\top Q u has the law of o^\top Q o, with o uniform on the sphere and independent of Q.

Write o = g / \|g\| with g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_N). Conditionally on Q, Proposition 0.5 gives \frac{1}{N} g^\top Q g - \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, Q \to 0, and \|g\|^2 / N \to 1.

By Theorem 3.4 (and its Wigner analogue), \|Q(z)\| stays bounded for z away from the support, so these bounds hold uniformly. Theorem 3.1 gives \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, Q \to m_c.

The equality in law gives convergence in probability. For the almost sure statement, use the fourth moment instead of the variance in Proposition 0.5: it is of order N^{-2} for the normalized form, which is summable, and Borel–Cantelli concludes. ∎

The spiked Wigner model

Theorem 6.3 — Outlier and eigenvector of a spiked Wigner matrix Let \hat\lambda and \hat v be the top eigenvalue and a unit top eigenvector of W + \theta v v^\top. Almost surely: \hat\lambda \to \begin{cases} \theta + 1/\theta & \text{if } \theta > 1, \\ 2 & \text{if } \theta \leq 1, \end{cases} \qquad |\langle v, \hat v \rangle|^2 \to \begin{cases} 1 - 1/\theta^2 & \text{if } \theta > 1, \\ 0 & \text{if } \theta \leq 1. \end{cases}

Proof. Let \lambda > 2 be an eigenvalue of W + \theta v v^\top. By the Wigner analogue of Theorem 3.4 (ch. 13), it is not an eigenvalue of W for large N. Let R(\lambda) = (\lambda - W)^{-1}.

  1. Eigenvalue equation. The determinant lemma gives \det(\lambda - W - \theta v v^\top) = \det(\lambda - W)\,\big(1 - \theta\, v^\top R(\lambda) v\big) , so \lambda is an eigenvalue iff \theta\, v^\top R(\lambda)\, v = 1.
  2. Limit equation. By Proposition 6.2, v^\top R(\lambda) v \to -m_{\mathrm{sc}}(\lambda), which decreases from 1 to 0 on (2, \infty). The equation -m_{\mathrm{sc}}(\lambda) = 1/\theta has a solution iff \theta > 1.
  3. Location. Insert m = -1/\theta in m^2 + \lambda m + 1 = 0: \lambda = \theta + 1/\theta.
  4. Eigenvector. From (\lambda - W)\hat v = \theta \langle v, \hat v\rangle\, v, \hat v = \theta \langle v, \hat v \rangle\, R v. Normalizing, |\langle v, \hat v \rangle|^2 = \frac{1}{\theta^2\, v^\top R^2 v} .
  5. Derivative. Since R(\lambda)^2 = -\frac{d}{d\lambda} R(\lambda), v^\top R(\lambda)^2 v = -\frac{d}{d\lambda}\, v^\top R(\lambda) v \to m_{\mathrm{sc}}'(\lambda) . Differentiating m^2 + \lambda m + 1 = 0 at m = -1/\theta gives m_{\mathrm{sc}}' = 1/(\theta^2 - 1), hence the alignment 1 - 1/\theta^2.

For \theta \leq 1 there is no outlier, the top eigenvalue sticks to the edge 2, and the eigenvector is asymptotically orthogonal to v (Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2011). ∎

The spiked covariance model

Theorem 6.4 — Phase transition for sample covariance matrices In the spiked covariance model with N/n \to c, for each k \leq r, almost surely, \hat\lambda_k \to \begin{cases} (1 + \ell_k)\Big(1 + \dfrac{c}{\ell_k}\Big) & \text{if } \ell_k > \sqrt{c}, \\ (1 + \sqrt{c})^2 & \text{if } \ell_k \leq \sqrt{c}, \end{cases} and the k-th sample eigenvector \hat u_k satisfies |\langle \hat u_k, u_k \rangle|^2 \to \zeta(\ell_k) = \frac{1 - c/\ell_k^2}{1 + c/\ell_k} \quad \text{if } \ell_k > \sqrt{c}, \qquad 0 \text{ otherwise} , with \langle \hat u_k, u_j \rangle \to 0 for j \neq k. These results are due to Baik, Ben Arous and Péché (2005), Baik and Silverstein (2006) and Paul (2007).

Proof. Eigenvalues, for r = 1. Write \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X X^\top and C = I + \ell u u^\top. The matrix \hat C has the eigenvalues of \Sigma C.

  1. For \lambda not an eigenvalue of \Sigma, the determinant lemma gives \det(\Sigma C - \lambda) = \det(\Sigma - \lambda)\, \big(1 + \ell\, u^\top (\Sigma - \lambda)^{-1} \Sigma u\big) .
  2. With (\Sigma - \lambda)^{-1}\Sigma = I + \lambda Q(\lambda), the outliers solve 1 + \ell\,\big(1 + \lambda\, u^\top Q(\lambda) u\big) = 0.
  3. Proposition 6.2 replaces u^\top Q u by m_c(\lambda). The limit equation is m_c(\lambda) = -\frac{1 + \ell}{\ell \lambda}.
  4. Inserting it into the Marchenko–Pastur equation and simplifying gives \lambda = (1 + \ell)(1 + c/\ell).
  5. This value is the correct branch of m_c, the one that tends to 0 at infinity, exactly when \ell > \sqrt{c}. The map \ell \mapsto (1 + \ell)(1 + c/\ell) reaches its minimum (1 + \sqrt c)^2 = b at \ell = \sqrt{c}.

Eigenvectors. The alignment follows from a contour integral of u^\top (\hat C - z)^{-1} u around the outlier; see Paul (2007) for Gaussian data and Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi (2011) in general. ∎

Key result — the BBP phase transition \ell > \sqrt{c} \iff \text{the spike creates an outlier at } (1 + \ell)(1 + c/\ell)

Two consequences matter for every application of the course.

  • Below the threshold, the top sample eigenvector carries asymptotically no information about u: no method based on it can estimate the signal direction.
  • Above it, the sample eigenvalue overestimates 1 + \ell and the eigenvector is only partly aligned. Both biases are known functions of \ell and c, so they can be inverted.

The inversion is explicit: \ell + c/\ell = \hat\lambda - 1 - c, and the larger root is \hat\ell = \frac{(\hat\lambda - 1 - c) + \sqrt{(\hat\lambda - 1 - c)^2 - 4c}}{2} . Ch. 10 uses it to correct the MUSIC algorithm.

Example 6.5 — Numbers for c = 0.5 The threshold is \sqrt{0.5} = 0.707.

  • \ell = 2: the outlier is at 3 \times 1.25 = 3.75 instead of 1 + \ell = 3, and \zeta = 0.875 / 1.25 = 0.70.
  • \ell = 1: the outlier is at 2 \times 1.5 = 3.0, barely above the edge 2.914, and \zeta = 0.5/1.5 = 0.33.
  • \ell = 0.6: no outlier. A spike that raises the variance by 60% in one direction is invisible to PCA with n = 2N samples.
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N, n = 400, 800
c = N / n                                   # threshold sqrt(c) = 0.707
u = np.zeros(N); u[0] = 1.0                 # spike direction

print(" ell   lambda_max  theory   |<u,u_hat>|^2  theory")
for ell in [0.3, 0.6, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0]:
    lam_max, align = [], []
    for _ in range(20):
        Y = rng.standard_normal((N, n))
        Y[0] *= np.sqrt(1 + ell)            # population covariance I + ell u u^T
        lam, vec = np.linalg.eigh(Y @ Y.T / n)
        lam_max.append(lam[-1]); align.append((vec[:, -1] @ u) ** 2)
    if ell > np.sqrt(c):
        th_l, th_a = (1 + ell) * (1 + c / ell), (1 - c / ell**2) / (1 + c / ell)
    else:
        th_l, th_a = (1 + np.sqrt(c)) ** 2, 0.0
    print(f"{ell:4.1f}   {np.mean(lam_max):9.3f}  {th_l:6.3f}   {np.mean(align):12.3f}  {th_a:6.3f}")

Above the threshold the averages match the limits within 0.07 for the eigenvalues and 0.02 for the alignments. Near it (\ell = 0.6 and 1.0) the finite-size effects are visible: the alignment is 0.05 instead of 0 and 0.35 instead of 0.33, because the transition is smoothed over a window of width of order N^{-1/3}.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • A spike \ell produces an outlier iff \ell > \sqrt{c}, at (1 + \ell)(1 + c/\ell); the eigenvector alignment is \frac{1 - c/\ell^2}{1 + c/\ell}.
  • For a spiked Wigner matrix, the threshold is \theta = 1, the outlier \theta + 1/\theta, the alignment 1 - 1/\theta^2.
  • Proof pattern: determinant lemma, isotropic quadratic form, scalar equation in the Stieltjes transform.

7. The largest eigenvalue and the Tracy–Widom law

Tests and detectors compare the largest eigenvalue with a threshold, so they need its fluctuations, not only its limit. They are of order n^{-2/3}, much smaller than the n^{-1/2} of classical statistics, and their law is the Tracy–Widom distribution.

Definition 7.1 — Airy kernel and Tracy–Widom laws Let \mathrm{Ai} be the Airy function, the solution of y'' = x y that decays at +\infty. The Airy kernel is K_{\mathrm{Ai}}(x, y) = \frac{\mathrm{Ai}(x)\, \mathrm{Ai}'(y) - \mathrm{Ai}'(x)\, \mathrm{Ai}(y)}{x - y} . The Tracy–Widom law for complex matrices (\beta = 2) has distribution function given by a Fredholm determinant, or by the Hastings–McLeod solution q of Painlevé II, q'' = s q + 2 q^3 with q(s) \sim \mathrm{Ai}(s) at +\infty: F_2(s) = \det(I - K_{\mathrm{Ai}})_{L^2(s, \infty)} = \exp\Big( -\int_s^\infty (x - s)\, q(x)^2\, dx \Big) . For real matrices (\beta = 1), F_1(s) = \exp\Big( -\frac{1}{2} \int_s^\infty q(x)\, dx \Big)\, F_2(s)^{1/2} .

Tracy and Widom (1994, 1996) derived these laws for the Gaussian unitary and orthogonal ensembles. Their means are about -1.77 (\beta = 2) and -1.21 (\beta = 1), their standard deviations about 0.90 and 1.27.

Both laws are asymmetric, with a heavier right tail: their skewness is positive, about 0.22 for \beta = 2 and 0.29 for \beta = 1.

To leading order in the exponent, F_\beta(s) decays like e^{-\beta |s|^3/24} as s \to -\infty. On the right, 1 - F_\beta(s) decays only like e^{-\frac{2\beta}{3} s^{3/2}}.

Theorem 7.2 — Tracy–Widom limit for Wishart matrices (Johansson, 2000; Johnstone, 2001) Let X be N \times n with independent real \mathcal{N}(0, 1) entries and N/n \to c \in (0, \infty). With \mu_{Nn} = \big(\sqrt{n - 1} + \sqrt{N}\big)^2, \qquad \sigma_{Nn} = \big(\sqrt{n - 1} + \sqrt{N}\big) \Big( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n - 1}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \Big)^{1/3}, the largest eigenvalue of X X^\top satisfies \frac{\lambda_{\max}(X X^\top) - \mu_{Nn}}{\sigma_{Nn}} \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{D}\ } F_1 . For complex Gaussian entries, the same holds with n in place of n - 1 and F_2 in place of F_1 (Johansson, 2000).

The case N > n reduces to N \leq n through X^\top X, which has the same non-zero eigenvalues.

Exchanging the two dimensions in the centring changes it by O(1) only, which is negligible on the scale \sigma_{Nn}.

Since \sigma_{Nn} / n is of order n^{-2/3}, the largest eigenvalue of \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X X^\top is (1 + \sqrt{c})^2 + O(n^{-2/3}).

Proof. Structure of the proof, complex case.

  1. Joint density. For N \leq n, the eigenvalues of the complex Wishart matrix X X^* have the density, with respect to Lebesgue measure on (0, \infty)^N, p(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N) \propto \prod_{i < j} (\lambda_i - \lambda_j)^2 \prod_i \lambda_i^{n - N} e^{-\lambda_i} .
  2. Determinantal structure. Writing the Vandermonde product as a determinant of Laguerre polynomials, orthonormal for \lambda^{n-N} e^{-\lambda}, makes the eigenvalues a determinantal point process with a kernel K_N of Christoffel–Darboux form.
  3. Gap probability. \mathbb{P}(\lambda_{\max} \leq t) is the probability of no eigenvalue in (t, \infty), that is \det(I - K_N)_{L^2(t, \infty)}.
  4. Edge scaling. Near \mu_{Nn}, the Plancherel–Rotach asymptotics of Laguerre polynomials show that, in trace norm on (s, \infty), \sigma_{Nn}\, K_N\big(\mu_{Nn} + \sigma_{Nn} x,\ \mu_{Nn} + \sigma_{Nn} y\big) \to K_{\mathrm{Ai}}(x, y) . The Fredholm determinants converge.

The real case needs Pfaffians instead of determinants (Johnstone, 2001). ∎

The limit is universal. Soshnikov (2002) proved it for symmetric sub-Gaussian entries when n - N = O(N^{1/3}), Péché (2009) removed this restriction, and Pillai and Yin (2014) treated general entries with enough moments. El Karoui (2007) extended it to complex data with a general population covariance, with centring and scaling computed from the equation of ch. 4.

In the spiked model of ch. 6, the transition also changes the fluctuations. For complex Gaussian data, Baik, Ben Arous and Péché (2005) showed that the top eigenvalue keeps Tracy–Widom fluctuations below the threshold, and has Gaussian fluctuations of order n^{-1/2} above it.

Computing the law

The Fredholm determinant is the practical route. Bornemann (2010, Mathematics of Computation) showed that a Gauss–Legendre discretization with a few dozen nodes gives the distribution function to near machine precision, and his review of the same year tabulates the moments of F_1 and F_2. For \beta = 1, the formula of Ferrari and Spohn (2005) writes F_1(s) = \det(I - B_s)_{L^2(0, \infty)}, \qquad B_s(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}\, \mathrm{Ai}\Big(\frac{x + y}{2} + s\Big) . 

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import airy

# Tracy-Widom (beta = 1) CDF by Nystrom discretization of a Fredholm determinant
# (Ferrari-Spohn formula F1(s) = det(I - B_s) on L^2(0, inf), B_s(x, y) = Ai((x+y)/2 + s)/2)
xg, wg = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(60)
L = 12.0                                     # truncate (0, inf) to (0, L)
x, w = L * (xg + 1) / 2, L * wg / 2

def F1(s):
    B = 0.5 * airy((x[:, None] + x[None, :]) / 2 + s)[0]
    sw = np.sqrt(w)
    return np.linalg.det(np.eye(len(x)) - sw[:, None] * B * sw[None, :])

s = np.linspace(-8, 6, 1401)
F = np.array([F1(t) for t in s])
dens = np.gradient(F, s)
mean = np.trapezoid(s * dens, s)
sd = np.sqrt(np.trapezoid((s - mean) ** 2 * dens, s))
q95 = s[np.searchsorted(F, 0.95)]

8. Source detection in high dimension and spectrum sensing

A cognitive radio may use a frequency band only when the licensed (primary) user is silent. It must decide, from n samples received on N antennas, whether a source is present. With N and n of the same order and an unknown noise level, the tests of ch. 6 and ch. 7 are the right tools.

The test

Under the null hypothesis \mathcal{H}_0 the samples are noise; under \mathcal{H}_1 a single source with unknown channel h \in \mathbb{C}^N is present: \mathcal{H}_0 : y_t = \sigma w_t, \qquad \mathcal{H}_1 : y_t = h\, s_t + \sigma w_t, \qquad t = 1, \dots, n , with independent standard complex Gaussian s_t and w_t, and unknown \sigma^2. The population covariance is R = h h^* + \sigma^2 I under \mathcal{H}_1.

The signal-to-noise ratio is \rho = \|h\|^2 / \sigma^2, and the sample covariance is \hat R = \frac{1}{n} \sum_t y_t y_t^*.

Proposition 8.1 — The generalized likelihood ratio test The generalized likelihood ratio, with h and \sigma^2 replaced by their maximum likelihood estimates, is an increasing function of T_N = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(\hat R)}{\frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat R} on the range 1 < T_N < N. The GLRT rejects \mathcal{H}_0 when T_N exceeds a threshold.

Proof. The likelihood of n circular Gaussian samples is proportional to \det(R)^{-n} \exp(-n\, \mathrm{tr}\, R^{-1} \hat R).

  1. Under \mathcal{H}_0, R = \sigma^2 I and the maximum is reached at \hat\sigma_0^2 = \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat R, with value (\hat\sigma_0^2)^{-Nn} e^{-Nn}.
  2. Under \mathcal{H}_1, the maximum over R = h h^* + \sigma^2 I (a classical computation, detailed by Bianchi et al., 2011) is reached with \|h\|^2 + \sigma^2 = \hat\lambda_1, the top eigenvalue, and h along its eigenvector. The noise variance and the maximum value are then \hat\sigma_1^2 = \frac{\mathrm{tr}\, \hat R - \hat\lambda_1}{N - 1}, \qquad \max = \hat\lambda_1^{-n}\, (\hat\sigma_1^2)^{-(N-1)n}\, e^{-Nn} .
  3. Ratio. With T = \hat\lambda_1 / \hat\sigma_0^2, the n-th root of the ratio is L(T) = \frac{1}{T\, \big(1 - \frac{T - 1}{N - 1}\big)^{N-1}} . Its logarithmic derivative is -\frac{1}{T} + \frac{N - 1}{N - T}, positive when T > 1. ∎

T_N is pivotal: multiplying the data by a constant does not change it, so its law under \mathcal{H}_0 does not depend on \sigma^2, and the threshold can be set without knowing the noise level.

Theorem 8.2 — The GLRT in the high-dimensional regime (Bianchi, Debbah, Maïda and Najim, 2011) Let N/n \to c and \rho be fixed. Almost surely, T_N \to \begin{cases} (1 + \rho)\big(1 + \frac{c}{\rho}\big) & \text{under } \mathcal{H}_1 \text{ with } \rho > \sqrt{c}, \\ (1 + \sqrt{c})^2 & \text{under } \mathcal{H}_0, \text{ or } \mathcal{H}_1 \text{ with } \rho \leq \sqrt{c}. \end{cases} Under \mathcal{H}_0, the fluctuations of T_N around (1 + \sqrt{c_n})^2 are of order n^{-2/3} with the Tracy–Widom law F_2, the same as those of \lambda_{\max}(\hat R)/\sigma^2.

Proof. By pivotality take \sigma = 1. Under \mathcal{H}_1, R = I + \rho\, u u^* with u = h/\|h\|, a spiked model with \ell = \rho: Theorem 6.4 gives the limit of \lambda_{\max}(\hat R).

The denominator satisfies \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat R \to 1 under both hypotheses, since the spike adds only \rho/N.

Under \mathcal{H}_0, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat R - 1 is of order 1/N, a sum of Nn independent terms. It is negligible on the scale n^{-2/3}, so Theorem 7.2 applies to T_N. ∎

Three practical conclusions follow.

  • Threshold. For a false-alarm probability \alpha, reject when T_N > \mu_{Nn}/n + q_{1-\alpha}\, \sigma_{Nn}/n, with the Tracy–Widom quantile q_{1-\alpha} (real data use F_1, as in the code below).
  • Detectability. A source with \rho < \sqrt{c} is asymptotically undetectable by this test: its power tends to the level \alpha. With N = 50 antennas and n = 100 samples, the limit is \rho = 0.707, or -1.5 dB.
  • Noise uncertainty. The energy detector, which compares \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat R with a threshold computed from \sigma^2, is optimal when \sigma^2 is known. Under an error on \sigma^2, its false-alarm rate tends to 0 or 1: this fragility is behind the “SNR wall” of Tandra and Sahai (2008).

Other eigenvalue-based detectors use the ratio \hat\lambda_{\max} / \hat\lambda_{\min}, whose limit under \mathcal{H}_0 is (1 + \sqrt{c})^2 / (1 - \sqrt{c})^2 (Cardoso, Debbah, Bianchi and Najim, 2008; Zeng and Liang, 2009). Estimating the number of sources uses the same ingredients, one eigenvalue at a time (Nadakuditi and Edelman, 2008; Kritchman and Nadler, 2009).

The code compares the GLRT with an energy detector calibrated for \sigma^2 = 1, when the noise variance is exactly 1 and when it is 1.1, a 0.4 dB error:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
N, n = 50, 100                               # sensors, samples: c = 0.5
c = N / n
q95 = 0.98                                   # 95% quantile of TW1 (ch. 7)
mu = (np.sqrt(n - 1) + np.sqrt(N)) ** 2 / n
sig = (np.sqrt(n - 1) + np.sqrt(N)) * (1 / np.sqrt(n - 1) + 1 / np.sqrt(N)) ** (1 / 3) / n
thr_glrt = mu + q95 * sig                    # threshold on T = lambda_max / (tr R / N)
thr_energy = 1 + 1.645 * np.sqrt(2 / (N * n))  # energy test calibrated for sigma^2 = 1

def statistics(snr, noise_var=1.0):
    h = np.ones(N) * np.sqrt(snr * noise_var / N)          # ||h||^2 / sigma^2 = snr
    Y = np.outer(h, rng.standard_normal(n)) + np.sqrt(noise_var) * rng.standard_normal((N, n))
    R = Y @ Y.T / n
    return np.linalg.eigvalsh(R)[-1] / (np.trace(R) / N), np.trace(R) / N

for snr, nv in [(0.0, 1.0), (0.0, 1.1), (0.4, 1.0), (0.8, 1.0), (1.2, 1.0), (2.0, 1.0)]:
    T = np.array([statistics(snr, nv) for _ in range(1000)])
    print(f"snr={snr:.1f}, noise variance={nv}: P(GLRT detects) = {np.mean(T[:, 0] > thr_glrt):.3f}, "
          f"P(energy detects) = {np.mean(T[:, 1] > thr_energy):.3f}, mean T = {T[:, 0].mean():.3f}")
print(f"GLRT threshold {thr_glrt:.3f}; (1+sqrt c)^2 = {(1 + c**0.5)**2:.3f}; "
      f"detectability threshold sqrt(c) = {c**0.5:.3f}")

The GLRT keeps its false-alarm rate near 0.04 whether the noise variance is 1 or 1.1, while the energy detector jumps from 0.046 to 1.000. Below the threshold (\rho = 0.4) the GLRT detects in 5.6% of the runs, about its level. Above it, its power rises to 0.55 at \rho = 1.2 and 0.96 at \rho = 2, against 0.33 and 0.61 for the energy detector.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • The single-source GLRT with unknown noise level is a test on T_N = \lambda_{\max} / \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, \hat R, which does not depend on \sigma^2.
  • Its threshold comes from the Tracy–Widom law; a source is detectable asymptotically iff \rho > \sqrt{c}.
  • The energy detector is fragile: a small error on the noise level drives its false-alarm rate to 1.

9. G-estimation, power estimation and positioning

The spectrum of \hat C is a distorted image of the spectrum of C, and ch. 4 gives the distortion explicitly. G-estimation, introduced by Girko, inverts it: it builds estimators of population quantities that are consistent when N and n grow together. This chapter derives the main estimators and uses them to locate a transmitter from received powers.

Definition 9.1 — Consistency in the double limit Let \theta_N be a functional of the population covariance C, such as \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, C^2 or an eigenvalue of C.

An estimator \hat\theta built from \hat C is (N, n)-consistent, or a G-estimator, if \hat\theta - \theta_N \to 0 \quad \text{a.s. as } N, n \to \infty,\ N/n \to c \in (0, \infty) . Classical consistency, with N fixed and n \to \infty, is the case c = 0; it does not imply this one.

Two simple G-estimators

Proposition 9.2 — Corrected traces Let \hat C = \frac{1}{n} Y Y^\top with Gaussian columns y_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, C) and c_n = N/n.

  1. The estimator below is (N, n)-consistent for \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, C^2, while \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat C^2 overestimates it by c\, (\frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, C)^2: \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat C^2 - c_n \Big(\frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat C\Big)^2 .
  2. If c < 1, the estimator below is unbiased and (N, n)-consistent for \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, C^{-1}, while \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat C^{-1} overestimates it by a factor 1/(1 - c): \frac{n - N - 1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat C^{-1} .

Proof. For the Wishart matrix n \hat C, classical moment formulas give \mathbb{E}\, \hat C^2 = \frac{n + 1}{n}\, C^2 + \frac{1}{n}\, \mathrm{tr}(C)\, C, \qquad \mathbb{E}\, \hat C^{-1} = \frac{n}{n - N - 1}\, C^{-1} . Taking normalized traces gives the biases. The normalized traces have variances of order N^{-2} by Theorem 2.2, which gives almost sure convergence. For \hat C^{-1} the gradient involves \hat C^{-2}, and the bound uses Theorem 3.4: when c < 1, the smallest eigenvalue of \hat C stays away from 0. ∎

For C = I and c = 0.5, the naive estimate of \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, C^{-1} = 1 is close to 2: plugging \hat C^{-1} into a linear discriminant or a Mahalanobis distance doubles it.

Estimating population eigenvalues

Suppose that C has K distinct eigenvalues \gamma_1 < \dots < \gamma_K with known multiplicities N_1, \dots, N_K, N_k / N fixed, and c < 1.

Let \hat\lambda_1 \leq \dots \leq \hat\lambda_N be the sample eigenvalues, and \mathcal{N}_k the indices of the k-th group of N_k of them.

Theorem 9.3 — Mestre’s estimator (2008) Assume the separation condition: the cluster of the limit spectrum associated with \gamma_k is disjoint from the others (Proposition 4.3). Let \hat\mu_1 \leq \dots \leq \hat\mu_N be the eigenvalues of \mathrm{diag}(\hat\lambda) - \frac{1}{n}\, \sqrt{\hat\lambda}\, \sqrt{\hat\lambda}^\top , where \sqrt{\hat\lambda} is the vector of the \sqrt{\hat\lambda_m}. Then the estimator below is (N, n)-consistent for \gamma_k: \hat\gamma_k = \frac{n}{N_k} \sum_{m \in \mathcal{N}_k} \big(\hat\lambda_m - \hat\mu_m\big) .

Proof. Sketch.

  1. Cauchy’s formula. For a contour \Gamma_k enclosing \gamma_k only, \frac{N_k}{N}\, \gamma_k = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\Gamma_k} \omega\, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, (\omega I - C)^{-1}\, d\omega .
  2. Change of variable. Put \omega = -1/\underline m(z). The deterministic equivalent of Theorem 4.1 reads \bar Q(z) = \frac{-1}{z \underline m(z)} (C - \omega)^{-1}. Hence \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\,(\omega - C)^{-1} = z\, \underline m(z)\, m(z) .
  3. Contours. Under the separation condition, as z runs around the k-th cluster of the limit spectrum, \omega(z) runs around \gamma_k only (Proposition 4.3).
  4. Plug-in. Replacing m and \underline m by their empirical versions, which converge uniformly on the contour, gives the consistent estimator, with \mathcal{C}_k positively oriented: \hat\gamma_k = -\frac{N}{N_k}\, \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{\mathcal{C}_k} z\, \hat m(z)\, \frac{\underline{\hat m}'(z)}{\underline{\hat m}(z)^2}\, dz .
  5. Residues. The integrand has poles at the \hat\lambda_m and at the zeros of \underline{\hat m}. These zeros solve \sum_m \frac{1}{\hat\lambda_m - \mu} = \frac{n - N}{\mu}, so they are exactly the \hat\mu_m. The residues inside \mathcal{C}_k sum to the formula. ∎

The naive estimator, the average of the sample eigenvalues of the k-th cluster, is biased: the clusters spread asymmetrically (Example 4.4), and the largest population eigenvalue is overestimated while the smallest is underestimated.

Power estimation and positioning by trilateration

A sensor with N antennas observes a transmitter through an M-dimensional subspace. As a teaching model, take the received covariance R = \sigma^2 I_N + P\, U U^*, \qquad U^* U = I_M , where P is the received power. The population eigenvalues are \sigma^2 + P, with multiplicity M, and the noise level \sigma^2 for the rest. Theorem 9.3 estimates both, hence the power.

With a path-loss model P = P_0\, d^{-\alpha} and known P_0 and \alpha, the received power gives the distance d = (P_0 / P)^{1/\alpha}.

Three sensors at known positions a_1, a_2, a_3 then locate the transmitter x. Subtracting the first circle equation \|x - a_1\|^2 = d_1^2 from the others gives a linear system: 2\, (a_s - a_1)^\top x = d_1^2 - d_s^2 + \|a_s\|^2 - \|a_1\|^2, \qquad s = 2, 3 .

Couillet, Silverstein, Bai and Debbah (2011) treat the realistic version, where several sources with their own antennas and random channels share the band. Their estimator of each source power comes from the same contour-integral argument.

Example 9.4 — Locating a transmitter Sensors at (0, 0), (100, 0) and (0, 100) metres, transmitter at (40, 30), P_0 = 9000 and \alpha = 2, unit noise power.

  • The distances are 50.0, 67.1 and 80.6 m, so the received SNRs are 3.6, 2.0 and 1.38.
  • Each sensor has N = 64 antennas, n = 256 samples and M = 16.
  • Over 200 runs, the cluster-mean estimator gives a median position error of 7.1 m, and Mestre’s estimator 1.45 m.

The separation condition of Theorem 9.3 holds at the first two sensors but not at the third. There, c = 0.25 and population eigenvalues 1 and 2.38 give a single support interval, about [0.28, 4.01]. Mestre’s estimator still beats the naive one at that sensor, but its consistency is no longer guaranteed (see the third group project).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)

def mestre(lam, n, sizes):
    """Mestre (2008) estimator of population eigenvalues with known multiplicities (N < n)."""
    lam = np.sort(lam)
    s = np.sqrt(lam)
    mu = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.diag(lam) - np.outer(s, s) / n))
    out, start = [], 0
    for Nk in sizes:
        out.append(n / Nk * np.sum(lam[start:start + Nk] - mu[start:start + Nk]))
        start += Nk
    return np.array(out)

N, n, M = 64, 256, 16                  # antennas, samples, signal dimension
sizes = [N - M, M]                     # noise eigenvalue 1 (sigma^2), signal eigenvalue 1 + P
anchors = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [100.0, 0.0], [0.0, 100.0]])
target = np.array([40.0, 30.0])
P0, alpha = 9000.0, 2.0                # received power P = P0 / d^alpha (noise power 1)

def received_power_estimates(P):
    pop = np.repeat([1.0, 1.0 + P], sizes)
    Y = np.sqrt(pop)[:, None] * rng.standard_normal((N, n))
    lam = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(Y @ Y.T / n))
    naive = lam[-M:].mean() - lam[:-M].mean()
    g = mestre(lam, n, sizes)
    return naive, g[1] - g[0]

def trilaterate(d):
    A = 2 * (anchors[1:] - anchors[0])
    b = d[0]**2 - d[1:]**2 + np.sum(anchors[1:]**2, 1) - np.sum(anchors[0]**2)
    return np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)[0]

dist = np.linalg.norm(anchors - target, axis=1)
P = P0 / dist**alpha
print("true distances:", np.round(dist, 1), " received SNR:", np.round(P, 2))
err = {"naive": [], "G-estimator": []}
for _ in range(200):
    est = np.array([received_power_estimates(p) for p in P])      # rows: anchors
    for k, name in enumerate(err):
        d_hat = (P0 / np.maximum(est[:, k], 1e-3)) ** (1 / alpha)
        err[name].append(np.linalg.norm(trilaterate(d_hat) - target))
for name, e in err.items():
    print(f"{name:12s}: median position error {np.median(e):5.2f} m, mean {np.mean(e):5.2f} m")

The naive error is a bias, not noise: it does not shrink with more runs, and it grows with c = N/n. The G-estimator removes it, and its remaining error decreases like 1/N.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • A G-estimator is consistent when N/n \to c > 0; plug-in estimators usually are not (traces of \hat C^2 and \hat C^{-1}, cluster means).
  • Mestre’s estimator recovers population eigenvalues from the sample ones by a contour integral of Stieltjes transforms, under a separation condition.
  • Consistent powers give consistent distances, and trilateration turns them into a position.

10. Array processing: angles of arrival, MUSIC and G-MUSIC

An array of antennas estimates the directions of the sources it receives. The MUSIC algorithm reads them from the noise subspace of the sample covariance. With large arrays and few snapshots, the sample subspaces are biased (ch. 6), MUSIC loses its resolution, and a random-matrix correction restores it.

Definition 10.1 — Narrow-band array model A uniform linear array of N antennas at half-wavelength spacing receives K < N sources from angles \theta_1, \dots, \theta_K. The steering vector and the snapshots are a(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \big( e^{i \pi m \sin\theta} \big)_{m = 0}^{N-1}, \qquad y_t = A\, s_t + \sigma w_t , with A = [a(\theta_1), \dots, a(\theta_K)], independent source signals of powers P = \mathrm{diag}(P_k), and white noise. The covariance is R = A P A^* + \sigma^2 I.

Proposition 10.2 — The MUSIC principle (Schmidt, 1986) Let \Pi be the orthogonal projector on the eigenspace of R for the eigenvalue \sigma^2, the noise subspace. If the angles are distinct and P is positive definite, the pseudo-spectrum \eta(\theta) = a(\theta)^* \Pi\, a(\theta) vanishes exactly at \theta \in \{\theta_1, \dots, \theta_K\}.

Proof. R - \sigma^2 I = A P A^* has range \mathrm{range}(A), of dimension K, so the noise subspace is \mathrm{range}(A)^\perp and \eta(\theta) = \|\Pi a(\theta)\|^2.

It vanishes iff a(\theta) \in \mathrm{range}(A). Any K + 1 \leq N steering vectors with distinct angles in (-\pi/2, \pi/2) form a Vandermonde matrix with distinct nodes e^{i\pi \sin\theta}, hence are linearly independent.

So a(\theta) lies in the range of A iff \theta is one of the \theta_k. ∎

MUSIC replaces \Pi by the projector \hat\Pi on the N - K smallest sample eigenvectors, and returns the K deepest local minima of \hat\eta(\theta) = a(\theta)^* \hat\Pi\, a(\theta).

It is consistent when n \to \infty with N fixed.

The large-array regime

Let the number of sources K be fixed while N/n \to c. The matrix R/\sigma^2 is then a spiked covariance (ch. 6) whose spikes \ell_1, \dots, \ell_K are the eigenvalues of A P A^* / \sigma^2, with eigenvectors u_k.

Theorem 10.3 — MUSIC is not consistent Assume \ell_k > \sqrt{c} for every k, with distinct spikes. For each \theta, almost surely, \hat\eta(\theta) - \Big( 1 - \sum_{k=1}^K \zeta(\ell_k)\, |a(\theta)^* u_k|^2 \Big) \to 0 , with \zeta(\ell) = \frac{1 - c/\ell^2}{1 + c/\ell} < 1 as in Theorem 6.4. At \theta = \theta_j the limit is positive, while the true pseudo-spectrum \eta(\theta) = 1 - \sum_k |a(\theta)^* u_k|^2 vanishes.

Proof. \hat\Pi = I - \sum_{k \leq K} \hat u_k \hat u_k^* and \|a(\theta)\| = 1. The eigenvector part of Theorem 6.4 holds for bilinear forms (Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2011): for a deterministic unit a, a^* \hat u_k \hat u_k^* a - \zeta(\ell_k)\, |a^* u_k|^2 \to 0 . Summing over k gives the claim. ∎

When two sources are close, their two spikes are unequal (the steering vectors are nearly collinear) and the smaller one has a small \zeta. The two minima of \hat\eta merge, and MUSIC sees one source.

Proposition 10.4 — Spiked G-MUSIC Let \hat\sigma^2 be the average of the N - K smallest sample eigenvalues, \hat\ell_k the inversion of \hat\lambda_k / \hat\sigma^2 by the formula of ch. 6, and \hat\zeta_k = \zeta(\hat\ell_k). The corrected pseudo-spectrum \hat\eta_G(\theta) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{|a(\theta)^* \hat u_k|^2}{\hat\zeta_k} satisfies \hat\eta_G(\theta) - \eta(\theta) \to 0 almost surely for each \theta.

Proof. \hat\sigma^2 \to \sigma^2, because the N - K smallest eigenvalues follow the Marchenko–Pastur law scaled by \sigma^2, whose mean is \sigma^2. By Theorem 6.4 and continuity, \hat\ell_k \to \ell_k and \hat\zeta_k \to \zeta(\ell_k) > 0. Theorem 10.3 concludes. ∎

This estimator is the spiked form studied by Hachem, Loubaton, Mestre, Najim and Vallet (2013). The original G-MUSIC of Mestre and Lagunas (2008) weights all sample eigenvectors and does not assume few sources; Vallet, Loubaton and Mestre (2012) treat deterministic source signals with the signal-plus-noise model of ch. 4.

Example 10.5 — Two close sources With N = 40 antennas, n = 80 snapshots (c = 0.5) and two sources of power 3 (4.8 dB):

  • at 10° and 16°, both methods resolve the two sources in every run, with an error of 0.09°;
  • at 10° and 12°, MUSIC resolves them in 15% of the runs and G-MUSIC in 83%.
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import find_peaks

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

def steer(theta_deg, N):                     # half-wavelength ULA, unit-norm steering vectors
    m = np.arange(N)[:, None]
    return np.exp(1j * np.pi * m * np.sin(np.deg2rad(np.atleast_1d(theta_deg)))) / np.sqrt(N)

def doa_trial(N, n, thetas, P, grid):
    K, c = len(thetas), N / n
    S = np.sqrt(np.array(P))[:, None] * (rng.standard_normal((K, n)) + 1j * rng.standard_normal((K, n))) / np.sqrt(2)
    W = (rng.standard_normal((N, n)) + 1j * rng.standard_normal((N, n))) / np.sqrt(2)
    Y = steer(thetas, N) @ S + W
    lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Y @ Y.conj().T / n)
    lam, U = lam[::-1], U[:, ::-1]
    x = lam[:K] / lam[K:].mean() - 1 - c      # invert lambda = (1 + l)(1 + c / l)
    ell = (x + np.sqrt(np.maximum(x**2 - 4 * c, 0))) / 2
    zeta = np.maximum((1 - c / ell**2) / (1 + c / ell), 0.05)   # eigenvector shrinkage
    proj = np.abs(U[:, :K].conj().T @ steer(grid, N)) ** 2
    out = {}
    for name, cost in [("MUSIC", 1 - proj.sum(0)), ("G-MUSIC", 1 - (proj / zeta[:, None]).sum(0))]:
        pk, _ = find_peaks(-cost)
        out[name] = np.sort(grid[pk[np.argsort(cost[pk])[:K]]]) if len(pk) >= K else None
    return out

N, n, grid = 40, 80, np.linspace(-30, 50, 4001)
for thetas, P in [([10.0, 16.0], [3.0, 3.0]), ([10.0, 12.0], [3.0, 3.0])]:

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • MUSIC locates sources at the zeros of the noise-subspace pseudo-spectrum, which needs consistent subspace estimates.
  • With large arrays, |a^* \hat u_k|^2 \approx \zeta(\ell_k)\, |a^* u_k|^2: MUSIC is biased and loses resolution.
  • Dividing by the estimated \zeta(\hat\ell_k) gives a consistent pseudo-spectrum (spiked G-MUSIC).

11. Community detection on large graphs

Spectral clustering splits a graph with the top eigenvectors of its adjacency matrix. On a random graph with planted communities, the adjacency matrix is a low-rank signal plus a Wigner-like noise, so ch. 6 says exactly when the eigenvectors see the communities.

Definition 11.1 — Two-community stochastic block model The n vertices carry labels \sigma_i \in \{-1, +1\}, half of each. Edges are independent, present with probability p_{\mathrm{in}} inside a community and p_{\mathrm{out}} between communities. Write \bar p = \frac{p_{\mathrm{in}} + p_{\mathrm{out}}}{2}, \qquad \theta = \frac{\sqrt{n}\, (p_{\mathrm{in}} - p_{\mathrm{out}})}{2 \sqrt{\bar p (1 - \bar p)}} . The dense regime keeps \bar p fixed and \theta of order 1, so p_{\mathrm{in}} - p_{\mathrm{out}} is of order n^{-1/2}.

Off the diagonal, the mean of the adjacency matrix is \mathbb{E} A = \bar p\, \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top + \frac{p_{\mathrm{in}} - p_{\mathrm{out}}}{2}\, \sigma\sigma^\top . After subtracting the first term and scaling, the centred adjacency matrix decomposes as B = \frac{A - \bar p\, \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top}{\sqrt{n \bar p (1 - \bar p)}} = W + \theta\, v v^\top + E, \qquad v = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} . Here W has independent centred entries of variance \frac{1}{n}(1 + O(n^{-1/2})), and E collects the diagonal correction, of norm O(n^{-1/2}).

Theorem 11.2 — Spectral detectability threshold In the dense regime, almost surely, \lambda_{\max}(B) \to \begin{cases} \theta + 1/\theta & \text{if } \theta > 1, \\ 2 & \text{if } \theta \leq 1, \end{cases} \qquad \frac{\langle \hat v, \sigma \rangle^2}{n} \to \Big(1 - \frac{1}{\theta^2}\Big)^+ , where \hat v is the unit top eigenvector of B. In terms of the edge probabilities, the eigenvector is informative iff (p_{\mathrm{in}} - p_{\mathrm{out}})^2 > \frac{4\, \bar p (1 - \bar p)}{n} .

Proof. W is a Wigner matrix with independent Bernoulli-type entries; its ESD tends to the semicircle law and its norm to 2. By Weyl’s inequality (Proposition 0.2) E moves the eigenvalues by o(1).

The conclusions of Theorem 6.3 hold for such Wigner matrices when the spike direction is delocalized, as v = \sigma / \sqrt{n} is (Capitaine, Donati-Martin and Féral, 2009; Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2011). In practice \bar p is replaced by the observed edge density, which changes B by a negligible amount. ∎

Nadakuditi and Newman (2012) pointed out this transition for community detection. Its message is that a spectral method fails below the threshold, even though the communities exist.

The sign of \hat v_i classifies vertex i. The entries of \sqrt{n}\, \hat v are approximately \sqrt{1 - 1/\theta^2}\, \sigma_i plus independent Gaussian noise of variance 1/\theta^2, a heuristic that the simulation confirms. The misclassification rate is then \varepsilon(\theta) \approx \Phi\big(-\sqrt{\theta^2 - 1}\big) , with \Phi the standard normal distribution function.

Example 11.3 — Numbers With n = 2000 and \bar p = 0.1, the threshold \theta = 1 is reached when p_{\mathrm{in}} - p_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{2 \times 0.3}{\sqrt{2000}} = 0.0134 , that is p_{\mathrm{in}} = 0.1067 and p_{\mathrm{out}} = 0.0933.

At \theta = 2, the top eigenvalue is predicted at 2.5, the squared overlap at 0.75 and the error rate at \Phi(-\sqrt{3}) = 0.042 . 

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
n, pbar = 2000, 0.1
sigma = np.repeat([1.0, -1.0], n // 2)                 # community labels

print("theta  top eig  theory  overlap^2  theory  error rate  theory")
for theta in [0.8, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0]:
    delta = 2 * theta * np.sqrt(pbar * (1 - pbar) / n)  # p_in - p_out
    P = pbar + delta / 2 * np.outer(sigma, sigma)
    U = rng.random((n, n))
    A = np.triu(U < P, 1).astype(float)
    A = A + A.T                                          # undirected, no self-loops
    p_hat = A.sum() / (n * (n - 1))
    B = (A - p_hat) / np.sqrt(n * p_hat * (1 - p_hat))   # centred, scaled adjacency
    lam, vec = np.linalg.eigh(B)
    v = vec[:, -1]
    overlap = (v @ sigma) ** 2 / n
    err = min(np.mean(np.sign(v) != sigma), np.mean(np.sign(v) != -sigma))
    if theta > 1:
        th = (theta + 1 / theta, 1 - 1 / theta**2, norm.cdf(-np.sqrt(theta**2 - 1)))
    else:
        th = (2.0, 0.0, 0.5)
    print(f"{theta:4.1f}  {lam[-1]:7.3f}  {th[0]:6.3f}  {overlap:9.3f}  {th[1]:6.3f}"
          f"  {err:10.3f}  {th[2]:6.3f}")

At \theta = 0.8 the classification is a coin toss (error 0.496). At \theta = 2 the error is 0.043 against 0.042 predicted, and at \theta = 3 it is 0.002.

The sparse regime

Real networks are sparse: p_{\mathrm{in}} = a/n and p_{\mathrm{out}} = b/n with fixed a, b. Formally, the dense threshold \theta = 1 becomes (a - b)^2 > 2\, (a + b) , which is the Kesten–Stigum threshold. Decelle, Krzakala, Moore and Zdeborová (2011) conjectured that it is the threshold for any method, and Mossel, Neeman and Sly (2015, 2018) and Massoulié (2014) proved it.

The adjacency spectrum, however, fails before that: vertices of high degree, of order \log n / \log\log n, create eigenvalues larger than the community outlier, with eigenvectors localized on them. The non-backtracking matrix (Krzakala, Moore, Mossel, Neeman, Sly, Zdeborová and Zhang, 2013; Bordenave, Lelarge and Massoulié, 2018) and the Bethe Hessian (Saade, Krzakala and Zdeborová, 2014) repair spectral clustering down to the threshold. For networks with heterogeneous degrees, Ali and Couillet (2018) derive degree-corrected spectral methods with random matrix tools.

Key takeaways — chapter 11

  • The centred adjacency matrix of a two-community block model is a Wigner matrix plus a rank-one spike of strength \theta (Definition 11.1).
  • Spectral clustering is informative iff \theta > 1; the overlap is 1 - 1/\theta^2 and the error about \Phi(-\sqrt{\theta^2 - 1}).
  • In sparse graphs the same threshold reads (a - b)^2 > 2(a + b), reached by non-backtracking and Bethe Hessian spectra.

12. Kernel random matrices and kernel methods

Kernel methods compute an n \times n matrix K_{ij} = f(\cdot) of pairwise similarities and use its spectrum, for kernel PCA, spectral clustering or kernel ridge regression. In high dimension, all pairwise distances concentrate, and the kernel matrix becomes, to first order, a linear kernel plus a low-rank part and a multiple of the identity.

Linearization of kernel matrices

Let x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^p be independent \mathcal{N}(0, I_p) vectors, X = [x_1, \dots, x_n], and n/p \to \gamma \in (0, \infty). The key observation is the concentration of the inner products: \frac{x_i^\top x_j}{p} = O\big(p^{-1/2}\big) \ (i \neq j), \qquad \frac{\|x_i\|^2}{p} = 1 + O\big(p^{-1/2}\big) . Every off-diagonal entry of the kernel matrix is therefore evaluated near 0, and a Taylor expansion of f there is accurate.

Theorem 12.1 — Kernel matrices linearize (El Karoui, 2010) Let f be C^3 in a neighbourhood of 0 and continuous at 1, and let K_{ij} = f(x_i^\top x_j / p). Then, in operator norm and in probability, \|K - \tilde K\| \to 0, where \tilde K = \Big( f(0) + \frac{f''(0)}{2p} \Big) \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top + f'(0)\, \frac{X^\top X}{p} + \big( f(1) - f(0) - f'(0) \big) I_n .

Proof. Sketch. Expand each off-diagonal entry to third order at 0, and bound each error matrix in operator norm.

  1. Order 1. The terms f'(0)\, x_i^\top x_j / p form f'(0) X^\top X / p, minus its diagonal.
  2. Order 2. The matrix of (x_i^\top x_j)^2 / p^2 has off-diagonal mean 1/p, which gives the term \frac{f''(0)}{2p} \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top. Its centred part has entries of order 1/p, independent enough for a norm of order \sqrt{n}/p \to 0.
  3. Order 3. Entries of order p^{-3/2}; even a crude bound gives a norm of order n\, p^{-3/2} \to 0.
  4. Diagonal. K_{ii} = f(\|x_i\|^2/p) \to f(1) uniformly in i, while the linear terms put f(0) + f'(0) on the diagonal: the identity term corrects the difference. ∎

So the spectrum of K is, up to o(1), one large eigenvalue of order n f(0) along \mathbf{1}, plus a Marchenko–Pastur bulk scaled by f'(0) and shifted by f(1) - f(0) - f'(0).

A nonlinear kernel, in this regime, sees the data almost like a linear one. The same analysis applies to distance kernels f(\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / p), expanded around 2 (El Karoui, 2010).

Kernel spectral clustering

Consider a two-class mixture x_i = j_i\, \mu + z_i, with labels j_i \in \{-1, +1\} (half of each), z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_p) and \|\mu\|^2 = \ell of order 1. A single coordinate cannot separate the classes; only the aggregation over p coordinates can.

Proposition 12.2 — Phase transition of kernel spectral clustering Let f'(0) > 0, H = I_n - \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top, and \hat v the unit top eigenvector of H K H, with p/n \to c. Almost surely, \frac{\langle \hat v, j \rangle^2}{n} \to \frac{1 - c/\ell^2}{1 + 1/\ell} \quad \text{if } \ell > \sqrt{c}, \qquad 0 \text{ otherwise} .

Proof. Theorem 12.1 applies to the mixture as well, since \|\mu\| is bounded. The centring H removes the \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top term, so the top eigenvectors of HKH are, up to o(1) in norm, those of H X^\top X H.

The matrix X = \mu j^\top + Z is a rank-one signal plus noise. Its top singular values and vectors have the limits of the spiked covariance model with \ell = \|\mu\|^2, in dimension p with n samples.

The top eigenvector of X^\top X is the right singular vector of X. Its alignment with j is the n-dimensional counterpart of Theorem 6.4, with 1 + 1/\ell in the denominator (Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2012). The centring does not affect it since j \perp \mathbf{1}. ∎

The phase transition of linear PCA survives the kernel: with f'(0) > 0, kernel spectral clustering on this mixture is not better than spectral clustering on X^\top X. Couillet and Benaych-Georges (2016) analyse general mixtures, where classes also differ by their covariances: the information then sits in f' and f'' at the concentration point, and the choice of the kernel controls which statistics of the classes are used.

The code checks both statements: the linearization error decreases like p^{-1/2}, and the clustering accuracy follows Proposition 12.2.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(13)

# (a) K = f(X^T X / p) with f = exp is close to its linearization (El Karoui, 2010)
for p in [100, 200, 400, 800]:
    n = 2 * p
    X = rng.standard_normal((p, n))                    # columns x_i ~ N(0, I_p)
    G = X.T @ X / p
    K = np.exp(G)                                      # f(0) = f'(0) = 1, f(1) = e
    K_lin = (1 + 1 / (2 * p)) * np.ones((n, n)) + G + (np.e - 2) * np.eye(n)
    gap = np.abs(np.linalg.eigvalsh(K - K_lin)).max()
    print(f"p={p:4d}, n={n:4d}: ||K - K_lin|| = {gap:.3f}, ||K|| = {np.linalg.eigvalsh(K)[-1]:.0f}")

# (b) kernel spectral clustering of x_i = j_i mu + z_i, with the same kernel
p, n = 400, 800
c = p / n
j = np.repeat([1.0, -1.0], n // 2)
H = np.eye(n) - np.ones((n, n)) / n                    # centring removes the f(0) 1 1^T part
print(" ||mu||^2  alignment  theory  error rate  theory")
for ell in [0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 3.0]:
    mu = np.zeros(p); mu[0] = np.sqrt(ell)
    Xm = np.outer(mu, j) + rng.standard_normal((p, n))
    v = np.linalg.eigh(H @ np.exp(Xm.T @ Xm / p) @ H)[1][:, -1]
    align = (v @ j) ** 2 / n
    err = min(np.mean(np.sign(v) != j), np.mean(np.sign(v) != -j))
    th = (1 - c / ell**2) / (1 + 1 / ell) if ell > np.sqrt(c) else 0.0
    th_err = norm.cdf(-np.sqrt(th / (1 - th)))
    print(f"{ell:8.1f}  {align:9.3f}  {th:6.3f}  {err:10.3f}  {th_err:6.3f}")

The linearization error goes from 0.98 to 0.41 as p goes from 100 to 800, against a matrix norm of 200 to 1600. With c = 0.5, the threshold is \ell = 0.71: at \ell = 0.5 the error is 0.43, close to chance, and at \ell = 3 it is 0.059 against 0.060 predicted by the Gaussian heuristic of ch. 11.

Key takeaways — chapter 12

  • In high dimension, f(x_i^\top x_j / p) concentrates near f(0), and K is close to a multiple of \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top, plus f'(0)\, X^\top X / p, plus a multiple of I.
  • Kernel spectral clustering of a mean-separated mixture inherits the spiked-model transition \|\mu\|^2 > \sqrt{p/n}.
  • What a kernel can extract in this regime is decided by a few derivatives of f at the concentration point.

13.1 The Marchenko–Pastur theorem for general entries

Theorem 3.1 holds for independent and identically distributed entries with mean 0 and variance 1, without any higher moment. The proof of Bai and Silverstein (2010) has three steps.

  1. Truncation and centring. Replace X_{ij} by X_{ij} \mathbf{1}_{|X_{ij}| \leq \eta_n \sqrt{n}}, recentred and rescaled, with \eta_n \to 0 slowly. Proposition 1.6 and a bound on the Lévy distance by the Frobenius norm of the difference show that the ESD does not change in the limit.
  2. Leave-one-out. With Q_{-j} the resolvent without the column x_j, Sherman–Morrison gives 1 + z\, m_N = \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\frac{1}{n} x_j^\top Q_{-j} x_j}{1 + \frac{1}{n} x_j^\top Q_{-j} x_j} .
  3. Concentration. The trace lemma (Proposition 0.5, in its fourth-moment form after truncation) gives, uniformly in j, \frac{1}{n} x_j^\top Q_{-j} x_j \approx \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, Q_{-j} \approx c_n m_N . The denominators stay away from 0 because |1 + q| \geq v / |z| when q is such a quadratic form.

Inserting the approximation gives 1 + z m_N \approx \frac{m_N}{1 + c_n m_N}, which rearranges into the equation of Theorem 3.1. The almost sure convergence of m_N - \mathbb{E}\, m_N uses a martingale decomposition along the columns instead of the Poincaré–Nash inequality.

13.2 The moment method

For the GOE of Theorem 2.5, expand the moments as sums over closed walks: \mathbb{E}\, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, W^{k} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_k} \mathbb{E}\big[W_{i_1 i_2} W_{i_2 i_3} \cdots W_{i_k i_1}\big] .

  • Only walks that use each edge at least twice contribute. Those that contribute at order 1 use each edge exactly twice and trace a tree with k/2 edges.
  • Such trees are counted by the Catalan number C_{k/2}, which is the number of non-crossing pair partitions of k points. Odd moments vanish.
  • So the even moments tend to 1, 2, 5, 14, \dots, the moments of the semicircle law, which is determined by its moments because it has compact support.

For \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} X X^\top, the walks alternate between row and column indices, and the count weights each non-crossing partition by a power of c. This gives the Narayana polynomials of Proposition 3.2.

The same expansion with k of order \log N controls the largest eigenvalue (Füredi and Komlós, 1981, for Wigner matrices; Yin, Bai and Krishnaiah, 1988, for sample covariance matrices). The fourth moment is necessary: Bai, Silverstein and Yin (1988) show that if \mathbb{E}\, X_{11}^4 = \infty, then \limsup \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma) = \infty almost surely, while the ESD still converges to \mu_c.

13.3 Free probability

Two sequences of matrices are asymptotically free when, roughly, their eigenvectors are in generic position with respect to each other, for instance B_N = U B U^* with U Haar-distributed (Voiculescu, 1991). Then the limit spectra of sums and products depend only on the limit spectra of the summands.

  • Sums. Let G(z) = -m(z) and define the R-transform by G\big(R(w) + 1/w\big) = w. The R-transforms of free matrices add. The semicircle law of variance s has R(w) = s\, w, so the sum of two free semicircles of variance 1 is a semicircle of variance 2.
  • Marchenko–Pastur. Substituting z = R(G) + 1/G into the equation of Theorem 3.1 gives R_{\mu_c}(w) = \frac{1}{1 - c\, w} , the R-transform of a free Poisson law with rate 1/c and jump size c.
  • Products. The S-transforms of free positive matrices multiply. The limit ESD of \hat C = C^{1/2} \Sigma C^{1/2} is the free multiplicative convolution H \boxtimes \mu_c, and Silverstein’s equation (Proposition 4.2) is its explicit form.

Mingo and Speicher (2017) give a complete introduction; Potters and Bouchaud (2020) use the R- and S-transforms throughout for applications.

13.4 Fluctuations of linear spectral statistics

A linear spectral statistic is \sum_k f(\lambda_k). The mutual information of ch. 5 (f = \log(1 + \rho\, \cdot)) and \log \det \hat C (f = \log) are examples. Proposition 2.4 already suggests that such sums fluctuate by O(1), without the \sqrt{N} of independent sums.

Theorem 13.1 — CLT for linear spectral statistics (Bai and Silverstein, 2004) Let the entries be independent and identically distributed, with mean 0, variance 1 and \mathbb{E}\, X_{11}^4 = 3 (as for the Gaussian), c_n \to c \in (0, 1), and f analytic on a neighbourhood of the support. Then \sum_{k=1}^N f(\lambda_k) - N \int f\, d\mu_{c_n} converges in law to a Gaussian, whose mean and variance are given by contour integrals of m_c. For f = \log and real data the limit law is \mathcal{N}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1 - c),\ -2 \log(1 - c)\Big) ; for complex data the mean is 0 and the variance -\log(1 - c).

The centring uses \mu_{c_n}, not \mu_c, and for f = \log it has a closed form: \int \log x\; \mu_c(dx) = \frac{c - 1}{c}\, \log(1 - c) - 1 \qquad (c < 1) . 

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
print("  N    n   mean (theory)       variance (theory)")
for N, n in [(50, 100), (200, 400)]:
    c = N / n
    centre = N * ((c - 1) / c * np.log(1 - c) - 1)          # N * int log x dMP_c(x)
    stats = []
    for _ in range(2000):
        X = rng.standard_normal((N, n))
        stats.append(np.linalg.slogdet(X @ X.T / n)[1] - centre)
    stats = np.array(stats)
    print(f"{N:4d} {n:4d}   {stats.mean():6.3f} ({0.5 * np.log(1 - c):6.3f})"
          f"     {stats.var():6.3f} ({-2 * np.log(1 - c):6.3f})")

For N = 200 and n = 400 the mean is -0.354 against -0.347 and the variance 1.38 against 1.39.

For MIMO channels, Hachem, Loubaton and Najim (2008) proved the corresponding CLT for the mutual information with a variance profile. It gives the outage probability \mathbb{P}(I_N < R) at a target rate R. With the numbers of ch. 5 (N = n = 8, 10 dB, mean 21.8 bits, standard deviation about 1.27 bits), the Gaussian approximation gives an outage probability of \Phi(-1.41) \approx 8\% at 20 bits per channel use.

13.5 Shrinkage of the sample covariance

Since the sample eigenvalues are biased, a better covariance estimator keeps the sample eigenvectors and changes the eigenvalues. Ledoit and Wolf (2004) shrink linearly towards a multiple of the identity, with an intensity estimated consistently when N/n \to c.

The best rotation-equivariant estimator \sum_k d_k\, \hat u_k \hat u_k^\top in Frobenius norm uses the oracle values d_k = \hat u_k^\top C\, \hat u_k. Ledoit and Péché (2011) show that d_k is asymptotically a function of \hat\lambda_k alone: d(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda}{\big|1 - c - c\, \lambda\, \breve m(\lambda)\big|^2}, \qquad \breve m(\lambda) = \lim_{\eta \downarrow 0} m_F(\lambda + i\eta) . Ledoit and Wolf (2012, 2020) estimate this nonlinear shrinkage from the data. The code compares the oracle with the formula, computing m_F by the deterministic equivalent of ch. 4:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N, n = 400, 800
c = N / n
pop = np.repeat([1.0, 4.0], N // 2)                  # population eigenvalues
Y = np.sqrt(pop)[:, None] * rng.standard_normal((N, n))
lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Y @ Y.T / n)
oracle = np.sum(U**2 * pop[:, None], axis=0)         # u_k^T C u_k

def m_F(z, iters=3000):                              # deterministic equivalent of ch. 4
    d = 1.0 + 0j
    for _ in range(iters):
        d = 0.5 * d + 0.5 * np.sum(pop / (pop / (1 + d) - z)) / n
    return np.mean(1 / (pop / (1 + d) - z))

for k in [20, 100, 200, 300, 380]:
    m = m_F(lam[k] + 1e-4j)
    d = lam[k] / abs(1 - c - c * lam[k] * m) ** 2
    print(f"sample eigenvalue {lam[k]:6.3f}: oracle {oracle[k]:.3f}, Ledoit-Peche formula {d:.3f}")

A sample eigenvalue of 0.22 is pulled up to about 1.31, and one of 7.32 pulled down to 3.85: the shrunk values stay between the population eigenvalues 1 and 4. The formula follows the oracle within 10%, and within 0.2% at the two extremes.

13.6 Universality by replacement

Many results of the course were proved for Gaussian entries. The Lindeberg replacement strategy transfers them: swap the entries one at a time for Gaussian ones and control the change of a smooth function of the matrix by a third-order Taylor expansion (Chatterjee, 2006). Matching the first two moments gives the ESD; matching four moments gives local statistics, which is the four moment theorem of Tao and Vu (2011).

Key takeaways — chapter 13

  • The Marchenko–Pastur law needs only a finite variance; the leave-one-out argument replaces Gaussian calculus for general entries.
  • Moments count non-crossing partitions (Catalan, Narayana); the fourth moment decides whether the largest eigenvalue sticks to the edge.
  • Free probability turns sums and products of generic matrices into additions of R-transforms and products of S-transforms.
  • Linear spectral statistics fluctuate by O(1) with a Gaussian limit; nonlinear shrinkage corrects the sample eigenvalues with the Stieltjes transform.

14. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is self-study. It points to active research that uses the tools of the course, in a neutral voice, with references to published work.

14.1 High-dimensional regression and double descent

For least squares with n samples, p isotropic Gaussian features, noise variance \sigma^2 and p/n = \gamma < 1, the excess prediction risk tends to \sigma^2\, \frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma} , a direct consequence of \mathbb{E}\, \mathrm{tr}(X^\top X)^{-1}, as in Proposition 9.2. It explodes at \gamma = 1 and, for the minimum-norm interpolator beyond it, decreases again: the double descent curve. Hastie, Montanari, Rosset and Tibshirani (2022) derive these curves for ridge and ridgeless regression with general covariances, using deterministic equivalents. Mei and Montanari (2022) do it for random features regression, where a nonlinear random matrix appears.

14.2 Neural networks

Pennington and Worah (2017) and Louart, Liao and Couillet (2018) characterize the spectrum of the Gram matrix of random features \sigma(W X), which ch. 12 anticipates: a nonlinearity acts through a few Gaussian moments of its derivatives. Fan and Wang (2020) extend this to the conjugate and neural tangent kernels of networks of linear width.

Pennington and Bahri (2017) model the Hessian of the loss with free probability. Martin and Mahoney (2021) observe heavy-tailed empirical spectra in the weight matrices of trained networks, and propose them as a diagnostic of training quality; a theory that explains these spectra is open.

14.3 Beyond Gaussian and independent data

Real data are neither Gaussian nor made of independent entries. Concentration of measure gives a framework: data that are Lipschitz images of Gaussian vectors, such as the outputs of generative networks, behave like Gaussian mixtures for the spectral quantities of the course (Seddik, Louart, Tamaazousti and Couillet, 2020). Couillet and Liao (2022) develop this program for machine learning.

Heavy tails change the picture: the limit ESD is no longer Marchenko–Pastur when the variance is infinite (Ben Arous and Guionnet, 2008). Dependent samples, such as time series, require new deterministic equivalents.

14.4 Detection and estimation below the spectral threshold

Below the BBP threshold of ch. 6, no eigenvalue separates from the bulk, but the signal is not invisible to all tests. Onatski, Moreira and Hallin (2013) show that likelihood ratio tests based on all eigenvalues keep a non-trivial power below the threshold.

Perry, Wein, Bandeira and Moitra (2018) and El Alaoui, Krzakala and Jordan (2020) determine when detection is possible at all in spiked models with structured priors. The gap between what is possible and what efficient algorithms achieve is an open question in many such models.

14.5 Random tensors

Data with more than two indices lead to spiked tensor models. Montanari and Richard (2014) introduced tensor PCA and found a gap between the statistical threshold and the one reached by known polynomial-time algorithms. Ben Arous, Mei, Montanari and Nica (2019) study the landscape of its likelihood. Goulart, Couillet and Comon (2022) analyse random tensors through random matrices built from their contractions.

14.6 Communications and positioning

Massive MIMO, with many more base-station antennas than users (Marzetta, 2010), is the regime where the deterministic equivalents of ch. 5 are most accurate. Hoydis, ten Brink and Debbah (2013) use them to size such systems.

The positioning problem of ch. 9 with many sources, unknown channel models and few samples remains a field of applications for G-estimation. Estimating the whole population spectrum from the sample one is a related inverse problem (El Karoui, 2008; Ledoit and Wolf, 2020).

Open problems

  • Non-asymptotic versions of the deterministic equivalents, with explicit constants, for moderate N and n.
  • Spectral methods with optimal thresholds for sparse, heterogeneous or dynamic graphs.
  • The choice of a kernel, or a nonlinearity, that extracts the most information from structured data in the proportional regime.
  • A predictive theory of the spectra of trained, not random, neural network weights.

Key takeaways — chapter 14

  • Deterministic equivalents give exact learning curves in the proportional regime: ridge, ridgeless and random-features regression, double descent.
  • Current work extends the tools to nonlinear features, structured and heavy-tailed data, sparse graphs and tensors.
  • Below spectral thresholds, the question becomes what any test or efficient algorithm can still detect.

  1. ★ Stieltjes transforms by hand. (a) Compute the Stieltjes transform of the uniform law on [0, 1] and recover its density at x = 1/2 with Theorem 1.4. (b) For A = \mathrm{diag}(0, 2), compute m_{\mu_A}(1 + i) and check properties 1–3 of Proposition 1.3.

    Solution: (a) With the principal branch of the logarithm, m(z) = \int_0^1 \frac{d\lambda}{\lambda - z} = \log \frac{1 - z}{-z} . Let z = x + i\varepsilon with 0 < x < 1. As \varepsilon \downarrow 0, the argument of 1 - z tends to 0 and that of -z to -\pi.

    So \Im\, m tends to \pi, and the density is 1.

    (b) The two atoms give m = \frac{1}{2}\Big( \frac{1}{-1 - i} + \frac{1}{1 - i} \Big) = \frac{1}{2}\Big( \frac{-1 + i}{2} + \frac{1 + i}{2} \Big) = \frac{i}{2} . Its imaginary part is positive, |m| = 0.5 \leq 1/\Im z = 1, and -iy\, m(iy) \to 1 by direct expansion.

  2. ★ Centring the data does not change the limit. Let \bar x = \frac{1}{n} X \mathbf{1} be the sample mean and \Sigma_c the sample covariance of the centred vectors x_j - \bar x. Show that \Sigma - \Sigma_c has rank one, and deduce that \Sigma_c has the same Marchenko–Pastur limit and the same limit of its largest eigenvalue as \Sigma.

    Solution: expanding the sum gives \Sigma_c = \Sigma - \bar x \bar x^\top, a rank-one positive semidefinite correction. By Proposition 1.6, \sup_t |F^{\Sigma} - F^{\Sigma_c}| \leq 1/N, so the limits coincide (Theorem 1.5).

    By interlacing (Proposition 0.2), \lambda_1(\Sigma_c) \geq \lambda_2(\Sigma). Since \Sigma_c \preceq \Sigma, Theorem 0.1 also gives \lambda_1(\Sigma_c) \leq \lambda_1(\Sigma).

    Both bounds tend to (1 + \sqrt{c})^2: the first because the second eigenvalue also sticks to the edge (Theorem 3.4 and the ESD near the edge).

  3. ★ Moments of the semicircle law. (a) Compute \int x^2 \sigma(dx) and \int x^4 \sigma(dx) from the density. (b) For the GOE of Theorem 2.5, compute \mathbb{E}\, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, W^2 exactly.

    Solution: (a) With x = 2 \sin t, \int x^{2k} \sigma(dx) = \frac{2^{2k+2}}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \sin^{2k} t \cos^2 t\, dt . Since \int \sin^2 \cos^2 = \pi/8 and \int \sin^4 \cos^2 = \pi/16, the moments are 1 and 2, the Catalan numbers C_1 and C_2.

    (b) The N(N - 1) off-diagonal entries have variance 1/N and the N diagonal ones 2/N, so \mathbb{E}\, \tfrac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, W^2 = \frac{1}{N}\Big( N - 1 + 2 \Big) = 1 + \frac{1}{N} .

  4. ★ Marchenko–Pastur in numbers. (a) For c = 0.25, give the support, the density at x = 1 and the asymptotic condition number. (b) With N = 100, how many samples are needed for an asymptotic condition number at most 4? (c) For N = 400 and n = 100, describe the spectrum.

    Solution: (a) The support is [0.25, 2.25], the density at 1 is \sqrt{1.25 \times 0.75} / (2\pi \times 0.25) = 0.616, the condition number 9.

    (b) With s = \sqrt{c}, we need (1 + s)/(1 - s) \leq 2, so s \leq 1/3, c \leq 1/9 and n \geq 900.

    (c) c = 4: a fraction 1 - 1/c = 0.75 of the eigenvalues is 0, and the others fill [(1 - 2)^2, (1 + 2)^2] = [1, 9].

  5. ★ The capacity of a large MIMO link. (a) For c = 1 and \rho = 10, solve the fixed-point equation of Theorem 5.3 in closed form and compute \mathcal{V}_1(10). (b) Show that \mathcal{V}_1(\rho) = \log \rho - 1 + o(1) as \rho \to \infty, and interpret.

    Solution: (a) With c = 1 the equation reads \delta (11 + \delta) = 10 (1 + \delta), so \delta^2 + \delta - 10 = 0 and \delta = (\sqrt{41} - 1)/2 = 2.7016. Then 1 + \rho/(1 + \delta) = 1 + \delta = 3.7016, and \mathcal{V}_1(10) = 2 \log 3.7016 - \frac{2.7016}{3.7016} = 1.888 \text{ nats} = 2.72 \text{ bits} .

    (b) In general \delta^2 + \delta - \rho = 0, so \delta \sim \sqrt{\rho} and \mathcal{V}_1(\rho) = 2 \log(1 + \delta) - \frac{\delta}{1 + \delta} = \log \rho - 1 + o(1) . The slope 1 per receive antenna is the multiplexing gain; the constant -1 is \int \log x\, \mu_1(dx).

  6. ★ Spikes in numbers. Let c = 0.5. (a) Where is the outlier of a spike \ell = 2, and what is the eigenvector alignment? (b) The top sample eigenvalue is 4.5 and the noise level is 1: estimate \ell. (c) With N = 100, how many samples are needed for a spike \ell = 0.5 to produce an outlier?

    Solution: (a) (1 + 2)(1 + 0.25) = 3.75 and \zeta = (1 - 0.125)/1.25 = 0.70. (b) \hat\lambda - 1 - c = 3, so \hat\ell = \frac{3 + \sqrt{9 - 2}}{2} = 2.82 . (c) We need \sqrt{N/n} < 0.5, so n > 400.

  7. ★ A Tracy–Widom threshold, in Python. Adapt the code of ch. 7 to compute the 99% quantile of F_1, deduce the 1% threshold for \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma) with N = 100 and n = 400, and check the false-alarm rate by simulation.

    Hints and expected result: the quantile is about 2.02, so the threshold is (898.5 + 2.02 \times 15.93)/400 = 2.327. Over 2000 simulations the false-alarm rate is about 0.8%, slightly below 1%, as at the 5% level in ch. 7.

  8. ★ Detectability of communities. A graph has n = 10\,000 vertices, \bar p = 0.05, p_{\mathrm{in}} = 0.055 and p_{\mathrm{out}} = 0.045. Is spectral clustering informative? Predict the overlap and the error rate.

    Solution: the threshold difference is 2\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95 / 10\,000} = 0.00436, so \theta = 0.01 / 0.00436 = 2.29 > 1. The predicted squared overlap is 1 - 1/\theta^2 = 0.81 and the error rate \Phi\big(-\sqrt{\theta^2 - 1}\big) = \Phi(-2.06) \approx 0.019 .

  9. ★★ The Poincaré–Nash bound for the GOE. With W = (G + G^\top)/\sqrt{2N} and m_N = \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}(W - z)^{-1}, show that \mathbb{E}\, |m_N - \mathbb{E}\, m_N|^2 \leq \frac{2}{N^2 v^4} .

    Solution: as a function of the N^2 independent entries of G, using the symmetry of Q, \frac{\partial m_N}{\partial G_{ab}} = -\frac{1}{N\sqrt{2N}}\, \mathrm{tr}\big(Q^2 (E_{ab} + E_{ba})\big) = -\frac{2}{N\sqrt{2N}}\, (Q^2)_{ab} . The sum of the squared moduli is \frac{2}{N^3} \|Q^2\|_F^2, and \|Q^2\|_F^2 \leq N / v^4. Theorem 2.2 concludes.

  10. ★★ The second moment of a sample covariance matrix. For independent entries with mean 0, variance 1 and fourth moment \kappa, show that \mathbb{E}\, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \Sigma^2 = 1 + c_n + \frac{\kappa - 2}{n} , and compare with Proposition 3.2.

    Solution: expand the trace as \mathrm{tr}\, \Sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i, k} \Big(\sum_j X_{ij} X_{kj}\Big)^2 . For i \neq k the expectation of the square is n; for i = k it is n\kappa + n(n - 1). Summing, \mathbb{E}\, \mathrm{tr}\, \Sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n^2}\Big(N(N-1)\, n + N\big(n^2 + (\kappa - 1)\, n\big)\Big) . Dividing by N gives the formula; its limit 1 + c is the second Narayana moment. The fourth moment only enters at order 1/n.

  11. ★★ Variance profiles that factorize. (a) Show that when \sigma_{ij}^2 = d_i depends on the row only, the system of Theorem 4.5 reduces to Theorem 4.1 with C = \mathrm{diag}(d_i). (b) What happens when \sigma_{ij}^2 = \tilde d_j depends on the column only?

    Solution: (a) The right-hand side of the equation for \tilde t_j no longer depends on j. So \tilde t_j = \tilde t = -1/(z(1 + \delta)) with \delta = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i d_i t_i. Then t_i = \frac{-1}{z(1 + d_i \tilde t)} = \frac{1}{\frac{d_i}{1 + \delta} - z} , the diagonal of \bar Q, and \delta = \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C \bar Q.

    (b) By symmetry it is the population model for the n \times n matrix Y^* Y, with \mathrm{diag}(\tilde d_j) as population covariance: column variances are a covariance between samples.

  12. ★★ An outlier below the bulk. Let c < 1 and C = I + \ell u u^\top with \ell \in (-1, 0). Adapt the proof of Theorem 6.4 to show that an outlier appears below (1 - \sqrt{c})^2 iff \ell < -\sqrt{c}, at the value (1 + \ell)(1 + c/\ell).

    Check it for c = 0.1 and \ell = -0.9.

    Solution: steps 1–4 of the proof are unchanged and give \lambda = (1 + \ell)(1 + c/\ell), now with \lambda to the left of the bulk. The map \ell \mapsto (1 + \ell)(1 + c/\ell) equals (1 - \sqrt{c})^2 at \ell = -\sqrt{c} and is below it on (-1, -\sqrt{c}). The branch condition holds exactly there.

    For c = 0.1 and \ell = -0.9, the outlier is at 0.1 \times (1 - 1/9) = 0.089, below the edge 0.468. A simulation with N = 200 and n = 2000 gives 0.089.

    For \ell = -0.2, there is no outlier: the smallest eigenvalue stays at the edge.

  13. ★★ The GLRT statistic is pivotal. (a) Show that T_N is invariant under y_t \mapsto \alpha U y_t for a non-zero scalar \alpha and a unitary U. Deduce that its law under \mathcal{H}_0 depends only on the dimensions.

    (b) For N = 50, n = 100 and \rho = 1, compare the limit of T_N under \mathcal{H}_1 with the 5% threshold of ch. 8.

    Solution: (a) \hat R \mapsto |\alpha|^2 U \hat R U^* multiplies the largest eigenvalue and the normalized trace by |\alpha|^2. Under \mathcal{H}_0, y_t = \sigma w_t, so the law of T_N is that of the case \sigma = 1.

    (b) The limit is (1 + 1)(1 + 0.5) = 3.0, essentially the finite-size threshold 3.001 at N = 50. At this size the test is at the edge of its range: T_N has mean about 2.94, still below its limit, and a simulation as in ch. 8 gives a power of about 0.33.

    Asymptotically the picture is better. With c = 0.5 fixed, the threshold decreases to the edge 2.914 while the limit under \mathcal{H}_1 stays at 3.0, so the power tends to 1: \rho = 1 > \sqrt{c} is detectable. The simulated power is about 0.58 at N = 200 and 0.9 at N = 800.

  14. ★★ Two G-estimators. (a) Using the Wishart moments of Proposition 9.2, show that the corrected estimator of \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, C^2 has a relative bias \frac{1}{n} - \frac{2}{n^2} for real Gaussian data. (b) For C = I, N = 100 and n = 200, compute the expectations of the naive and corrected estimators of \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, C^{-1}.

    Solution: (a) Let t_1 = \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, C and t_2 = \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, C^2. By Proposition 9.2, \mathbb{E}\, \frac{1}{N} \mathrm{tr}\, \hat C^2 = \frac{n + 1}{n}\, t_2 + c_n t_1^2 . The trace \mathrm{tr}\, \hat C has mean \mathrm{tr}\, C and variance \frac{2}{n} \mathrm{tr}\, C^2, so \mathbb{E}\, c_n \Big(\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}\, \hat C\Big)^2 = c_n t_1^2 + \frac{2}{n^2}\, t_2 . The difference is t_2 (1 + \frac{1}{n} - \frac{2}{n^2}).

    (b) The naive estimator has mean n/(n - N - 1) = 200/99 = 2.02; the corrected one, \frac{99}{200} \times 2.02 = 1.

  15. ★★★ The resolution of G-MUSIC, in Python. With the code of ch. 10 (N = 40, two sources of power 3), estimate the probability of resolution of MUSIC and G-MUSIC for separations from 1° to 6° and n \in \{40, 80, 160\}. Relate the results to the spikes \ell_{1,2} = P(1 \pm |a(\theta_1)^* a(\theta_2)|).

    Hints and expected result: for 10° and 12°, |a(\theta_1)^* a(\theta_2)| = 0.39, so the spikes are 4.17 and 1.83, both above \sqrt{c} = 0.71 at n = 80.

    Their alignments are \zeta = 0.87 and 0.67. This imbalance is what merges the two minima of MUSIC (15% resolution against 83% for G-MUSIC).

    As the separation shrinks, the smaller spike approaches \sqrt{c} and both methods fail; larger n lowers \sqrt{c} and moves the failure to smaller separations. A complete answer plots both probabilities against the separation and marks where the smaller spike crosses \sqrt{c}.

  16. ★★★ Kernel clustering when the classes differ by their covariance. Let x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (1 + j_i a / \sqrt{p}) I_p) with p = 400, n = 800 and a \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}.

    Compare spectral clustering (top eigenvector of HKH) with three kernels: the linear kernel X^\top X / p, the Gaussian kernel e^{-t/2} and the kernel (t - 2)^2, where t = \|x_i - x_j\|^2 / p. Explain the ranking with ch. 12.

    Hints and expected result: the linear kernel fails (error about 0.49): the classes have the same mean. The class information is in \psi_i = \|x_i\|^2 / p, whose means differ by 2a/\sqrt{p} while each fluctuates by \sqrt{2/p}. A rule based on \psi_i alone has error about \Phi(-a/\sqrt{2}): 0.24, 0.08, 0.02, 0.002.

    Expand f(\psi_i + \psi_j - 2 x_i^\top x_j / p) around 2 and write \tilde\psi = \psi - \mathbf{1}. The first-order terms in \psi are killed by the centring.

    At second order, the cross term 2 \tilde\psi_i \tilde\psi_j of the square brings the information in as f''(2)\, \tilde\psi \tilde\psi^\top. The bulk -2 f'(2)\, X^\top X / p hides it when f'(2) \neq 0.

    So the kernel with f'(2) = 0 is near the \psi-rule (errors about 0.25, 0.08, 0.02, 0.003). The Gaussian kernel is much worse while the signal is weak (about 0.48, 0.45, 0.07, 0.01): it stays close to chance up to a = 2. This is the mechanism analysed by Couillet and Benaych-Georges (2016).

  17. ★★★ Outage of a MIMO channel. For N = n \in \{2, 4, 8\} at 10 dB, estimate by Monte Carlo the mean and standard deviation of I_N, and compare the Gaussian approximation of ch. 13 of the outage probability \mathbb{P}(I_N < R) with the empirical one, at the rates where the approximation predicts 5% and 1%. Discuss the tails.

    Hints and expected result: the means are about 5.55, 10.96 and 21.8 bits and the standard deviations about 1.32, 1.28 and 1.27 bits: the fluctuations do not grow with N. With 20 000 draws, the empirical outage at the predicted 5% point is about 4.6%, 4.8% and 4.8%, and at the predicted 1% point about 0.6%, 0.9% and 0.9%, up to a Monte Carlo standard error of about 0.1 percentage point.

    The Gaussian approximation is already good at N = 4; at N = 2 the left tail is lighter than Gaussian. Far tails, needed for very low outage targets, call for large-deviation estimates rather than the CLT.
