Audience: a student with a good M1 in mathematics, physics or computer science, at ease with Gaussian vectors and multivariable calculus, who wants to understand — and prove — why a network trained to remove noise can generate images, sounds and weather forecasts.

Empirical first: plain Langevin sampling of the mixture \frac12\mathcal{N}(-4, 1) + \frac12\mathcal{N}(4, 1), started in the right mode, crosses to the other mode about twice in 10 000 time units, so after 500 units only 10 % of the chains sit on the left instead of 50 % (ch. 2). Progressive denoising from white noise, driven by the exact score, reproduces the weight 0.321 of the left mode of another mixture up to 0.003 (ch. 3). Why does denoising succeed where direct sampling stalls, and why does a network trained on finitely many examples not simply return them (ch. 4)?

Modern generative models sample a probability distribution known only through examples: images, sounds, physical fields. This course, taught by Stéphane Mallat (ENS Ulm / Collège de France, as listed on master-mva.com), builds them from one idea. A transport equation turns white noise into data by progressive denoising, and the score of the noisy distributions that drives it is estimated by a deep network.

The multiscale thread — orthonormal bases, sparsity, wavelets and the factorisation of a probability across scales — explains what these networks compute and why they can learn in high dimension. The stochastic calculus is shared with Stochastic Calculus in Machine Learning, the denoisers with Image Denoising, and the image applications with Generative Models for Images and Deep Learning for Image Restoration and Synthesis.

0. Review: densities, Gaussian smoothing and integration by parts

Everything in this course happens to probability densities on \mathbb{R}^d: they are pushed by maps, smoothed by Gaussian noise and differentiated. This chapter collects the M1 tools used later, with short proofs, so that the later chapters can use them without stopping.

0.1 Comparing probability distributions

Definition 0.1 — Divergences and distances For two probability densities p and q on \mathbb{R}^d, the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the total variation distance are

\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \int p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\, dx, \qquad \mathrm{TV}(p, q) = \frac12 \int |p(x) - q(x)|\, dx .

For laws \mu, \nu with finite second moments, the Wasserstein-2 distance is

W_2(\mu, \nu)^2 = \inf_{(X, Y)} \ \mathbb{E}\|X - Y\|^2 ,

the infimum running over all couplings: pairs of random vectors with X \sim \mu and Y \sim \nu.

Proposition 0.2 — Pinsker and data processing For all densities p, q, the divergence is non-negative and controls the total variation:

\mathrm{TV}(p, q) \le \sqrt{\tfrac12\, \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q)} .

For any measurable map F, applying it to both laws can only decrease the divergence:

\mathrm{KL}(F_\# p \,\|\, F_\# q) \le \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) .

Proof. Non-negativity is Jensen’s inequality for the convex function -\log. Pinsker’s inequality and the data-processing inequality are proved in Cover and Thomas (2006, ch. 2 and 11). ∎

The data-processing inequality is the step that turns a bound on whole trajectories into a bound on the generated images (Theorem 3.4).

0.2 Gaussian smoothing

Write \varphi_\sigma for the density of \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I):

\varphi_\sigma(u) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2}\, e^{-\|u\|^2 / (2\sigma^2)} .

Proposition 0.3 — Adding Gaussian noise Let X have law p and Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) be independent of X. For a, \sigma > 0, the vector Y = aX + \sigma Z has the density

p_Y(y) = \int p(x)\, \varphi_\sigma(y - a x)\, dx ,

which is infinitely differentiable and positive everywhere, even when p is neither (an empirical measure, for instance). If p = \mathcal{N}(m, C), then

Y \sim \mathcal{N}(a m,\ a^2 C + \sigma^2 I).

Proof. Condition on X = x: the law of Y is then \mathcal{N}(ax, \sigma^2 I), and integrating over x gives the formula. Differentiation under the integral is allowed because every derivative of \varphi_\sigma is bounded.

In the Gaussian case, Y is an affine image of the Gaussian vector (X, Z), with the stated mean and covariance. ∎

With a = 1 and \sigma^2 = 2t, the density of X + \sqrt{2t}\, Z solves the heat equation \partial_t p = \Delta p. It is the simplest instance of the Fokker–Planck equation of ch. 2.

0.3 The best estimator in mean square

Theorem 0.4 — Conditional expectation and the Wiener estimator Among all measurable functions f with \mathbb{E}\|f(Y)\|^2 < \infty, the conditional expectation minimises the mean square error:

Key result — the minimum mean square error (MMSE) estimator

\arg\min_f \ \mathbb{E}\|X - f(Y)\|^2 = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]

If X \sim \mathcal{N}(m, C) and Y = X + \sigma Z, the minimiser is linear and the posterior covariance does not depend on Y:

\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = m + C (C + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} (Y - m), \qquad \mathrm{Cov}[X \mid Y] = \sigma^2 C (C + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} .

Proof. Write g(Y) = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]. The error X - g(Y) is orthogonal to every square-integrable function of Y, hence

\mathbb{E}\|X - f(Y)\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\|X - g(Y)\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\|g(Y) - f(Y)\|^2 .

In the Gaussian case, X - m - K(Y - m) with K = C(C + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} is uncorrelated with Y. Being jointly Gaussian with Y, it is independent of Y, which gives both formulas.

The identity K = I - \sigma^2 (C + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} simplifies the covariance C - KC to the stated one. ∎

0.4 Integration by parts and Stein’s identity

Proposition 0.5 — Integration by parts in \mathbb{R}^d; Stein’s identity Let f be a differentiable function and v a differentiable vector field on \mathbb{R}^d such that f v vanishes at infinity fast enough to be integrable with its derivatives. Then

\int f(x)\, \nabla \cdot v(x)\, dx = -\int v(x) \cdot \nabla f(x)\, dx .

In particular, for Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) and a smooth v of moderate growth,

\mathbb{E}[Z \cdot v(Z)] = \mathbb{E}[\nabla \cdot v(Z)] .

Proof. The divergence theorem on a ball of radius R gives the identity up to a boundary term, which tends to zero as R \to \infty. For Stein’s identity, take f = \varphi_1 and use \nabla \varphi_1(z) = -z\, \varphi_1(z). ∎

This one identity produces the Fokker–Planck equation (ch. 2) and the score-matching losses (ch. 4).

0.5 Orthonormal bases

If (g_k)_{k \le d} is an orthonormal basis of \mathbb{R}^d, then every vector is the sum of its coefficients times the basis vectors, and norms are preserved (Parseval):

x = \sum_{k=1}^d \langle x, g_k \rangle\, g_k, \qquad \|x\|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^d \langle x, g_k \rangle^2 .

Proposition 0.6 — White noise in an orthonormal basis If Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I), its coefficients \langle Z, g_k \rangle in any orthonormal basis are independent \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) variables. Indeed, the coefficient vector is G^\top Z with G orthogonal, whose covariance is \sigma^2 G^\top G = \sigma^2 I.

White noise therefore looks the same in every basis, while an image has a few large coefficients in a well-chosen basis. This asymmetry is the principle of denoising by thresholding (ch. 5).

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • KL divergence controls total variation (Pinsker) and can only decrease under a common map (data processing).
  • Adding Gaussian noise makes any law smooth and positive; Gaussian laws stay Gaussian.
  • The best estimator in mean square is the conditional expectation; for Gaussian signals it is the linear Wiener estimator.
  • Integration by parts moves derivatives from a density to a test function; Stein’s identity is its Gaussian form.
  • White noise has independent coefficients of equal variance in every orthonormal basis.

1. Transport of probabilities and generation by transport

A generative model must produce new samples of an unknown law p, known only through examples x_1, \dots, x_n. The transport approach draws white noise Z and applies a map, X = T(Z). This chapter defines such maps, constructs them in dimension one and in general, and then replaces a single map by a flow, the form used in all later chapters.

1.1 Push-forward and change of variables

Definition 1.1 — Push-forward Let \gamma be a probability law on \mathbb{R}^d and T a measurable map from \mathbb{R}^d to itself.

The push-forward of \gamma by T is the law of the image T(Z) of a random vector Z of law \gamma:

T_\# \gamma(A) = \gamma\big(T^{-1}(A)\big), \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \int f \, d(T_\# \gamma) = \mathbb{E}\, f(T(Z)) .

The map T transports \gamma onto p when T_\# \gamma = p. Throughout, \gamma denotes the standard Gaussian law \mathcal{N}(0, I), the white noise.

Proposition 1.2 — Change of variables If T is a C^1 diffeomorphism and \gamma has density \gamma(z), then T_\# \gamma has the density

p(x) = \gamma\big(T^{-1}(x)\big)\, \big|\det \nabla T^{-1}(x)\big| ,

and along the map,

\log p(T(z)) = \log \gamma(z) - \log \big|\det \nabla T(z)\big| .

Proof. For a test function f, substitute x = T(z) in \mathbb{E} f(T(Z)) = \int f(T(z))\, \gamma(z)\, dz. The Jacobian of the inverse substitution gives the first formula. The second follows from \nabla T^{-1}(T(z)) = (\nabla T(z))^{-1}. ∎

Example 1.3 — Affine maps, and three families of generators The map T(z) = m + Az with A invertible transports \gamma onto \mathcal{N}(m, AA^\top), and \log|\det \nabla T| = \log|\det A| is constant.

The decoder of a variational autoencoder, the generator of a GAN and a normalising flow are all parametrised maps T_\theta trained so that T_{\theta\#}\gamma is close to the data law. Only the flow, which is invertible, gives access to the density through Proposition 1.2.

1.2 Monotone transport in dimension one

Theorem 1.4 — The monotone rearrangement Let \mu be a law on \mathbb{R} with continuous distribution function F_\mu, and \nu a law with quantile function F_\nu^{-1}(u) = \inf\{x : F_\nu(x) \ge u\}. The non-decreasing map

T = F_\nu^{-1} \circ F_\mu

transports \mu onto \nu. If both laws have finite second moments, it is optimal among all couplings:

\mathbb{E}\,|T(X) - X|^2 = W_2(\mu, \nu)^2, \qquad X \sim \mu .

Proof. Transport. Because F_\mu is continuous, U = F_\mu(X) is uniform on [0, 1]. For a uniform U,

\mathbb{P}\big(F_\nu^{-1}(U) \le x\big) = \mathbb{P}\big(U \le F_\nu(x)\big) = F_\nu(x),

so T(X) \sim \nu.

Optimality. For any coupling (X, Y) with the given marginals,

\mathbb{E}|X - Y|^2 = \mathbb{E}X^2 + \mathbb{E}Y^2 - 2\,\mathbb{E}[XY],

so one must maximise \mathbb{E}[XY]. Hoeffding’s formula writes, with H the joint distribution function,

\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \iint \big(H(x, y) - F_\mu(x) F_\nu(y)\big)\, dx\, dy .

Since H(x, y) \le \min(F_\mu(x), F_\nu(y)), the covariance is largest when equality holds everywhere. Equality holds for the pair (X, T(X)): both coordinates are non-decreasing functions of the same uniform variable U, so the events \{X \le x\} and \{T(X) \le y\} are nested. ∎

The code transports \mathcal{N}(0, 1) onto the two-component Gaussian mixture below, whose distribution function is inverted on a grid:

\nu = 0.3\, \mathcal{N}(-2, 0.5^2) + 0.7\, \mathcal{N}(1.5, 0.8^2) . 

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
w, m, s = np.array([0.3, 0.7]), np.array([-2.0, 1.5]), np.array([0.5, 0.8])
F = lambda t: (w * stats.norm.cdf((np.asarray(t)[..., None] - m) / s)).sum(axis=-1)

grid = np.linspace(-8, 8, 40001)
T = lambda u: np.interp(stats.norm.cdf(u), F(grid), grid)   # T = F^{-1} o Phi

z = rng.standard_normal(100_000)          # source sample, N(0, 1)
x = T(z)                                  # pushed-forward sample
print(f"KS distance to the target: {stats.kstest(x, F).statistic:.4f}")
print(f"mean {x.mean():.3f} (exact {(w * m).sum():.3f})")
print(f"cost E|T(Z) - Z|^2, monotone map : {np.mean((x - z) ** 2):.3f}")
print(f"cost of a random coupling        : {np.mean((rng.permutation(x) - z) ** 2):.3f}")

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance is 0.0019 and the mean 0.449 against 0.450. The monotone map moves the mass at cost 0.951, the independent coupling at cost 4.324:

W_2\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\ \nu\big)^2 \approx 0.95 .

1.3 Optimal transport in higher dimension

Theorem 1.5 — Brenier (1991) Let \mu and \nu be laws on \mathbb{R}^d with finite second moments, the first one having a density. There is a convex function \phi whose gradient transports \mu onto \nu, and this map is the unique optimal coupling:

W_2(\mu, \nu)^2 = \mathbb{E}\, \|\nabla \phi(X) - X\|^2, \qquad X \sim \mu .

Proof. See Villani (2003, Theorem 2.12) or Santambrogio (2015, ch. 1). The key step is Kantorovich duality: an optimal coupling is supported by the subdifferential of a convex function, which is single-valued almost everywhere for \mu because \mu has a density. ∎

In dimension one, gradients of convex functions are exactly the non-decreasing maps, and Theorem 1.4 is recovered. Between Gaussians the optimal map is affine, but in general it is the solution of a Monge–Ampère equation.

Estimating it from samples suffers from the curse of dimensionality. The empirical measure of n samples is at Wasserstein distance of order n^{-1/d} from the true law in dimension d \ge 3 (Fournier and Guillin, 2015). Diffusion models avoid computing an optimal map: they build some transport, not the optimal one, from denoisers (ch. 3).

1.4 Normalising flows

A normalising flow composes simple invertible maps, T = T_K \circ \dots \circ T_1, whose Jacobian determinants are cheap. By Proposition 1.2, the log-density is a sum:

\log p_\theta(x) = \log \gamma(z_0) - \sum_{k=1}^K \log \big|\det \nabla T_k(z_{k-1})\big|, \qquad z_k = T_k(z_{k-1}),\ z_K = x .

In a coupling layer (Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio, 2017), the vector is split as z = (z_a, z_b), and z_b is transformed by a scale and a shift computed from z_a:

T(z_a, z_b) = \big(z_a,\ z_b \odot e^{\alpha(z_a)} + \beta(z_a)\big) .

The Jacobian is triangular, and its log-determinant is the sum of the coordinates of \alpha(z_a); \alpha and \beta are arbitrary networks.

Proposition 1.6 — Maximum likelihood is a KL projection For a model density p_\theta, the expected negative log-likelihood under the data law p splits as

\mathbb{E}_{p}\big[-\log p_\theta(X)\big] = \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, p_\theta) + H(p),

where the entropy H(p) = -\int p \log p does not depend on \theta. Maximising the likelihood therefore minimises \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, p_\theta). The proof is one line: add and subtract \int p \log p.

Flows are exact but constrained: every layer must be invertible with a tractable determinant, which limits their expressiveness on images. The next section removes this constraint by letting time be continuous.

1.5 Flows of vector fields and the continuity equation

Let v(t, x) be a velocity field, regular enough for the ODE \dot x = v(t, x) to have a unique solution. Its flow map \Phi_t sends an initial point to its position at time t.

Theorem 1.7 — The continuity equation If X_0 \sim \rho_0 and X_t = \Phi_t(X_0), the densities \rho_t = \Phi_{t\#} \rho_0 solve

Key result — transport of a density by a velocity field

\partial_t \rho_t + \nabla \cdot (\rho_t\, v) = 0

in the weak sense, and along each trajectory the log-density changes by minus the divergence:

\frac{d}{dt} \log \rho_t(X_t) = -\nabla \cdot v(t, X_t) .

Proof. For a smooth test function f with compact support,

\frac{d}{dt} \int f \rho_t = \frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E} f(X_t) = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla f(X_t) \cdot v(t, X_t)\big] = \int \rho_t\, v \cdot \nabla f = -\int f\, \nabla \cdot (\rho_t v),

by Proposition 0.5. This is the weak form of the equation.

For the second claim, expand the total derivative along the trajectory, then divide the continuity equation by \rho_t:

\frac{d}{dt}\log\rho_t(X_t) = \partial_t \log \rho_t + v \cdot \nabla \log \rho_t, \qquad \partial_t \log\rho_t = -\nabla\cdot v - v \cdot \nabla \log \rho_t .

The two terms in v \cdot \nabla\log\rho_t cancel. ∎

Example 1.8 — A linear flow With v(t, x) = c\,x in dimension one, X_t = e^{ct} X_0. If X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), then \rho_t is the density of \mathcal{N}(0, e^{2ct}).

One checks directly that \partial_t \rho_t = -c\, \partial_x (x \rho_t). Along a trajectory, \log\rho_t(X_t) decreases at rate c, the divergence of v.

The converse direction drives the rest of the course. Choose first a path of densities \rho_t joining white noise to the data, then find a velocity v that solves the continuity equation for this path; integrating the ODE backwards then generates data. The velocity is never unique: adding w / \rho_t with \nabla \cdot w = 0 changes nothing. Diffusion models (ch. 3) choose the path made by adding noise, and stochastic interpolants (ch. 7) choose more general paths.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • A generative model is a map T with T_\# \gamma \approx p; the density of a push-forward follows from the change of variables formula.
  • In dimension one, the non-decreasing map F_\nu^{-1} \circ F_\mu transports \mu onto \nu at the optimal cost W_2^2.
  • In dimension d, the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function (Brenier), but it is hard to estimate from samples.
  • Normalising flows compose invertible layers with cheap determinants and are trained by maximum likelihood.
  • A velocity field transports densities according to the continuity equation; any path of densities can be realised by some velocity.

2. Fokker–Planck and Langevin equations

Noise added continuously in time is described by a stochastic differential equation (SDE), and the density of its solution obeys a partial differential equation, the Fokker–Planck equation. The Langevin equation is the SDE whose density converges to a prescribed law \pi: the first sampling algorithm that needs only the score \nabla \log \pi.

2.1 Brownian motion, SDEs and Itô’s formula

Definition 2.1 — Brownian motion and SDE A Brownian motion (W_t)_{t \ge 0} in \mathbb{R}^d starts at 0, has continuous paths and independent increments, and W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (t - s) I) for s < t.

Given a drift b(t, x) and a scalar diffusion coefficient g(t), the SDE

dX_t = b(t, X_t)\, dt + g(t)\, dW_t

is the limit, as h \to 0, of the Euler–Maruyama scheme

X_{k+1} = X_k + h\, b(t_k, X_k) + g(t_k) \sqrt{h}\, \xi_k, \qquad \xi_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \text{ i.i.d.}

When b is Lipschitz in x, the SDE has a unique strong solution (Øksendal, 2003, Theorem 5.2.1).

All diffusions of this course have a scalar coefficient g(t), which keeps the formulas short.

Theorem 2.2 — Itô’s formula For f \in C^2(\mathbb{R}^d) and X solution of the SDE above,

df(X_t) = \Big( b \cdot \nabla f + \frac{g^2}{2}\, \Delta f \Big)(t, X_t)\, dt + g(t)\, \nabla f(X_t) \cdot dW_t .

Taking expectations, the stochastic integral vanishes:

\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E} f(X_t) = \mathbb{E}\Big[ b \cdot \nabla f(X_t) + \frac{g^2}{2}\, \Delta f(X_t) \Big] .

Proof. Sketch. Expand f(X_{k+1}) - f(X_k) to second order along an Euler–Maruyama step. The increment \Delta X = h\, b + g\sqrt h\, \xi has second moment matrix g^2 h\, I + O(h^2).

The second-order term \frac12 \Delta X^\top \nabla^2 f\, \Delta X therefore contributes \frac{g^2}{2} \Delta f\, h on average, and its fluctuations vanish in the limit. Third-order terms are O(h^{3/2}). A rigorous proof is in Øksendal (2003, Theorem 4.2.1). ∎

2.2 The Fokker–Planck equation

Theorem 2.3 — Fokker–Planck If X_t has a smooth density p_t, then

Key result — the Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (p_t\, b) + \frac{g^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t

Equivalently, it is a continuity equation with the velocity b - \frac{g^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t:

\partial_t p_t + \nabla \cdot \Big( p_t \big( b - \tfrac{g^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t \big) \Big) = 0 .

Proof. Write the expectation in Theorem 2.2 as an integral against p_t, and move the derivatives from f to p_t with Proposition 0.5 (once for the drift, twice for the Laplacian):

\int f\, \partial_t p_t = \int p_t \Big( b \cdot \nabla f + \frac{g^2}{2} \Delta f \Big) = \int f \Big( -\nabla \cdot (p_t b) + \frac{g^2}{2} \Delta p_t \Big).

This holds for every test function f. The second form uses \Delta p_t = \nabla \cdot (p_t \nabla \log p_t). ∎

The second form is the bridge to ch. 1: the ODE \dot x = b - \frac{g^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t(x) has the same marginals p_t as the SDE, by Theorem 1.7. Its velocity requires the score \nabla \log p_t.

Proposition 2.4 — The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process The SDE dX_t = -X_t\, dt + \sqrt 2\, dW_t has the explicit solution

X_t = e^{-t} X_0 + \sqrt{2} \int_0^t e^{-(t - s)}\, dW_s \ \overset{\text{law}}{=}\ e^{-t} X_0 + \sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}}\; Z, \qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \text{ independent of } X_0 .

Its stationary law is \gamma = \mathcal{N}(0, I), and a Gaussian start X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2 I) gives

X_t \sim \mathcal{N}\big(e^{-t} m,\ (s^2 e^{-2t} + 1 - e^{-2t})\, I\big) .

Proof. Itô’s formula applied to e^t X_t gives d(e^t X_t) = \sqrt 2\, e^t\, dW_t. Integrate from 0 to t. The stochastic integral of a deterministic function is Gaussian, with variance

2 \int_0^t e^{-2(t - s)}\, ds = 1 - e^{-2t}

per coordinate (Itô isometry). The Gaussian case then follows from Proposition 0.3. ∎

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process forgets its initial condition exponentially fast. It is the forward noising process of ch. 3.

2.3 Langevin dynamics

Definition 2.5 — Langevin dynamics and relative Fisher information For a target density \pi > 0, the Langevin diffusion is

dX_t = \nabla \log \pi(X_t)\, dt + \sqrt 2\, dW_t .

The relative Fisher information of a density q with respect to \pi is

I(q \,\|\, \pi) = \int q \, \Big\| \nabla \log \frac{q}{\pi} \Big\|^2 .

The drift needs only the score: normalising constants of \pi disappear in \nabla \log \pi.

Theorem 2.6 — Stationarity and entropy decay The law \pi is stationary for the Langevin diffusion, and the divergence to \pi decreases at the rate of the Fisher information:

\frac{d}{dt}\, \mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, \pi) = -\, I(p_t \,\|\, \pi) \le 0 .

Suppose moreover that \pi satisfies a logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant \rho > 0:

\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, \pi) \le \frac{1}{2\rho}\, I(q \,\|\, \pi) \quad \text{for all densities } q .

Then the convergence is exponential:

\mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, \pi) \le e^{-2\rho t}\, \mathrm{KL}(p_0 \,\|\, \pi) .

Proof. With b = \nabla \log \pi and g = \sqrt 2, Theorem 2.3 reads

\partial_t p_t = \nabla \cdot \big( p_t\, \nabla \log (p_t / \pi) \big),

which vanishes when p_t = \pi. Differentiating under the integral, and using \int \partial_t p_t = 0,

\frac{d}{dt} \int p_t \log \frac{p_t}{\pi} = \int \partial_t p_t \, \log \frac{p_t}{\pi} = -\int p_t\, \Big\| \nabla \log \frac{p_t}{\pi} \Big\|^2,

by Proposition 0.5. Under the log-Sobolev inequality the derivative is at most -2\rho\, \mathrm{KL}, and Grönwall’s lemma concludes. ∎

By the Bakry–Émery criterion, a law \pi = e^{-U} with \nabla^2 U \succeq \rho I satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant \rho (Bakry, Gentil and Ledoux, 2014, ch. 5). For \pi = \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda I), \rho = 1/\lambda: the narrow directions converge fast and the wide ones slowly.

2.4 Unadjusted Langevin and its bias

The Euler–Maruyama discretisation of the Langevin diffusion is the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA):

x_{k+1} = x_k + h\, \nabla \log \pi(x_k) + \sqrt{2h}\, \xi_k .

Example 2.7 — ULA on a Gaussian For \pi = \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda) in dimension one, the iteration is x_{k+1} = (1 - h/\lambda)\, x_k + \sqrt{2h}\, \xi_k. It is stable if and only if |1 - h/\lambda| < 1, that is h < 2\lambda.

Its stationary variance v solves v = (1 - h/\lambda)^2 v + 2h, hence

v = \frac{\lambda}{1 - h/(2\lambda)} .

With \lambda = 1 and h = 0.5, ULA samples \mathcal{N}(0, 1.333) instead of \mathcal{N}(0, 1): the bias is of order h, and the step must be smaller than the smallest variance.

A Metropolis–Hastings correction removes the bias (MALA; Roberts and Tweedie, 1996). For strongly log-concave and smooth targets, non-asymptotic bounds show that ULA reaches accuracy \varepsilon in a number of steps polynomial in d and 1/\varepsilon (Dalalyan, 2017; Durmus and Moulines, 2017).

2.5 Metastability: why Langevin fails on multimodal laws

When \pi = e^{-U} has two modes separated by a barrier of height \Delta U, a Langevin trajectory spends long times in one mode before crossing. The Eyring–Kramers law, made rigorous by Bovier, Eckhoff, Gayrard and Klein (2004), gives a mean crossing time of order e^{\Delta U}, up to a prefactor fixed by the curvatures of U. The log-Sobolev constant is then exponentially small.

For the mixture \frac12 \mathcal{N}(-a, 1) + \frac12 \mathcal{N}(a, 1), the barrier is \Delta U = \log\big(p(a) / p(0)\big) \approx a^2/2 - \log 2 for a \ge 2. The code runs 200 ULA chains of horizon 500 for three separations.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

def score(x, a):        # grad log of the mixture 0.5 N(-a, 1) + 0.5 N(a, 1)
    return -x + a * np.tanh(a * x)

h, n_steps, n_chains = 0.01, 50_000, 200          # time horizon n_steps * h = 500
for a in [2.0, 3.0, 4.0]:
    x = np.full(n_chains, a)                      # every chain starts in the right mode
    side, switches = np.ones(n_chains), np.zeros(n_chains)
    for _ in range(n_steps):                      # ULA: x <- x + h score + sqrt(2h) xi
        x = x + h * score(x, a) + np.sqrt(2 * h) * rng.standard_normal(n_chains)
        new = np.where(np.abs(x) > a / 2, np.sign(x), side)   # count a switch at +-a/2
        switches += new != side
        side = new
    print(f"a = {a}: switches per unit time {switches.mean() / (n_steps * h):.4f}, "
          f"share of chains in the left mode at the end {np.mean(x < 0):.2f}")

lam, h = 1.0, 0.5                                  # bias of ULA on N(0, lam)
x = np.zeros(100_000)
for _ in range(200):
    x = x - h * x / lam + np.sqrt(2 * h) * rng.standard_normal(x.size)
print(f"ULA variance for h = {h}: {x.var():.3f} (formula {lam / (1 - h / (2 * lam)):.3f}, target {lam})")

The switching rates are 0.0703, 0.0075 and 0.0002 per unit time for a = 2, 3, 4. The barriers are 1.31, 3.81 and 7.31, so Kramers’ law predicts ratios e^{-2.5} = 0.082 and e^{-3.5} = 0.030 between consecutive rates; the measured ratios are 0.107 and 0.029.

For a = 4, only 10 % of the chains reach the left mode instead of 50 %. The last line checks Example 2.7: variance 1.334 for the predicted 1.333.

Diffusion models escape this trap. They never run a Langevin chain on the data law itself: they follow the path of noisy laws p_t, which is nearly Gaussian, hence unimodal, at large t.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • An SDE dX = b\, dt + g\, dW is the limit of Euler–Maruyama steps; Itô’s formula adds the term \frac{g^2}{2}\Delta f.
  • Densities of SDEs obey the Fokker–Planck equation, a continuity equation with velocity b - \frac{g^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t.
  • The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process maps any law to \mathcal{N}(0, I) exponentially fast, with explicit Gaussian transitions.
  • Langevin dynamics samples \pi using only its score; KL decays at the rate of the Fisher information, exponentially under a log-Sobolev inequality.
  • ULA is biased by O(h) and needs h below the smallest variance; on multimodal laws it is trapped for times of order e^{\Delta U}.

3. Score diffusion: generation by progressive denoising

Score diffusion turns the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process around. Noise is added to the data until nothing but white noise remains; the time-reversed process then removes it step by step and generates data. The reversal requires the scores of the noisy laws, and these scores are denoisers.

3.1 The forward noising process

Let X_0 \sim p_0 = p be the data law and run the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process of Proposition 2.4 up to a time T. Its marginal at time t is

X_t = a_t X_0 + \sigma_t Z, \qquad a_t = e^{-t}, \quad \sigma_t^2 = 1 - e^{-2t} .

We write p_t for its density, smooth by Proposition 0.3. Since \gamma = \mathcal{N}(0, I) satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with \rho = 1, Theorem 2.6 gives

\mathrm{KL}(p_T \,\|\, \gamma) \le e^{-2T}\, \mathrm{KL}(p_0 \,\|\, \gamma),

so a moderate T (5 to 10) makes p_T indistinguishable from white noise.

Other schedules are used in practice. The variance-exploding process X_t = X_0 + \sigma(t) Z adds noise without shrinking (Song and Ermon, 2019; Karras et al., 2022). The discrete chain of DDPM (Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020), with small variances \beta_k,

x_k = \sqrt{1 - \beta_k}\; x_{k-1} + \sqrt{\beta_k}\; \xi_k ,

is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with a time-dependent speed.

3.2 Time reversal

Theorem 3.1 — Reverse-time diffusion (Anderson, 1982) Let X solve dX_t = b(t, X_t)\, dt + g(t)\, dW_t on [0, T] with smooth marginals p_t. The reversed process Y_s = X_{T - s} is a diffusion:

dY_s = \Big( -b(T - s, Y_s) + g(T - s)^2\, \nabla \log p_{T - s}(Y_s) \Big)\, ds + g(T - s)\, dB_s ,

for a Brownian motion B. For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, the reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE with the same marginals are

Key result — the reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE

dY_s = \big( Y_s + 2\, \nabla \log p_{T-s}(Y_s) \big)\, ds + \sqrt 2\, dB_s, \qquad \frac{dy}{ds} = y + \nabla \log p_{T-s}(y)

Proof. Marginals. Set q_s = p_{T - s}. By Theorem 2.3, with b and g evaluated at T - s,

\partial_s q_s = \nabla \cdot (q_s b) - \frac{g^2}{2} \Delta q_s .

The Fokker–Planck equation of the proposed reverse SDE has drift \tilde b = -b + g^2 \nabla \log q_s. Using \nabla \cdot (q_s \nabla \log q_s) = \Delta q_s, its right-hand side is

-\nabla \cdot (q_s \tilde b) + \frac{g^2}{2} \Delta q_s = \nabla \cdot (q_s b) - g^2 \Delta q_s + \frac{g^2}{2} \Delta q_s = \nabla \cdot (q_s b) - \frac{g^2}{2} \Delta q_s .

So q_s solves the Fokker–Planck equation of the reverse SDE, and the marginals agree. The identity of the laws of whole paths is due to Anderson (1982) and Haussmann and Pardoux (1986).

Probability flow. By the second form of Theorem 2.3, the forward densities are transported by the velocity -x - \nabla \log p_t(x). Running this ODE backwards in time gives the stated equation (Song et al., 2021). ∎

The generative algorithm follows. Draw Y_0 \sim \gamma, which is close to p_T; integrate either equation from s = 0 to s = T; output Y_T, approximately distributed as p_0. The SDE injects fresh noise at every step, while the ODE is a deterministic transport map from noise to data, as in ch. 1.

3.3 The score is a denoiser: the Tweedie–Miyasawa formula

Theorem 3.2 — Tweedie–Miyasawa formula Let Y = aX + \sigma Z with Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) independent of X, and let p_Y be its density. Then the score of p_Y is an affine function of the MMSE denoiser:

\nabla \log p_Y(y) = \frac{a\, \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] - y}{\sigma^2} .

Equivalently, the best denoiser is read off the score:

\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] = \frac{y + \sigma^2\, \nabla \log p_Y(y)}{a} .

Proof. Differentiate the formula of Proposition 0.3 under the integral, using the identity

\sigma^2\, \nabla \varphi_\sigma(u) = -u\, \varphi_\sigma(u) .

This gives

\nabla p_Y(y) = \int p(x)\, \varphi_\sigma(y - ax)\, \frac{a x - y}{\sigma^2}\, dx .

Divide by p_Y(y). The ratio p(x)\, \varphi_\sigma(y - ax) / p_Y(y) is the conditional density of X given Y = y, so the right-hand side becomes (a\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] - y)/\sigma^2. ∎

Robbins (1956) attributes the formula to Tweedie, and Miyasawa (1961) published it in the Gaussian-noise form used here; Efron (2011) revived it in statistics. It turns the reverse SDE into a sequence of partial denoisings.

With D_t(y) = \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t = y], one reverse Euler step of size h at time t reads

y \ \leftarrow\ y + h\, y + \frac{2h}{\sigma_t^2} \big( a_t D_t(y) - y \big) + \sqrt{2h}\, \xi .

It moves the current point towards the rescaled denoised estimate a_t D_t(y), then adds a little fresh noise. Generation is progressive denoising.

Example 3.3 — Gaussian data If X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda) in dimension one, then p_t = \mathcal{N}(0, v_t) with v_t = \lambda a_t^2 + \sigma_t^2, and the score is -y / v_t. Theorem 3.2 gives

\mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t = y] = \frac{y - \sigma_t^2\, y / v_t}{a_t} = \frac{a_t \lambda}{a_t^2 \lambda + \sigma_t^2}\; y ,

which is the Wiener estimator of Theorem 0.4 for the observation X_t / a_t = X_0 + (\sigma_t / a_t) Z.

The code samples the mixture \nu of ch. 1.2. Its noisy versions p_t are explicit mixtures, so the exact score is available, and both reverse equations are integrated with 500 Euler steps. The last lines check Theorem 3.2 against a direct quadrature of the posterior mean.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from scipy.special import logsumexp

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
w, m, s = np.array([0.3, 0.7]), np.array([-2.0, 1.5]), np.array([0.5, 0.8])

def score(t, x):
    """Exact score of p_t, the law of e^{-t} X_0 + sqrt(1 - e^{-2t}) Z, X_0 ~ mixture."""
    mt, vt = np.exp(-t) * m, np.exp(-2 * t) * s**2 + 1 - np.exp(-2 * t)
    logc = np.log(w) - 0.5 * (x[:, None] - mt) ** 2 / vt - 0.5 * np.log(vt)
    r = np.exp(logc - logsumexp(logc, axis=1, keepdims=True))     # posterior weights
    return (r * (mt - x[:, None]) / vt).sum(axis=1)

T, n, N = 5.0, 500, 20_000
ts = np.linspace(T, 1e-3, n + 1)                 # reverse time grid
y_sde = rng.standard_normal(N)                   # start from N(0, 1), close to p_T
y_ode = y_sde.copy()
for k in range(n):
    t, h = ts[k], ts[k] - ts[k + 1]
    y_sde = y_sde + h * (y_sde + 2 * score(t, y_sde)) + np.sqrt(2 * h) * rng.standard_normal(N)
    y_ode = y_ode + h * (y_ode + score(t, y_ode))

F = lambda u: (w * stats.norm.cdf((np.asarray(u)[..., None] - m) / s)).sum(axis=-1)
print(f"exact weight of the left mode: {F(0.0):.3f}")
for name, y in [("reverse SDE", y_sde), ("probability-flow ODE", y_ode)]:
    print(f"{name:21s}: weight of the left mode {np.mean(y < 0):.3f}, "
          f"KS distance {stats.kstest(y, F).statistic:.4f}")

# Tweedie: E[X_0 | X_t = x] = (x + sigma_t^2 score(t, x)) / e^{-t}, checked by quadrature
t, x, g = 0.5, np.array([0.0, 1.0]), np.linspace(-8, 8, 16001)
a, v = np.exp(-t), 1 - np.exp(-2 * t)
post = (w * stats.norm.pdf((g[:, None] - m) / s) / s).sum(axis=1)[:, None] \
       * np.exp(-0.5 * (x - a * g[:, None]) ** 2 / v)            # p_0(x_0) p(x | x_0)
print("posterior mean by quadrature:", (g[:, None] * post).sum(0) / post.sum(0))
print("Tweedie formula             :", (x + v * score(t, x)) / a)

The left-mode weight is 0.321 exactly, 0.323 with the SDE and 0.318 with the ODE, with Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances 0.0055 and 0.0080. Both posterior means agree to all printed digits:

\mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_{0.5} = 0] = 0.483, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_{0.5} = 1] = 1.492 .

3.4 How accurate is the generated law?

In practice the score is replaced by an estimate s_\theta(t, x), and the reverse SDE starts from \gamma instead of p_T. Let q_0 be the law of the output.

Theorem 3.4 — Score error controls the sampling error Under regularity conditions on p and s_\theta, and for the continuous-time reverse SDE,

\mathrm{KL}(p_0 \,\|\, q_0) \ \le\ e^{-2T}\, \mathrm{KL}(p_0 \,\|\, \gamma) + \int_0^T \mathbb{E}\, \big\| s_\theta(t, X_t) - \nabla \log p_t(X_t) \big\|^2\, dt .

Proof. Sketch. Compare the laws of the whole reverse paths. By data processing (Proposition 0.2), the divergence between the final points is at most the divergence between the paths. By the chain rule for KL, the latter is the divergence between the initial points, \mathrm{KL}(p_T \,\|\, \gamma), plus the divergence between the path laws given the same start.

Both SDEs have diffusion \sqrt 2, and their drifts differ by 2(\nabla\log p_t - s_\theta). By Girsanov’s theorem, the path divergence is one quarter of the expected time integral of the squared drift difference, which is the integral above. The first term is bounded in §3.1. The technical conditions, and the extra error of the time discretisation, are handled by Chen et al. (2023). ∎

Two lessons follow. First, the quality of a diffusion model is governed by the mean square error of its score along the noising path — the very loss trained in ch. 4. Second, no log-Sobolev inequality for p is needed: unlike Langevin sampling, multimodality costs nothing once the scores are known. Chen et al. (2023) show that, with an accurate score, the number of steps grows only polynomially with the dimension.

3.5 Images and sound

For images, x is the vector of pixel values, and the denoiser D_\theta(x, t) is a convolutional U-Net that receives the noise level (Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020; Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021). Karras et al. (2022) showed how preconditioning the network by the noise level, and choosing the time grid, reduce the number of network evaluations from about a thousand to a few tens.

Latent diffusion runs the same process in the latent space of an autoencoder, which lowers the cost at high resolution (Rombach et al., 2022). The deterministic sampler DDIM (Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021) integrates the probability-flow ODE.

For sound, the signal is a waveform of tens of thousands of samples per second. DiffWave (Kong et al., 2021) and WaveGrad (Chen et al., 2021) generate raw waveforms with dilated convolutional denoisers conditioned on a spectrogram. Other systems diffuse a time–frequency representation and invert it afterwards.

In every case the algorithm is the one of §3.2: what changes is the denoiser architecture, the representation and the conditioning (ch. 6).

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process carries any data law to white noise; its marginals are a_t X_0 + \sigma_t Z.
  • The time-reversed process is a diffusion whose drift involves the score \nabla \log p_t; the probability-flow ODE has the same marginals.
  • Tweedie–Miyasawa: the score of a noisy law is (a\, \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] - Y)/\sigma^2, so reversing the diffusion is progressive denoising.
  • The KL error of the generated law is bounded by the integrated mean square error of the score, without any log-Sobolev assumption.
  • Images and sounds use the same sampler, with U-Net or dilated convolutional denoisers, in pixel, latent or waveform space.

4. Learning the score with deep networks; generalisation

The sampler of ch. 3 needs the scores \nabla \log p_t of a law known only through examples. This chapter shows that they can be learned by regression — training a denoiser — and then faces the central puzzle: the exact minimiser of the training loss only reproduces the training set, yet trained networks generate new data.

4.1 Score matching

The obvious loss \mathbb{E}_p \| s(X) - \nabla \log p(X) \|^2 involves the unknown score. Integration by parts removes it.

Theorem 4.1 — Score matching (Hyvärinen, 2005) For a smooth vector field s such that p\, s vanishes at infinity,

\mathbb{E}_p \big\| s(X) - \nabla \log p(X) \big\|^2 = \mathbb{E}_p \Big[ \|s(X)\|^2 + 2\, \nabla \cdot s(X) \Big] + \mathbb{E}_p \|\nabla \log p(X)\|^2 .

The last term does not depend on s, so s can be fitted with the first expectation, estimated on samples.

Proof. Expand the square. The cross term is

\mathbb{E}_p[s \cdot \nabla \log p] = \int s \cdot \nabla p = -\int p\, \nabla \cdot s

by Proposition 0.5. ∎

The divergence \nabla \cdot s costs d backward passes in dimension d, which is prohibitive for images, and the score of the clean data law may not even exist on a low-dimensional data set. Adding noise solves both problems.

4.2 Denoising score matching

Theorem 4.2 — Denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011) Let Y = aX + \sigma Z as in Theorem 3.2. For every vector field s with \mathbb{E}\|s(Y)\|^2 < \infty,

\mathbb{E} \big\| s(Y) - \nabla \log p_Y(Y) \big\|^2 = \mathbb{E} \Big\| s(Y) + \frac{Z}{\sigma} \Big\|^2 - C,

where C \ge 0 does not depend on s. With the denoiser D(y) = (y + \sigma^2 s(y))/a, the right-hand loss equals \frac{a^2}{\sigma^4}\, \mathbb{E}\|D(Y) - X\|^2.

Proof. Given X = x, the noisy vector has density \varphi_\sigma(y - ax), whose score is -(y - ax)/\sigma^2 = -Z/\sigma. Expanding both squares, the terms in \|s\|^2 agree. The cross terms agree too:

\mathbb{E}[s(Y) \cdot \nabla \log p_Y(Y)] = \int s \cdot \nabla p_Y = \iint p(x)\, s(y) \cdot \nabla_y \varphi_\sigma(y - ax)\, dx\, dy = \mathbb{E}\Big[ s(Y) \cdot \Big( -\frac{Z}{\sigma} \Big) \Big],

where \nabla p_Y was differentiated under the integral of Proposition 0.3. The remaining terms do not involve s. For the last claim,

a D(Y) - aX = Y - aX + \sigma^2 s(Y) = \sigma^2 \big( s(Y) + Z/\sigma \big) .

∎

Learning the score at noise level t is therefore exactly training an MMSE denoiser, whose minimiser is \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t] (Theorem 0.4), consistent with Tweedie–Miyasawa. All noise levels are trained jointly with one network. Writing the score as -\varepsilon_\theta(x, t) / \sigma_t, so that \varepsilon_\theta predicts the noise Z (Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020), the loss is

Key result — the denoising loss of diffusion models

\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, X_0,\, Z} \ \big\| \varepsilon_\theta(a_t X_0 + \sigma_t Z,\ t) - Z \big\|^2

The law of t weights the noise levels. By Theorem 3.4, it should not neglect the small noise levels, where the final details of the samples are decided (Karras et al., 2022).

4.3 Networks for the score

The network takes the noisy vector and the noise level as inputs. For images, it is a U-Net (Ronneberger, Fischer and Brox, 2015): a convolutional encoder that halves the resolution several times, a symmetric decoder, and skip connections between equal resolutions. It is translation-equivariant, local at fine scales and global at coarse ones — a multiscale architecture.

The code trains a small multilayer perceptron on the make_moons data set of scikit-learn (4000 points in the plane), with times drawn log-uniformly in [10^{-3}, 5] and the noise level given as \log t. It samples 2000 points with the reverse SDE of Theorem 3.1.

import numpy as np, torch
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons

torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)     # tiny model: one thread is fastest
data, _ = make_moons(n_samples=4000, noise=0.05, random_state=0)
mu, sd = data.mean(0), data.std(0)
X = torch.tensor((data - mu) / sd, dtype=torch.float32)

net = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(3, 128), torch.nn.SiLU(),
                          torch.nn.Linear(128, 128), torch.nn.SiLU(),
                          torch.nn.Linear(128, 128), torch.nn.SiLU(),
                          torch.nn.Linear(128, 2))            # eps_theta(x, log t)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, 3000)
T, eps = 5.0, 1e-3
for step in range(3000):                                     # denoising score matching
    x0 = X[torch.randint(0, len(X), (512,))]
    t = torch.exp(torch.empty(512, 1).uniform_(np.log(eps), np.log(T)))   # log-uniform times
    z = torch.randn_like(x0)
    xt = torch.exp(-t) * x0 + torch.sqrt(1 - torch.exp(-2 * t)) * z
    loss = ((net(torch.cat([xt, torch.log(t)], 1)) - z) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()

@torch.no_grad()
def sample(n, steps=500):                                    # reverse SDE, Euler-Maruyama
    ts = eps + (T - eps) * torch.linspace(1, 0, steps + 1) ** 2   # finer steps near t = 0
    y = torch.randn(n, 2)
    for k in range(steps):
        t, h = ts[k], ts[k] - ts[k + 1]
        s = -net(torch.cat([y, torch.log(t).expand(n, 1)], 1)) / torch.sqrt(1 - torch.exp(-2 * t))
        y = y + h * (y + 2 * s) + torch.sqrt(2 * h) * torch.randn_like(y)
    return y

clean, _ = make_moons(n_samples=20000, noise=0.0, random_state=1)
ref = torch.tensor((clean - mu) / sd, dtype=torch.float32)
dist = lambda A: torch.cdist(A, ref).min(1).values.median().item()
print(f"median distance to the noiseless moons: generated {dist(sample(2000)):.3f}, "
      f"training data {dist(X[:2000]):.3f}, Gaussian noise {dist(torch.randn(2000, 2)):.3f}")

The generated points lie at median distance 0.061 from the noiseless moons, against 0.050 for the data, which carry noise of their own, and 0.323 for white noise. Two choices matter here. Training times drawn uniformly in [0, 5] leave too few small noise levels, and the same network then reaches only 0.215. A uniform time grid in the sampler makes the last steps too coarse and gives 0.113.

4.4 Memorisation: what the exact minimiser does

Training uses N examples x_1, \dots, x_N, so the expectation over X_0 in the loss is an average over the empirical law \hat p = \frac1N \sum_i \delta_{x_i}.

Proposition 4.3 — The empirical minimiser memorises Over all measurable functions, the empirical denoising loss at time t is minimised by the score of the Gaussian mixture \hat p_t with components \mathcal{N}(a_t x_i, \sigma_t^2 I) and weights 1/N. Its denoiser is a softmax average of the training points:

\hat D_t(y) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i(y)\, x_i, \qquad w_i(y) = \frac{\exp\!\big(-\|y - a_t x_i\|^2 / 2\sigma_t^2\big)}{\sum_j \exp\!\big(-\|y - a_t x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_t^2\big)} .

The reverse SDE driven by these scores and started from \hat p_T outputs exactly \hat p: every sample is a training point.

Proof. By Theorem 4.2 applied to \hat p, the loss is minimised by the denoiser \mathbb{E}_{\hat p}[X_0 \mid X_t = y]. Bayes’ formula with the prior \hat p and the likelihood \varphi_{\sigma_t}(y - a_t x_i) gives the weights w_i.

The scores are then those of \hat p_t (Theorem 3.2). Theorem 3.1, applied to the forward process started from \hat p, shows that the reverse process ends with the law \hat p. ∎

The code runs the reverse SDE with the exact empirical scores for N = 10 and N = 100 training points, then applies one last denoising step.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import softmax
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
for N in [10, 100]:
    train, _ = make_moons(n_samples=N, noise=0.05, random_state=N)
    fresh, _ = make_moons(n_samples=500, noise=0.05, random_state=N + 1)

    def denoiser(t, x):                        # E[X_0 | X_t = x] under the empirical measure
        a, v = np.exp(-t), 1 - np.exp(-2 * t)
        r = softmax(-0.5 * ((x[:, None, :] - a * train) ** 2).sum(-1) / v, axis=1)
        return r @ train

    T, steps, eps = 5.0, 400, 1e-5
    ts = eps + (T - eps) * np.linspace(1, 0, steps + 1) ** 2
    y = rng.standard_normal((500, 2))
    for k in range(steps):                     # reverse SDE with the exact empirical score
        t, h = ts[k], ts[k] - ts[k + 1]
        score = (np.exp(-t) * denoiser(t, y) - y) / (1 - np.exp(-2 * t))   # Tweedie
        y = y + h * (y + 2 * score) + np.sqrt(2 * h) * rng.standard_normal(y.shape)
    y = denoiser(eps, y)                       # final denoising step
    nn = lambda A: np.median(np.sqrt(((A[:, None, :] - train) ** 2).sum(-1)).min(1))
    print(f"N = {N:3d}: distance to the nearest training point, generated {nn(y):.1e}, "
          f"fresh data {nn(fresh):.3f}")

For both sizes, the median distance from a generated point to the nearest training point is 0 to machine precision, while fresh data from the same law lie at 0.190 (N = 10) and 0.050 (N = 100). A perfect optimiser of the training loss is a copying machine.

4.5 Generalisation

A network generalises only because it does not reach the empirical minimiser: its architecture and its training impose an inductive bias. The question is whether that bias matches the data.

Without structure, the answer is pessimistic. For densities with \beta bounded derivatives in dimension d, the minimax rate of estimation from n samples, in squared L^2 error, is n^{-2\beta/(2\beta + d)} (Tsybakov, 2009).

For a 64 \times 64 image, d = 4096, and this rate is useless.

Kadkhodaie, Guth, Simoncelli and Mallat (2024) measured the transition on face images. They trained the same denoiser architecture on non-overlapping training sets of increasing size N. For small N the networks memorise: their samples are training images. For large N, two networks trained on disjoint sets generate nearly the same image from the same random seed. They have learned nearly the same score, independent of the particular examples: they generalise.

The transition occurs at training-set sizes far below what the generic rate would require. The explanation proposed by the authors is the subject of ch. 5: the inductive bias of these networks is a shrinkage in bases adapted to the geometry of images.

In practice, sample quality is measured by the Fréchet Inception Distance between features of real and generated images (Heusel et al., 2017). Memorisation is detected by searching the training set for near-duplicates of generated samples (Carlini et al., 2023; Somepalli et al., 2023).

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Score matching replaces the unknown score by a divergence term, through integration by parts.
  • Denoising score matching is a regression: learning the score at noise level t is training the MMSE denoiser \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t].
  • One network, given the noise level, is trained on all levels with the loss \mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_\theta - Z\|^2; small noise levels must be well represented.
  • The exact minimiser of the empirical loss is the score of a Gaussian mixture centred on the training points, and it only reproduces them.
  • Generalisation comes from the inductive bias of the network; it is observed with training sets far smaller than generic density estimation would need.

5. Interpreting the networks: denoising, sparsity and multiscale bases

A trained denoiser is a black box with millions of parameters. This chapter opens it with two exact tools — homogeneity and the Jacobian — and reads it as a shrinkage in an adaptive orthonormal basis. The classical theory of thresholding in wavelet bases then explains why such shrinkage works, and multiscale factorisations explain how to sample efficiently.

5.1 The Jacobian of a denoiser

A network is bias-free if all its affine layers are linear (no additive constants) and its non-linearities are ReLUs. Mohan, Kadkhodaie, Simoncelli and Fernandez-Granda (2020) showed that bias-free convolutional denoisers lose almost nothing in performance and generalise across noise levels.

Proposition 5.1 — Bias-free networks are adaptive linear filters A bias-free ReLU network D is positively homogeneous, D(\lambda y) = \lambda D(y) for \lambda > 0. Hence, wherever it is differentiable,

D(y) = \nabla D(y)\, y .

Proof. Linear layers and the ReLU commute with multiplication by \lambda > 0, and so does their composition. Differentiating D(\lambda y) = \lambda D(y) with respect to \lambda at \lambda = 1 gives \nabla D(y)\, y = D(y) (Euler’s identity). ∎

Locally, the denoiser is the linear filter \nabla D(y) applied to y; the filter changes with the image. For the ideal denoiser, the Jacobian has a statistical meaning.

Proposition 5.2 — Jacobian of the MMSE denoiser Let Y = X + \sigma Z and D(y) = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]. Then

\nabla D(y) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\, \mathrm{Cov}[X \mid Y = y],

a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix. Moreover the mean square error is \sigma^2 times the expected trace of the Jacobian:

\mathbb{E}\|X - D(Y)\|^2 = \sigma^2\, \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{tr}\, \nabla D(Y)\big] .

Proof. Write D = N / p_Y with N(y) = \int x\, p(x)\, \varphi_\sigma(y - x)\, dx. Differentiate under the integral; the gradient in y of \varphi_\sigma(y - x) is \varphi_\sigma(y - x)\, (x - y)/\sigma^2. This gives

\nabla N(y) = \frac{p_Y(y)}{\sigma^2} \big( \mathbb{E}[XX^\top \mid y] - D(y)\, y^\top \big), \qquad \nabla p_Y(y) = \frac{p_Y(y)}{\sigma^2} \big( D(y) - y \big).

The quotient rule then yields

\nabla D = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \Big( \mathbb{E}[XX^\top \mid y] - D\, y^\top - D\, (D - y)^\top \Big) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \Big( \mathbb{E}[XX^\top \mid y] - D D^\top \Big).

For the error, the mean square error of the conditional mean is the expected trace of the posterior covariance, and the first formula concludes:

\mathbb{E}\|X - D(Y)\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\, \mathrm{tr}\, \mathrm{Cov}[X \mid Y] .

∎

Diagonalise the symmetric Jacobian, \nabla D(y) = \sum_k \lambda_k(y)\, e_k(y) e_k(y)^\top. For a bias-free denoiser, Proposition 5.1 then gives

D(y) = \sum_k \lambda_k(y)\, \langle y, e_k(y) \rangle\, e_k(y) .

The denoiser shrinks the coefficients of y in an orthonormal basis (e_k(y)) adapted to y itself. The trace \sum_k \lambda_k(y) is an effective dimension: by Proposition 5.2, a small error requires that few directions survive.

Kadkhodaie, Guth, Simoncelli and Mallat (2024) computed these eigenvectors for trained image denoisers. They are oscillating patterns that follow the contours and stay regular inside smooth regions, which the authors call geometry-adaptive harmonic bases. The eigenvalues decay fast, so the effective dimension is small. This is their explanation of generalisation in ch. 4.5: the networks are biased towards sparse shrinkage in such bases, which suits images.

5.2 Thresholding in an orthonormal basis

Before adaptive bases, consider a fixed orthonormal basis (g_k). By Proposition 0.6, the observation y = f + \sigma z has coefficients y_k = f_k + \sigma z_k with independent z_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).

Proposition 5.3 — The projection oracle Among estimators that keep or discard each coefficient, \hat f = \sum_{k \in S} y_k g_k, the smallest risk is obtained by keeping exactly the coefficients above the noise level, S = \{k : |f_k| > \sigma\}, and equals

R_{\mathrm{or}}(f) = \sum_k \min(f_k^2, \sigma^2) .

Proof. Keeping coefficient k costs the noise variance \sigma^2; discarding it costs the bias f_k^2. The risk is a sum over k by Parseval, and each term is minimised separately. ∎

The oracle risk is small when f is sparse: few coefficients exceed \sigma. The oracle is not available, but a threshold nearly matches it.

Theorem 5.4 — Donoho and Johnstone (1994) Soft thresholding, \hat f_k = \mathrm{sign}(y_k)\, (|y_k| - \tau)_+, with the universal threshold \tau = \sigma \sqrt{2 \log d} satisfies

\mathbb{E}\|\hat f - f\|^2 \le (2 \log d + 1) \Big( \sigma^2 + \sum_k \min(f_k^2, \sigma^2) \Big) .

Proof. Key step. The maximum of d independent \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) variables exceeds \tau with probability at most 1/\sqrt{\pi \log d}, so pure-noise coefficients are killed with high probability. A one-dimensional risk bound for soft thresholding, summed over k, gives the result; see Donoho and Johnstone (1994) or Mallat (2009, ch. 11). ∎

A thresholding estimator is piecewise linear, and its Jacobian is the orthogonal projector \sum_{k \in S(y)} g_k g_k^\top onto the kept coefficients. It fits the form of §5.1 with eigenvalues 0 or 1 and a fixed basis. A trained denoiser goes further and adapts the basis to the image.

5.3 Wavelet bases and multiresolution

Definition 5.5 — Discrete Haar basis Consider signals of length d = 2^J, and a scale 2^j with 1 \le j \le J.

The Haar detail vector at scale 2^j and position n is supported by the 2^j samples starting at n 2^j. It equals 2^{-j/2} on the first half of them, -2^{-j/2} on the second half, and zero elsewhere.

These d - 1 vectors, with the constant vector d^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}, form an orthonormal basis. The fast transform computes, scale after scale, normalised sums and differences of neighbouring pairs in O(d) operations.

The coarse approximations at scales 2^j form a multiresolution: each one is the sum of the next coarser approximation and of the details at its scale.

Proposition 5.6 — Piecewise-constant signals are sparse in the Haar basis If f is constant except for K jumps, it has at most K J + 1 nonzero Haar coefficients, where J = \log_2 d. Its oracle risk is therefore at most \sigma^2 (K \log_2 d + 1), instead of d \sigma^2 for the noisy signal.

Proof. At a given scale, the supports of the detail vectors are disjoint, so each jump lies inside at most one of them. A detail vector whose support contains no jump sees a constant signal, and its coefficient is zero because the vector has zero mean. Summing over the J scales and adding the constant vector gives the count. Proposition 5.3 gives the risk. ∎

The code denoises a piecewise-constant signal with 11 jumps, d = 1024 and \sigma = 0.2, by hard thresholding at the universal threshold. It compares the result with the best linear low-pass filter, whose cut-off is chosen by an oracle that knows f.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
d, sigma = 1024, 0.2
u = np.arange(d) / d
jumps = [0.1, 0.13, 0.15, 0.23, 0.25, 0.4, 0.44, 0.65, 0.76, 0.78, 0.81]
heights = [4, -5, 3, -4, 5, -4.2, 2.1, 4.3, -3.1, 2.1, -4.2]
f = sum(h * (u > j) for j, h in zip(jumps, heights)) / 4    # piecewise constant, 11 jumps
y = f + sigma * rng.standard_normal(d)

def haar(x):                                   # orthonormal Haar transform, all scales
    c, out = x.copy(), []
    while len(c) > 1:
        out.append((c[0::2] - c[1::2]) / np.sqrt(2))   # details at this scale
        c = (c[0::2] + c[1::2]) / np.sqrt(2)           # coarser approximation
    return out + [c]

def ihaar(coefs):
    c = coefs[-1]
    for det in reversed(coefs[:-1]):
        x = np.empty(2 * len(c))
        x[0::2], x[1::2] = (c + det) / np.sqrt(2), (c - det) / np.sqrt(2)
        c = x
    return c

print("nonzero Haar coefficients of the clean signal:",
      sum(int((np.abs(c) > 1e-12).sum()) for c in haar(f)))
tau = sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.log(d))           # universal threshold
cy = haar(y)
kept = [np.abs(c) > tau for c in cy[:-1]] + [np.ones(1, bool)]
f_thr = ihaar([c * k for c, k in zip(cy, kept)])
print(f"noisy MSE {np.mean((y - f) ** 2):.5f}, Haar hard thresholding MSE "
      f"{np.mean((f_thr - f) ** 2):.5f} with {sum(int(k.sum()) for k in kept)} coefficients")

Y = np.fft.rfft(y)                              # best linear low-pass, oracle cut-off
err = [np.mean((np.fft.irfft(Y * (np.arange(Y.size) < K), d) - f) ** 2) for K in range(1, Y.size)]
print(f"best Fourier low-pass MSE {min(err):.5f} with the {int(np.argmin(err)) + 1} lowest frequencies")

The clean signal has 68 nonzero Haar coefficients, below the bound 11 \times 10 + 1 = 111. Thresholding keeps 45 coefficients, and its mean square error is about 0.6 times that of the best low-pass filter:

\text{noisy } 0.0404, \qquad \text{Haar thresholding } 0.0069, \qquad \text{best low-pass } 0.0120 .

A linear filter must choose one cut-off for the whole signal: it blurs the jumps or keeps noise in the flat parts. Thresholding adapts to the position of the jumps. In two dimensions, separable wavelets remain sparse for images with isolated singularities but not optimally so along regular contours; bases adapted to the geometry, such as curvelets or bandlets, do better (Mallat, 2009). The geometry-adaptive harmonic bases of §5.1 are the data-driven version of this idea.

5.4 Multiscale score diffusion

Natural images have a power spectrum decaying like |\omega|^{-2} (Field, 1987; Ruderman and Bialek, 1994). For an N \times N image, the covariance eigenvalues therefore range over a factor of order N^2. This ill-conditioning slows down the sampler.

Proposition 5.7 — Conditioning dictates the number of steps Let p = \mathcal{N}(0, \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_k)) with \lambda_{\min} \le \lambda_k \le 1. A reverse Euler–Maruyama step of size h at forward time t multiplies the k-th coordinate by

m_k(t) = 1 + h - \frac{2h}{v_k(t)}, \qquad v_k(t) = \lambda_k a_t^2 + \sigma_t^2 .

Because v_k(t) \to \lambda_k as t \to 0, the last steps are stable in every coordinate only if h \le 2\lambda_{\min} / (2 - \lambda_{\min}). A uniform grid on [0, T] then needs at least

n \ \ge\ \frac{T\,(2 - \lambda_{\min})}{2\, \lambda_{\min}} \ \approx\ \frac{T \kappa}{2}

steps, where \kappa = 1/\lambda_{\min} is the condition number of the covariance.

Proof. The score of p_t is -y_k / v_k(t) in coordinate k, so the step y \leftarrow y + h(y + 2\nabla \log p_t(y)) + \sqrt{2h}\,\xi multiplies y_k by m_k(t).

Stability requires m_k > -1, that is h (2/v_k - 1) < 2; near t = 0 this reads h < 2\lambda_k / (2 - \lambda_k). The smallest variance gives the binding constraint, and n = T/h. ∎

The stability bound is only necessary: accuracy requires steps well below it. The code computes the exact output law of the sampler for a Gaussian with spectrum \lambda_k = k^{-2}, k \le d, and finds the number of uniform steps that brings the KL divergence per coordinate below 10^{-3}. It compares with a white spectrum, and with the same spectrum after each octave 2^j \le k < 2^{j+1} is renormalised to unit maximal variance.

import numpy as np

def kl_per_dim(lam, n, T=8.0):
    """Exact output law of n reverse Euler-Maruyama steps for N(0, diag(lam)), exact score."""
    h, var = T / n, lam * np.exp(-2 * T) + 1 - np.exp(-2 * T)      # start exactly from p_T
    for k in range(n):
        t = T - k * h
        v = lam * np.exp(-2 * t) + 1 - np.exp(-2 * t)               # variance of p_t
        var = (1 + h - 2 * h / v) ** 2 * var + 2 * h                 # y <- y + h (y + 2 score) + noise
    r = var / lam
    return np.mean(0.5 * (r - 1 - np.log(r)))                       # KL per coordinate

def steps_needed(lam, tol=1e-3):
    n = 8
    while kl_per_dim(lam, n) > tol:
        n = int(1.2 * n) + 1
    return n

for d in [8, 16, 32]:
    k = np.arange(1, d + 1)
    lam = 1.0 / k**2                                  # spectrum 1/k^2: condition number d^2
    octave = 4.0 ** np.floor(np.log2(k))              # renormalise each octave [2^j, 2^(j+1))
    print(f"d = {d:2d}: steps for the 1/k^2 spectrum {steps_needed(lam):6d}, "
          f"white {steps_needed(np.ones(d)):3d}, octave-renormalised {steps_needed(lam * octave):3d}")
print("one coordinate of variance 1/4:", steps_needed(np.array([0.25])))

For d = 8, 16, 32, the k^{-2} spectrum needs 4640, 16 635 and 71 537 steps, roughly a factor 4 each time d doubles, as \kappa = d^2 predicts. The white spectrum needs 68 steps whatever d.

After renormalisation, every eigenvalue lies in [1/4, 1]. The counts 207, 249 and 299 never exceed the 518 steps that a single coordinate of variance 1/4 requires, the worst case of that interval.

This is the principle of wavelet score-based generative modelling (Guth, Coste, De Bortoli and Mallat, 2022). An orthonormal wavelet transform splits the image x into a coarse approximation x_J and details \bar x_j at each scale. Since the map from x_{j-1} to (x_j, \bar x_j) is orthogonal, the chain rule of probability gives an exact factorisation:

Key result — multiscale factorisation of a probability density

p(x) = p(x_J) \prod_{j=1}^{J} p\big(\bar x_j \mid x_j\big)

Each conditional law p(\bar x_j \mid x_j) is sampled by its own score diffusion, conditioned on the coarser image (ch. 6), on normalised wavelet coefficients. Within one scale, the spectrum varies by a bounded factor, as in the renormalised experiment above. Guth and coauthors prove, for Gaussian stationary fields, that the number of steps per scale is then bounded independently of the image size, and they observe the same gain on images and on fields of statistical physics.

The factorisation across scales is also the structure of the renormalisation group of statistical physics. Marchand, Ozawa, Biroli and Mallat (2023) estimate the energies of these conditional laws with low-dimensional models, scale by scale, to estimate and sample fields of statistical physics.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • A bias-free denoiser satisfies D(y) = \nabla D(y)\, y: it is a linear filter that adapts to the input.
  • The Jacobian of the MMSE denoiser is the posterior covariance divided by \sigma^2; its trace, the effective dimension, gives the error.
  • Trained denoisers shrink coefficients in geometry-adaptive orthonormal bases; thresholding in a fixed basis is the classical special case.
  • The projection oracle costs \sum_k \min(f_k^2, \sigma^2), and thresholding at \sigma\sqrt{2\log d} reaches it up to 2 \log d + 1; sparse signals are denoised well.
  • Wavelets make piecewise-regular signals sparse; the wavelet factorisation p(x_J)\prod_j p(\bar x_j \mid x_j) makes each sampling problem well conditioned, so the number of steps per scale stays bounded.

6. Conditional generation

Most applications generate data given complementary information y: a class label, a text, a coarser image, or a measurement such as a blurred or incomplete image. This chapter shows that conditioning is Bayes’ rule written on scores, and studies the two ways it is used in practice: guidance and posterior sampling for inverse problems.

6.1 Conditional scores

Proposition 6.1 — Bayes’ rule on scores Let (X_0, Y) be a pair of random variables and X_t = a_t X_0 + \sigma_t Z the noised version of X_0, with Z independent of (X_0, Y). The conditional score splits into a prior score and a likelihood score:

\nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid y) = \nabla_x \log p_t(x) + \nabla_x \log p_t(y \mid x) .

Moreover, the denoising loss with the label as an extra input, \mathbb{E}\| \varepsilon_\theta(X_t, t, Y) - Z \|^2, is minimised by the conditional score -\sigma_t \nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid y).

Proof. Bayes’ formula gives p_t(x \mid y) = p_t(x)\, p_t(y \mid x) / p(y), and p(y) does not depend on x. For the loss, apply Theorem 4.2 to the conditional law of X_0 given Y = y, for each y. ∎

Two strategies follow. The first trains one network on pairs (x, y), the second combines an unconditional score with a likelihood term. Classifier guidance (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021) takes the likelihood from a classifier p_\phi(y \mid x_t) trained on noisy inputs, and adds its gradient to the score.

6.2 Classifier-free guidance

Definition 6.2 — Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2021) One network s_\theta(x, t, y) is trained with the label replaced by a null token a fraction of the time, so that it learns both the conditional score and the unconditional score s_\theta(x, t, \varnothing). Sampling uses, for a guidance weight w \ge 0,

s_w(x, t, y) = (1 + w)\, s_\theta(x, t, y) - w\, s_\theta(x, t, \varnothing) .

Proposition 6.3 — What guidance targets With exact scores, the guided score is the conditional score plus w times the likelihood score:

s_w(x, t, y) = \nabla \log p_t(x \mid y) + w\, \nabla \log p_t(y \mid x) .

At t = 0 this is the score of the tilted law \pi_w(x) \propto p(x \mid y)\, p(y \mid x)^w, which concentrates on inputs the classifier finds typical of y. For t > 0, however, s_w is in general not the score of the noised tilted law.

Proof. The first identity is Proposition 6.1 applied to \nabla \log p_t(x \mid y) - \nabla \log p_t(x). At t = 0, the score of \pi_w is \nabla \log p(x \mid y) + w \nabla \log p(y \mid x) by definition.

For t > 0, the score of the noised tilted law is \mathbb{E}[\nabla \log \pi_w(X_0) \mid X_t = x] / a_t, an average computed under \pi_w. The guided score instead contains w \nabla \log p_t(y \mid x), the gradient of the logarithm of an average. Powers and averages do not commute, so the two differ in general; the code below shows a case. ∎

The code uses two overlapping classes, \mathcal{N}((\mp 1, 0), 0.8^2 I) with equal weights, for which every score is explicit. It samples class 1 with guidance weights 0, 1 and 4, and compares with the tilted law \pi_w computed by quadrature on the first coordinate.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
means = np.array([[-1.0, 0.0], [1.0, 0.0]])      # class 0 and class 1, overlapping
labels, wts, s2 = np.array([0, 1]), np.array([0.5, 0.5]), 0.64   # each class N(mean, 0.8^2 I)

def score(t, x, comps):
    """Exact score of p_t for the mixture restricted to `comps` (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noising)."""
    a, v = np.exp(-t), np.exp(-2 * t) * s2 + 1 - np.exp(-2 * t)
    mu = a * means[comps]
    logc = np.log(wts[comps]) - 0.5 * ((x[:, None, :] - mu) ** 2).sum(-1) / v
    r = np.exp(logc - logsumexp(logc, axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return (r[:, :, None] * (mu - x[:, None, :])).sum(1) / v

def guided_sample(y, w, n=4000, T=6.0, steps=400):
    ts = T * np.linspace(1, 0, steps + 1) ** 2 + 1e-3
    x = rng.standard_normal((n, 2))
    cond, uncond = np.flatnonzero(labels == y), np.arange(len(means))
    for k in range(steps):
        t, h = ts[k], ts[k] - ts[k + 1]
        s = (1 + w) * score(t, x, cond) - w * score(t, x, uncond)   # classifier-free guidance
        x = x + h * (x + 2 * s) + np.sqrt(2 * h) * rng.standard_normal(x.shape)
    return x

conf = lambda u: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * u / s2))     # exact p(y = 1 | x), x_1 = u
u = np.linspace(-6, 8, 20001)                      # quadrature for the tilted law at t = 0
for w in [0.0, 1.0, 4.0]:
    x = guided_sample(y=1, w=w)
    tilt = np.exp(-0.5 * (u - 1) ** 2 / s2) * conf(u) ** w      # p(x | 1) p(1 | x)^w
    tilt /= tilt.sum()
    print(f"w = {w}: guided x_1 mean {x[:, 0].mean():.2f}, std {x[:, 0].std():.2f}, "
          f"p(1|x) {conf(x[:, 0]).mean():.3f} | tilted law: mean {(u * tilt).sum():.2f}, "
          f"p(1|x) {(conf(u) * tilt).sum():.3f}")

Without guidance the sampler reproduces the class law: mean 0.99 and standard deviation 0.81 for the first coordinate, average classifier confidence 0.843 against 0.847 exactly. With w = 4, the samples move away from the other class (mean 1.76), lose diversity (standard deviation 0.60) and become more typical (confidence 0.984).

The tilted law \pi_4 has mean 1.37 and confidence 0.954 only: guidance overshoots the law it is usually said to sample. In image generation, moderate guidance trades diversity for fidelity to the prompt, which is why it is used with text conditioning.

6.3 Inverse problems

In an inverse problem, y = A x_0 + \sigma_y n with a known linear operator A (blur, mask, subsampling) and n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I). A diffusion model of the prior p(x_0) can sample the posterior p(x_0 \mid y) with the scores of Proposition 6.1. The prior score is the trained network; the difficulty is the likelihood of the noisy state,

p_t(y \mid x_t) = \int p(y \mid x_0)\, p(x_0 \mid x_t)\, dx_0 ,

which averages the likelihood over the unknown clean image.

Proposition 6.4 — Gaussian prior: exact likelihood of the noisy state If x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, C), the posterior p(x_0 \mid x_t) is Gaussian with mean D_t(x_t), the Wiener estimator, and covariance

\Sigma_t = \Big( C^{-1} + \frac{a_t^2}{\sigma_t^2} I \Big)^{-1} ,

and the likelihood of the noisy state is exactly

p_t(y \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}\big(y;\ A D_t(x_t),\ \sigma_y^2 I + A \Sigma_t A^\top\big) .

Proof. Theorem 0.4 applied to the observation x_t / a_t = x_0 + (\sigma_t / a_t) Z gives the Gaussian posterior. The covariance C - C(C + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{a_t^2} I)^{-1} C simplifies to the inverse above.

Given x_t, y = A x_0 + \sigma_y n is an affine function of the Gaussian vector x_0 plus independent noise, with mean A D_t(x_t) and covariance A \Sigma_t A^\top + \sigma_y^2 I. ∎

Diffusion posterior sampling (Chung et al., 2023) approximates p(x_0 \mid x_t) by a point mass at the denoised estimate D_t(x_t), which is available from Tweedie’s formula. It thus uses \mathcal{N}(y;\ A D_t(x_t),\ \sigma_y^2 I) and drops the term A \Sigma_t A^\top.

Proposition 6.4 shows what this costs. At small t, \Sigma_t \to 0 and the approximation is exact. At large t, the dropped term is of the size of the prior covariance, so the likelihood is far too sharp and pulls the early iterations too hard towards the measurements (Exercise 14).

Kadkhodaie and Simoncelli (2021) proposed another route for linear inverse problems. Their algorithm alternates denoising steps of the prior’s denoiser with projections onto the measurement constraints, and uses no separate likelihood network.

6.4 Other kinds of conditioning

  • Text. Text-to-image models feed an embedding of the prompt to the denoiser through cross-attention layers, train with pairs of images and captions, and sample with classifier-free guidance (Rombach et al., 2022).
  • Coarse scales. In the multiscale factorisation of ch. 5.4, each conditional law p(\bar x_j \mid x_j) is a conditional generation problem whose information is the coarser image; super-resolution is the same problem.
  • Physical parameters and measurements. For scientific data, y can be a parameter of the physical model or sparse observations. Conditional generation then produces an ensemble of fields compatible with them, which is the basis of the forecasts of ch. 7.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Conditioning adds a likelihood score to the prior score; a network given the label as input learns the conditional score directly.
  • Classifier-free guidance extrapolates between conditional and unconditional scores; it targets the tilted law p(x \mid y)\, p(y \mid x)^w only at t = 0, and in practice trades diversity for fidelity.
  • For inverse problems, the likelihood of a noisy state averages over the clean image; for a Gaussian prior it is exact and explicit.
  • Diffusion posterior sampling replaces that average by the denoised estimate: exact at small noise, overconfident at large noise.

7. Stochastic interpolants and the prediction of chaotic systems

Diffusion models transport white noise to data. Stochastic interpolants transport any law to any other, for instance the present state of a physical system to its future state. This chapter builds them from the continuity equation of ch. 1 and applies them to the probabilistic forecast of a chaotic system, the problem of weather prediction.

7.1 Stochastic interpolants

Definition 7.1 — Stochastic interpolant (Albergo, Boffi and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023) Let (x_0, x_1) be drawn from a coupling of two laws \rho_0 and \rho_1, and z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) be independent of them. For t \in [0, 1], set

x_t = \alpha(t)\, x_0 + \beta(t)\, x_1 + \gamma(t)\, z ,

with smooth coefficients such that \alpha(0) = \beta(1) = 1, \alpha(1) = \beta(0) = 0 and \gamma(0) = \gamma(1) = 0. Write \rho_t for the law of x_t, and its time derivative as

\dot x_t = \dot\alpha(t)\, x_0 + \dot\beta(t)\, x_1 + \dot\gamma(t)\, z .

The interpolant is a path of laws from \rho_0 to \rho_1, defined by sampling and not by an equation. The next theorem finds a velocity that realises it.

Theorem 7.2 — The velocity of an interpolant Define b(t, x) = \mathbb{E}[\dot x_t \mid x_t = x]. Then \rho_t solves the continuity equation

\partial_t \rho_t + \nabla \cdot (\rho_t\, b) = 0 ,

so the ODE \dot X = b(t, X) started from X_0 \sim \rho_0 gives X_1 \sim \rho_1, provided it has unique solutions. Moreover b is the minimiser of the regression loss

\mathcal{L}(v) = \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}\, \big\| v(t, x_t) - \dot x_t \big\|^2\, dt .

Proof. For a test function f, by the tower property of conditional expectation,

\frac{d}{dt} \mathbb{E} f(x_t) = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla f(x_t) \cdot \dot x_t\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla f(x_t) \cdot b(t, x_t)\big] = \int \rho_t\, b \cdot \nabla f ,

which is the weak form of the continuity equation, as in Theorem 1.7. The ODE transports \rho_0 along a solution of the same equation, and uniqueness of that solution gives \rho_t at every time. The regression statement is Theorem 0.4 applied for each t. ∎

When \gamma > 0 on (0, 1), the law \rho_t is smooth, and the same argument as in Theorem 3.2 gives its score:

\nabla \log \rho_t(x) = -\frac{\mathbb{E}[z \mid x_t = x]}{\gamma(t)} .

Proposition 7.3 — A family of SDEs with the same marginals For every \varepsilon(t) \ge 0, the SDE

dX_t = \big( b(t, X_t) + \varepsilon(t)\, \nabla \log \rho_t(X_t) \big)\, dt + \sqrt{2 \varepsilon(t)}\, dW_t

started from \rho_0 has the marginals \rho_t.

Proof. By Theorem 2.3, the right-hand side of its Fokker–Planck equation is

-\nabla \cdot (\rho\, b) - \varepsilon\, \nabla \cdot (\rho \nabla \log \rho) + \varepsilon\, \Delta \rho = -\nabla \cdot (\rho\, b),

because \rho \nabla \log \rho = \nabla \rho. This is the continuity equation of Theorem 7.2. ∎

Example 7.4 — Two familiar special cases Take x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) independent of the data x_1, \gamma = 0, and \alpha(t) = \cos(\pi t / 2), \beta(t) = \sin(\pi t / 2).

Since \alpha^2 + \beta^2 = 1, the law of x_t is that of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck marginal a_\tau X_0 + \sigma_\tau Z for the time \tau with a_\tau = \beta(t). The ODE of Theorem 7.2 is then the probability-flow ODE of ch. 3, after a change of time.

With \alpha(t) = 1 - t and \beta(t) = t, the interpolant moves on straight lines: this is flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) or rectified flow (Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023).

Interpolants thus contain diffusion models, but they also allow a coupling between x_0 and x_1 given by the data. That is what forecasting needs.

7.2 Chaos and probabilistic forecasts

The Lorenz-63 system (Lorenz, 1963) is a three-variable model of convection:

\dot x = 10\,(y - x), \qquad \dot y = x\,(28 - z) - y, \qquad \dot z = x y - \tfrac83\, z .

Its trajectories converge to a strange attractor, on which nearby trajectories separate exponentially, at a rate given by the largest Lyapunov exponent, about 0.9. An observation error of size \delta therefore grows to the size of the attractor after a time of order \log(1/\delta)/0.9. Beyond that horizon, a single deterministic forecast is meaningless.

The meaningful target is the conditional law of the future state given the observations, p(x_{t + \tau} \mid y_t), represented by an ensemble of forecasts. A forecast trained with the mean square error converges to the conditional mean (Theorem 0.4), which blurs the possible futures together. Ensembles are compared with proper scoring rules.

Proposition 7.5 — The CRPS is a proper scoring rule For a forecast distribution function F on \mathbb{R} and an outcome x, the continuous ranked probability score is

\mathrm{CRPS}(F, x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \big( F(u) - \mathbf{1}[x \le u] \big)^2\, du = \mathbb{E}|X - x| - \tfrac12\, \mathbb{E}|X - X'| ,

where X, X' are independent draws from F. If the outcome is drawn from G, the expected score is minimised by F = G.

Proof. For x \sim G and each u,

\mathbb{E}\big(F(u) - \mathbf{1}[x \le u]\big)^2 = (F(u) - G(u))^2 + G(u)(1 - G(u)) .

Integrating in u, the first term vanishes only for F = G, and the second does not depend on F. The second expression of the CRPS is obtained by writing both terms as integrals of distribution functions (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). ∎

A forecaster cannot improve its expected CRPS by reporting anything but its true belief. For a deterministic forecast, the CRPS reduces to the absolute error, so ensembles and point forecasts are compared on the same scale.

7.3 Forecasting with a conditional interpolant

We observe the present state of Lorenz-63 with noise, y = x + 2\, \eta, \eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), and forecast the state x_1 at lead time \tau = 0.5. In normalised units, the interpolant starts from x_0 = y + 0.5\, \xi and ends at x_1:

x_s = (1 - s)\, x_0 + s\, x_1, \qquad \dot x_s = x_1 - x_0 .

Given y, the start and the end are independent, so Theorem 7.2, applied conditionally on y, says that the ODE with velocity b(s, x, y) transports \mathcal{N}(y, 0.25 I) onto p(x_1 \mid y). A network learns b by regression, and each forecast is an ensemble of 50 ODE solutions.

import numpy as np, torch

rng = np.random.default_rng(5); torch.manual_seed(5); torch.set_num_threads(1)

def lorenz_flow(x, tau, dt=0.01):                     # RK4 for Lorenz-63, vectorised
    f = lambda x: np.stack([10 * (x[:, 1] - x[:, 0]), x[:, 0] * (28 - x[:, 2]) - x[:, 1],
                            x[:, 0] * x[:, 1] - 8 / 3 * x[:, 2]], 1)
    for _ in range(int(round(tau / dt))):
        k1 = f(x); k2 = f(x + dt / 2 * k1); k3 = f(x + dt / 2 * k2); k4 = f(x + dt * k3)
        x = x + dt / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)
    return x

x = lorenz_flow(rng.uniform([-20, -25, 5], [20, 25, 45], (12_000, 3)), 10.0)   # on the attractor
tau, s_obs = 0.5, 2.0
x1 = lorenz_flow(x, tau)                              # future state
y = x + s_obs * rng.standard_normal(x.shape)          # noisy observation of the present
m, sd = x.mean(0), x.std(0)
Y, X1 = [torch.tensor((a - m) / sd, dtype=torch.float32) for a in (y, x1)]
ntr = 10_000

net = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(7, 128), torch.nn.SiLU(), torch.nn.Linear(128, 128),
                          torch.nn.SiLU(), torch.nn.Linear(128, 3))     # velocity b(s, x_s, y)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for step in range(3000):                              # regression of the interpolant velocity
    i = torch.randint(0, ntr, (512,))
    x0 = Y[i] + 0.5 * torch.randn(512, 3)             # base point: observation + noise
    s = torch.rand(512, 1)
    xs = (1 - s) * x0 + s * X1[i]                     # interpolant between x0 and the future
    loss = ((net(torch.cat([s, xs, Y[i]], 1)) - (X1[i] - x0)) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

@torch.no_grad()
def forecast(yc, n_ens=50, steps=50):                 # Euler on dx/ds = b(s, x, y)
    yy = yc.repeat_interleave(n_ens, 0)
    z = yy + 0.5 * torch.randn_like(yy)
    for k in range(steps):
        s = torch.full((len(z), 1), k / steps)
        z = z + net(torch.cat([s, z, yy], 1)) / steps
    return z.view(len(yc), n_ens, 3)

def crps(ens, obs):                                   # ensemble CRPS, averaged over coordinates
    e1 = (ens - obs[:, None]).abs().mean(1)
    e2 = (ens[:, :, None] - ens[:, None, :]).abs().mean((1, 2))
    return (e1 - 0.5 * e2).mean().item()

te = slice(ntr, ntr + 1000)
ens = forecast(Y[te])
clim = X1[torch.randint(0, ntr, (1000, 50))]          # climatology ensemble
pers = Y[te][:, None]                                 # persistence: forecast = observation
print(f"CRPS (normalised units): generative {crps(ens, X1[te]):.3f}, "
      f"persistence {crps(pers, X1[te]):.3f}, climatology {crps(clim, X1[te]):.3f}")
print(f"ensemble spread {ens.std(1).mean():.3f}, error of the ensemble mean "
      f"{(ens.mean(1) - X1[te]).pow(2).mean().sqrt():.3f}")

On 1000 test cases, the generative ensemble has a CRPS of 0.302, against 0.560 for climatology (random states of the attractor) and 1.136 for persistence (the observation itself). Its spread, 0.553, is a little below the error of its mean, 0.672: the ensemble is slightly under-dispersed, a common defect that calibration diagnostics detect (Exercise 17).

Chen et al. (2024) start the forecast from a point mass at the current state instead. An ODE would then give a single trajectory, so they generate with an SDE of the kind of Proposition 7.3, which they relate to a Föllmer process; its diffusion term creates the spread of the ensemble.

7.4 Weather and climate

Machine-learned weather models are trained on decades of reanalysis data. GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023), a graph neural network trained with a mean square error, forecasts the medium range more accurately than the leading physical model on most targets. Its forecasts become smooth at long lead times, as Theorem 0.4 predicts for a conditional mean.

GenCast (Price et al., 2025) is a conditional diffusion model: each step samples the next state given the two previous ones. It produces 15-day global ensemble forecasts that are more skilful than the operational ensemble of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts on most of the evaluated targets, including extreme events.

Stochastic interpolants and diffusion models thus bring the tools of this course — scores, denoisers, transport, scoring rules — to the oldest probabilistic forecasting problem in physics.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • A stochastic interpolant \alpha x_0 + \beta x_1 + \gamma z joins two laws; its velocity \mathbb{E}[\dot x_t \mid x_t] is learned by regression and transports one law to the other.
  • A whole family of SDEs, with drift b + \varepsilon \nabla \log \rho_t, shares the same marginals; diffusion models and flow matching are special cases.
  • Chaos makes single forecasts useless beyond a few Lyapunov times; the target is the conditional law of the future, represented by an ensemble.
  • The CRPS is a proper scoring rule: it compares ensembles and point forecasts honestly.
  • A conditional interpolant gives sharp, nearly calibrated ensembles on Lorenz-63; diffusion models such as GenCast now produce operational-quality weather ensembles.

8. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study and as background for the research presentations that follow each lecture. It surveys active questions in neutral terms; every work cited is listed in the references.

8.1 Convergence theory of diffusion samplers

Theorem 3.4 bounds the error by the score error in continuous time. Chen, Chewi, Li, Li, Salim and Zhang (2023) added the time discretisation and showed that, with an L^2-accurate score, the number of steps grows polynomially in the dimension, without log-concavity. Benton, De Bortoli, Doucet and Deligiannidis (2024) obtained bounds nearly linear in the dimension under minimal assumptions.

These results take the score error as given. Open problem: bound the score error achievable by a network trained on n samples of a structured law, such as images, in terms that do not degrade exponentially with the dimension.

8.2 Memorisation, generalisation and creativity

Proposition 4.3 shows that perfect training memorises. Carlini et al. (2023) and Somepalli et al. (2023) extracted near-copies of training images from large trained models, so memorisation is a practical issue for privacy and copyright.

Kadkhodaie, Guth, Simoncelli and Mallat (2024) located the transition to generalisation and related it to geometry-adaptive harmonic bases (ch. 4.5, 5.1). Biroli, Bonnaire, De Bortoli and Mézard (2024) analysed diffusion in high dimension with statistical physics. They identify a time at which the trajectory commits to one class of the data (“speciation”) and a later time at which it may collapse onto a training point.

Open problems: characterise mathematically the inductive bias of convolutional U-Nets, and predict the training-set size at which a given architecture stops memorising a given data law.

8.3 Multiscale models, sparsity and physics

The factorisation of ch. 5.4 reduces generation to conditional laws of wavelet coefficients. Guth, Coste, De Bortoli and Mallat (2022) analysed its numerical complexity. Kadkhodaie, Guth, Mallat and Simoncelli (2023) learned these conditionals with denoisers whose receptive fields are local at each scale, a Markov model across scales that remains low-dimensional at every scale.

Marchand, Ozawa, Biroli and Mallat (2023) connected the same factorisation to the renormalisation group and estimated interaction energies of physical fields scale by scale. Diffusion models are also used to emulate turbulent flows and other fields from numerical simulations; the samples must then reproduce physical statistics (spectra, extremes), not only look plausible.

Open problem: which classes of laws admit conditional laws across scales that are both well conditioned and low-dimensional, so that the curse of dimensionality is avoided provably?

8.4 Faster sampling

A sampler with hundreds of network evaluations is slow. Deterministic solvers of the probability-flow ODE (Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021; Karras et al., 2022) reduce the count to tens. Progressive distillation (Salimans and Ho, 2022) trains a student network to do two teacher steps at once, repeatedly. Consistency models (Song, Dhariwal, Chen and Sutskever, 2023) learn to map any point of a trajectory directly to its end point.

Straighter transport paths, as in rectified flow (Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023), make single-step integration more accurate. Open problem: understand the trade-off between the number of steps and sample diversity, and what one-step generators lose.

8.5 Bridges, couplings and discrete data

Diffusion Schrödinger bridges (De Bortoli, Thornton, Heng and Doucet, 2021) join two arbitrary laws by the entropy-regularised optimal transport path, computed by iterated score learning. Stochastic interpolants (ch. 7) give a regression-based alternative with a prescribed coupling.

For discrete data such as text or graphs, the Gaussian noise is replaced by a Markov chain on a finite set (Austin et al., 2021). Open problem: a theory of the choice of the path — interpolant coefficients, coupling, noise — that minimises the statistical and numerical errors together.

8.6 Forecasting physical systems

GenCast (Price et al., 2025) showed that diffusion ensembles can compete with operational physical ensembles in weather forecasting. Chen et al. (2024) gave the interpolant formulation used in ch. 7.

Open questions include the calibration of ensembles for rare and extreme events, the conservation of physical invariants, long autoregressive rollouts that remain stable, and the assimilation of sparse observations. The related MVA course Interactions: Complexity, Stochasticity, Extrema and Rare Events treats the probabilistic side of extremes.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • The sampling error of diffusion models is well understood given the score error; the statistical error of learned scores is not.
  • Memorisation is real and measurable; generalisation depends on an inductive bias that is only partly understood.
  • Multiscale factorisations link generative models, sparsity and the renormalisation group.
  • Fast samplers, bridges, discrete diffusions and physical forecasting are active fronts.

    Solution: T(z) = m + Az with m = (1, -1) and A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, so T(Z) \sim \mathcal{N}(m, AA^\top) with AA^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} (Example 1.3).

    Since \det A = 2, Proposition 1.2 at x = m, that is z = 0, gives the density \gamma(0)/2 = 1/(4\pi) \approx 0.0796, and \log|\det\nabla T| = \log 2.

  2. ★ Monotone transport between Gaussians. Let \mu = \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and \nu = \mathcal{N}(m, s^2) with s > 0. Compute the map of Theorem 1.4 and W_2(\mu, \nu)^2, and evaluate for m = 1 and s = 2. Then give the monotone map from the standard Gaussian to the exponential law of mean 1.

    Solution: F_\nu^{-1}(u) = m + s\,\Phi^{-1}(u), so T(x) = m + s x. Then

    W_2^2 = \mathbb{E}\,(m + (s - 1) X)^2 = m^2 + (s - 1)^2 ,

    which equals 2 for m = 1, s = 2. For the exponential law, F^{-1}(u) = -\log(1 - u), so T(x) = -\log(1 - \Phi(x)).

  3. ★ A coupling layer. In \mathbb{R}^2, let T(z_a, z_b) = \big(z_a,\ z_b\, e^{z_a/2} + z_a^2\big). Give the inverse map, the log-determinant of the Jacobian, and the density of T(Z) at x = (0, 1) for Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2).

    Solution: the inverse keeps x_a and returns z_b = (x_b - x_a^2)\, e^{-x_a/2}. The Jacobian is lower triangular with diagonal (1, e^{z_a/2}), so \log|\det\nabla T(z)| = z_a/2.

    At x = (0, 1), z = (0, 1) and the log-determinant is 0, so the density is \gamma(0, 1) = e^{-1/2}/(2\pi) \approx 0.0965.

  4. ★ Forgetting the initial condition. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process of Proposition 2.4 starts from the deterministic point X_0 = 3 in dimension one. Give the law of X_t, the divergence \mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, 1)), and the time after which it is below 0.01.

    Solution: X_t \sim \mathcal{N}(3 e^{-t},\ 1 - e^{-2t}). The divergence between Gaussians in dimension one is

    \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, v) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\big) = \tfrac12 (v + \mu^2 - 1 - \log v),

    hence

    \mathrm{KL}(t) = \tfrac12 \big( 8 e^{-2t} - \log(1 - e^{-2t}) \big) ,

    which is 0.614 at t = 1 and 0.083 at t = 2, and falls below 0.01 for t > 3.05. For large t, \mathrm{KL}(t) \approx 4.5\, e^{-2t}. This is the rate of Theorem 2.6, although the initial divergence is infinite for a point mass.

  5. ★ The bias of unadjusted Langevin (Python). For \pi = \mathcal{N}(0, 1), run ULA with 100 000 chains for h \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5\} and compare the empirical stationary variance with Example 2.7.

    Hints and expected result: the formula 1/(1 - h/2) gives 1.053, 1.333 and 4.0 for the first three steps; the simulation matches to about 0.01 after a few hundred iterations.

    For h = 2.5, the multiplier 1 - h = -1.5 has modulus larger than 1 and the chains diverge. The step must stay below twice the smallest variance, the same constraint as in Proposition 5.7.

  6. ★ Tweedie for a Gaussian prior. Let X \sim \mathcal{N}(2, 1) and Y = X + 0.5\, Z. Compute the score of p_Y, then \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] with Theorem 3.2, and compare with Theorem 0.4. Evaluate at y = 3.

    Solution: p_Y = \mathcal{N}(2, 1.25) and \nabla \log p_Y(y) = -(y - 2)/1.25. Theorem 3.2 with a = 1 and \sigma^2 = 0.25 gives

    \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] = y - 0.2\,(y - 2) = 0.8\, y + 0.4 ,

    the Wiener estimator 2 + \frac{1}{1.25}(y - 2). At y = 3 the estimate is 2.8.

  7. ★ The irreducible denoising loss. Let X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda I_d) and X_t = a X_0 + \sigma Z. Find the function \varepsilon minimising \mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(X_t) - Z\|^2 and the minimal value. Specialise to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck schedule with \lambda = 1.

    Solution: the minimiser is \mathbb{E}[Z \mid X_t] (Theorem 0.4). The pair (Z, X_t) is Gaussian with \mathrm{Cov}(Z, X_t) = \sigma I and \mathrm{Var}(X_t) = (a^2\lambda + \sigma^2) I, so

    \mathbb{E}[Z \mid X_t] = \frac{\sigma}{a^2 \lambda + \sigma^2}\, X_t .

    The minimal loss is the trace of the conditional covariance of Z:

    d \Big( 1 - \frac{\sigma^2}{a^2\lambda + \sigma^2} \Big) = \frac{d\, a^2 \lambda}{a^2 \lambda + \sigma^2} .

    With \lambda = 1 and a_t^2 + \sigma_t^2 = 1, it equals d\, e^{-2t}. The loss cannot go to zero at small t, which is why its printed value says little about the quality of a model.

  8. ★ Bayes’ rule on scores for two classes. Let p(x \mid y) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_y, s^2 I) for y \in \{0, 1\} with equal prior weights. Compute \nabla_x \log p(y = 1 \mid x) and check Proposition 6.1 at t = 0. Evaluate at the origin for \mu_1 = -\mu_0 = (1, 0) and s^2 = 0.64.

    Solution: p(1 \mid x) is a logistic function of (\mu_1 - \mu_0) \cdot x / s^2, hence

    \nabla_x \log p(1 \mid x) = \big(1 - p(1 \mid x)\big)\, \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{s^2} .

    On the other side, with the posterior weights r_y = p(y \mid x), the difference of scores is the same vector:

    \nabla \log p(x \mid 1) - \nabla \log p(x) = \frac{\mu_1 - r_0 \mu_0 - r_1 \mu_1}{s^2} = r_0\, \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{s^2} .

    At the origin, p(1 \mid 0) = 0.5, and the gradient is (1.5625, 0).

  9. ★★ Langevin dynamics of a Gaussian, exactly. For \pi = \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda) in dimension one and p_0 = \mathcal{N}(m_0, v_0), show that p_t = \mathcal{N}(m_t, v_t) with

    m_t = m_0\, e^{-t/\lambda}, \qquad v_t = \lambda + (v_0 - \lambda)\, e^{-2t/\lambda} .

    Compute \mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, \pi) and compare with Theorem 2.6.

    Solution: the SDE dX = -X/\lambda\, dt + \sqrt 2\, dW is linear, so p_t stays Gaussian. Itô’s formula for X and X^2 gives \dot m = -m/\lambda and \dot v = -2v/\lambda + 2.

    With r_t = v_t/\lambda, the divergence is

    \mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, \pi) = \tfrac12 \Big( r_t - 1 - \log r_t + \frac{m_t^2}{\lambda} \Big) .

    The mean term decays exactly like e^{-2t/\lambda}, the rate of Theorem 2.6 with \rho = 1/\lambda (Bakry–Émery). The variance term, of order (r_t - 1)^2, decays like e^{-4t/\lambda}. The bound is therefore sharp, and attained by a shifted start.

  10. ★★ The reverse SDE for Gaussian data. Let X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \lambda) in dimension one, and v_t = \lambda a_t^2 + \sigma_t^2. Show that the reverse SDE of Theorem 3.1 with the exact score, started from \mathcal{N}(0, v_T), has variance v_{T - s} at time s.

    Show that the probability-flow ODE maps y_0 to \sqrt{\lambda / v_T}\; y_0, and compare with Exercise 2.

    Solution: the score is -y/v_t, and the variance u(s) of the reverse SDE satisfies \dot u = 2(1 - 2/v_{T-s})\, u + 2.

    Since \frac{d}{dt} v_t = 2(1 - v_t), the function u(s) = v_{T-s} has derivative 2 v_{T-s} - 2, which is also the right-hand side at u = v_{T-s}. Uniqueness for this linear ODE concludes.

    For the ODE \dot y = y\,(1 - 1/v_{T-s}), the solution is linear in y_0, and the same computation shows that y^2 scales like v_{T-s}. The map y_0 \mapsto \sqrt{\lambda / v_T}\, y_0 is the monotone, hence optimal, transport between the two Gaussians of Exercise 2.

  11. ★★ Score matching estimates a Gaussian. Fit the model score s(x) = -(x - \mu)/v of \mathcal{N}(\mu, v) to data x_1, \dots, x_n by minimising the empirical version of the objective of Theorem 4.1. Show that the minimisers are the empirical mean and variance.

    Solution: s(x)^2 + 2 s'(x) = (x - \mu)^2 / v^2 - 2/v. Its empirical mean is minimised in \mu by \bar x.

    With S = \frac1n \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2, the objective becomes S/v^2 - 2/v, whose derivative -2S/v^3 + 2/v^2 vanishes at v = S. Score matching coincides here with maximum likelihood.

  12. ★★ The Jacobian of the MMSE denoiser. (a) For X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, C) and Y = X + \sigma Z, compute \nabla D and show that its eigenvalues lie in [0, 1).

    1. For X = \pm 1 with probability \frac12 each in dimension one, compute D(y) and D'(0). Can a denoiser amplify the noise locally?

    Solution: (a) D(y) = C (C + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} y, whose eigenvalues are \lambda_k / (\lambda_k + \sigma^2) \in [0, 1), as Proposition 5.2 predicts with \mathrm{Cov}[X \mid Y] = \sigma^2 C (C + \sigma^2 I)^{-1}.

    1. The posterior weights give D(y) = \tanh(y / \sigma^2), so that

    D'(y) = \frac{1 - \tanh^2(y/\sigma^2)}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\mathrm{Var}[X \mid y]}{\sigma^2} .

    At the origin, D'(0) = 1/\sigma^2, larger than 1 when \sigma < 1: near the decision boundary between two modes, the denoiser amplifies small changes of y.

  13. ★★ Uniform and adaptive time grids. (a) Using Proposition 5.7, give the minimal number of uniform steps on [0, 8] when \lambda_{\min} = 10^{-4}, the order of magnitude for a 100 \times 100 image with a |\omega|^{-2} spectrum.

    1. Choose instead the step h(t) = c\, v_{\min}(t) with v_{\min}(t) = \lambda_{\min} a_t^2 + \sigma_t^2. Show that about (T + \frac12 \log \kappa)/c steps suffice.

    Solution: (a) n \ge 8\,(2 - 10^{-4}) / (2 \cdot 10^{-4}) = 79\,996.

    1. The number of steps is about \int_0^T dt / h(t). With v_{\min}(t) = 1 - (1 - \lambda_{\min}) e^{-2t} and the substitution u = e^{2t},

    \int_0^T \frac{dt}{v_{\min}(t)} = \frac12 \log \frac{e^{2T} - 1 + \lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\min}} \approx T + \frac12 \log \kappa .

    Non-uniform grids turn a cost linear in \kappa into a logarithmic one for stability. Accuracy and the conditioning of the learning problem still favour the multiscale factorisation of ch. 5.4.

  14. ★★ Diffusion posterior sampling for a Gaussian prior. In dimension one, let x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), y = x_0 + \sigma_y n, and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noising. Compute p_t(y \mid x_t) exactly and its DPS approximation, and the ratio of their gradients in x_t at t = 1 for \sigma_y = 0.1.

    Solution: since a_t^2 + \sigma_t^2 = 1, Proposition 6.4 gives \Sigma_t = \sigma_t^2 and D_t(x_t) = a_t x_t. Hence the exact likelihood is Gaussian with mean a_t x_t and variance \sigma_y^2 + \sigma_t^2, while DPS uses the variance \sigma_y^2.

    Both gradients are a_t (y - a_t x_t) divided by the variance, so their ratio is (\sigma_y^2 + \sigma_t^2)/\sigma_y^2. At t = 1, \sigma_t^2 = 1 - e^{-2} = 0.865, and the DPS gradient is 87.5 times too strong.

  15. ★★ The velocity of a Gaussian interpolant. In dimension one, let x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and x_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2) be independent, and x_t = (1 - t) x_0 + t x_1. Compute b(t, x) of Theorem 7.2, check the continuity equation, and identify the map x_0 \mapsto X_1 of the ODE.

    Solution: x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(tm, V_t) with V_t = (1 - t)^2 + t^2 s^2, and \dot x_t = x_1 - x_0 has mean m and covariance t s^2 - (1 - t) with x_t. Gaussian conditioning gives

    b(t, x) = m + \frac{t s^2 - (1 - t)}{V_t}\, (x - tm) .

    Since \dot V_t = 2\,(t s^2 - (1 - t)), a linear velocity with slope \dot V_t / (2V_t) keeps the law Gaussian with variance V_t and mean tm, which solves the continuity equation. The ODE map is affine and increasing, so it is x_0 \mapsto m + s x_0, the optimal map of Exercise 2. In dimension d \ge 2 the interpolant map is not optimal in general.

  16. ★★★ Memorisation and generalisation on digits (Python). Train a denoiser as in ch. 4.3 — a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 256 units, whose inputs are the 64 pixels and \log t — on the 8 × 8 digits of sklearn.datasets.load_digits rescaled to [-1, 1]. Use two disjoint training sets of size N \in \{100, 700\}, then sample 200 images from each network with the same random seed. Compare the median distance between the two networks’ samples with the same seed, and between random pairs.

    Hints and expected result: this reproduces, at toy scale, the test of Kadkhodaie et al. (2024). In a test run with 3000 training steps of batch 256 and 300 sampling steps, the same-seed distance was about 0.7 times the random-pair distance for N = 100, and 0.35 times for N = 700. The two networks converge to the same generator as N grows.

    For very small N (10 images), the training is unstable at small noise levels; compare instead with the exact empirical denoiser of Proposition 4.3, which reproduces training images exactly.

  17. ★★★ Point forecasts, ensembles and calibration (Python). Extend the Lorenz-63 code of ch. 7.3. (a) Train a deterministic network by mean square error to predict x_1 from y, and compare its CRPS (its mean absolute error) with that of the ensemble. (b) Compare the variance of its predictions with the variance of the true future states. (c) Draw a rank histogram of the truth within the ensemble, and repeat for lead times \tau \in \{0.25, 0.5, 1\}.

    Hints and expected result: by Theorem 0.4, the deterministic network estimates the conditional mean. Its root mean square error should be close to that of the ensemble mean, but its CRPS should be larger than the ensemble’s, by Proposition 7.5. Its predictions have a smaller variance than the true states: it blurs the possible futures, the more so as \tau grows.

    A calibrated ensemble gives a flat rank histogram. A U-shaped histogram signals under-dispersion, which the spread 0.553 against the error 0.672 already suggests at \tau = 0.5.

  18. ★★★ Conditioning across scales (Python). Let x \in \mathbb{R}^d be a stationary Gaussian vector with circulant covariance of spectrum 1/(|k|^2 + 1), |k| \le d/2. Compute the condition number of its covariance. Then apply one Haar step at each scale, and compute the condition number of the conditional covariance of the details given the approximation, \mathrm{Cov}(\bar x_j \mid x_j), for d = 32, \dots, 256. Relate the result to Proposition 5.7 and the key result of ch. 5.4.

    Hints and expected result: build the covariance as F^* \mathrm{diag}(\text{spectrum})\, F with the unitary DFT matrix. The conditional covariance is the Schur complement C_{dd} - C_{da} C_{aa}^{-1} C_{ad}.

    The condition number of the covariance grows like d^2/4 (257, 1025, 4097 and 16 385). The conditional covariances at every scale have condition numbers between 3.3 and 4.0, whatever d. Each conditional sampling problem is therefore well conditioned, and Proposition 5.7 gives a number of steps per scale independent of d, as in Guth et al. (2022).

Projects and assessment

The official validation (master-mva.com) is a project on one of the challenges proposed on challenge.data.ens.fr, followed by an oral exam at the end of March with the submission of a report; the Université Paris-Saclay course detail lists it as partial continuous assessment, and no weights are displayed. The first group subject below follows this format; the other subjects are paper studies and experiments sized for practice or for a research-oriented report, and the written exam is a proposal for self-assessment before the oral.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Langevin, annealed Langevin and diffusion on multimodal laws. Compare the samplers of ch. 2 and ch. 3 on Gaussian mixtures in dimension 2 to 50, whose scores are exact.
    • Implement ULA, MALA, annealed Langevin over noise levels (Song and Ermon, 2019) and the reverse SDE and probability-flow ODE of Theorem 3.1.
    • Measure the error on the mode weights and the KL or Wasserstein error against the number of score evaluations; check the Kramers rate of ch. 2.5.
    • Success criterion: a figure of error against cost for each sampler, explained by Theorems 2.6 and 3.4.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters 2 and 3.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Opening a denoiser: Jacobians and wavelets. Train a small bias-free convolutional denoiser on synthetic piecewise-regular 1D signals, at several noise levels.
    • Check D(y) = \nabla D(y)\, y (Proposition 5.1), compute the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the Jacobian, and compare the effective dimension with the error (Proposition 5.2).
    • Compare with Haar and Daubechies thresholding (Theorem 5.4), following Mohan et al. (2020).
    • Success criterion: the network at least matches wavelet thresholding, and its Jacobian eigenvectors are shown to adapt to the jumps.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapter 5.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: memorisation and generalisation. Reproduce at small scale the main experiment of Kadkhodaie, Guth, Simoncelli and Mallat (2024) on the scikit-learn digits, or on a public face data set downsampled to 32 \times 32 if a free GPU is available.
    • Train denoisers on disjoint training sets of increasing size N; sample with a common seed; measure nearest-neighbour distances to the training sets and the distance between the two networks’ samples.
    • Success criterion: a curve showing the transition from memorisation to generalisation, as in Exercise 16.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters 3–5.

Written exam (proposal)

Two hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no calculator needed; four exercises.

  1. Question (4 points). State the Fokker–Planck equation of dX = b(X)\, dt + \sqrt 2\, dW, and deduce that the Langevin diffusion leaves \pi invariant. Explain in two sentences why Langevin sampling is slow for a mixture of two well-separated Gaussians, and why the reverse diffusion is not.

    Expected: Theorem 2.3 with g = \sqrt 2; with b = \nabla \log \pi the right-hand side is \nabla \cdot (p \nabla \log(p/\pi)), which vanishes at p = \pi.

    Metastability with crossing times of order e^{\Delta U} (ch. 2.5); the reverse diffusion follows the noisy laws p_t, nearly Gaussian at large t, and Theorem 3.4 needs no log-Sobolev inequality.

  2. Question (6 points). Let X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 4) in dimension one, noised by the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Give p_t, its score, the denoiser \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t], and the probability-flow ODE. Compute the denoised value at t = \frac12 \log 2 for X_t = 1.

    Expected: p_t = \mathcal{N}(0, v_t) with v_t = 4 e^{-2t} + 1 - e^{-2t} = 1 + 3e^{-2t}; score -y/v_t; Tweedie gives \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t = y] = 4 a_t\, y / v_t; ODE \dot y = y\,(1 - 1/v_{T-s}).

    At t = \frac12 \log 2: a_t^2 = \frac12 and v_t = 2.5, so the denoised value is 4 \times 0.707 / 2.5 \approx 1.13.

  3. Question (5 points). Prove the denoising score-matching identity of Theorem 4.2, and deduce that the empirical minimiser memorises the training set.

    Expected: expansion of both squares, the cross term rewritten with \nabla p_Y under the integral, the noise score -Z/\sigma; then Proposition 4.3: the empirical minimiser is the score of the Gaussian mixture centred on the training points, and Theorem 3.1 returns \hat p.

  4. Question (5 points). Explain classifier-free guidance and what it targets at t = 0. For an inverse problem y = x_0 + \sigma_y n with a Gaussian prior \mathcal{N}(0, 1), give the exact likelihood p_t(y \mid x_t) and say when diffusion posterior sampling is accurate.

    Expected: Definition 6.2 and Proposition 6.3, the tilted law p(x \mid y) p(y \mid x)^w and why guidance is a heuristic for t > 0.

    Proposition 6.4 with \Sigma_t = \sigma_t^2, so that the likelihood is Gaussian with mean a_t x_t and variance \sigma_y^2 + \sigma_t^2. DPS drops \sigma_t^2: accurate when \sigma_t^2 \ll \sigma_y^2, overconfident early in the sampling (Exercise 14).
