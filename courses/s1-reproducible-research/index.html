0. Review: floating point, probability and graphs

Three M1 tools return throughout the course: the rounding model of floating-point arithmetic (ch. 6 and 10), Bayes’ formula with the vocabulary of tests (ch. 5 and 8), and directed acyclic graphs with hash functions (ch. 7). This chapter states them in the form used later.

0.1 Floating-point numbers

Definition 0.1 — Binary floating-point numbers A binary floating-point format of precision p represents the non-zero numbers x = \pm\, m \times 2^{e-p+1}, \qquad m \in \mathbb{N}, \quad 2^{p-1} \le m < 2^{p}, with an exponent e in a bounded range, so that 2^e \le |x| < 2^{e+1}. IEEE 754 binary64 (float64) has p = 53; binary32 (float32) has p = 24.

The unit roundoff of the format is u = 2^{-p}, \qquad u_{64} \approx 1.11 \times 10^{-16}, \qquad u_{32} \approx 5.96 \times 10^{-8}.

Proposition 0.2 — The standard model of rounding With rounding to nearest, and without overflow or underflow, each operation \circ \in \{+, -, \times, /\} satisfies \mathrm{fl}(x \circ y) = (x \circ y)(1 + \delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u .

Proof. IEEE 754 requires the computed result to be the exact result, correctly rounded. Let z = x \circ y \neq 0 with 2^e \le |z| < 2^{e+1}.

In that range, consecutive floating-point numbers are 2^{e-p+1} apart, so the nearest one is at distance at most half of that: |\mathrm{fl}(z) - z| \le 2^{e-p} \le 2^{-p}\,|z| = u\,|z| .

Setting \delta = (\mathrm{fl}(z) - z)/z gives the claim. ∎

Each operation is accurate, but associativity is lost: a sum depends on the order of its terms. Near 10^{16}, binary64 numbers are 2 apart, so adding 1 to 10^{16} does nothing.

import numpy as np

u = np.finfo(np.float64).eps / 2          # unit roundoff of binary64
print(f"u = {u:.3e}")                      # 1.110e-16
print(0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3, repr(0.1 + 0.2))   # False 0.30000000000000004
print((1e16 + 1.0) - 1e16, (1e16 - 1e16) + 1.0)  # 0.0 1.0
print(np.spacing(1e16))                    # gap between neighbours of 1e16: 2.0

0.2 Bayes’ formula and tests

For events A and B with \mathbb{P}(B) > 0, Bayes’ formula with the law of total probability reads \mathbb{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\,\mathbb{P}(A)}{\mathbb{P}(B \mid A)\,\mathbb{P}(A) + \mathbb{P}(B \mid A^c)\,\mathbb{P}(A^c)} .

Definition 0.3 — Level, power and p-value A test at level \alpha rejects a true null hypothesis with probability at most \alpha (type I error). Its power 1 - \beta is the probability of rejecting under a given alternative; \beta is the type II error.

For a statistic that is \mathcal{N}(0, 1) under the null hypothesis, the two-sided p-value of an observed value z is p = 2\,\big(1 - \Phi(|z|)\big), where \Phi is the standard normal distribution function. Thus p \le 0.05 exactly when |z| \ge 1.96.

0.3 Directed acyclic graphs and hash functions

Definition 0.4 — DAG and topological order A directed graph G = (V, E) is acyclic (a DAG) if it has no directed cycle. A topological order is a bijective numbering \pi : V \to \{1, \dots, |V|\} such that every edge goes forward: (u, v) \in E \implies \pi(u) < \pi(v).

Proposition 0.5 — Topological orders exist exactly on DAGs A finite directed graph has a topological order if and only if it is acyclic.

Proof. On a cycle v_1 \to \dots \to v_k \to v_1, an order would need \pi(v_1) < \dots < \pi(v_k) < \pi(v_1), which is impossible.

Conversely, a finite DAG has a vertex without incoming edge: otherwise, walking backwards along incoming edges would revisit a vertex and close a cycle. Number that vertex 1, delete it, and conclude by induction on |V|. ∎

Definition 0.6 — Hash function, collision A hash function maps strings of any length to strings of fixed length, H : \{0, 1\}^* \to \{0, 1\}^k , with k = 160 for SHA-1 and k = 256 for SHA-256. A collision is a pair x \neq x' with H(x) = H(x'). Collisions exist by the pigeonhole principle; H is collision resistant when no feasible computation is known to find one.

Proposition 0.7 — Birthday bound If q inputs receive independent uniform hash values in \{0, 1\}^k, the probability that two of them collide is at most \frac{q(q-1)}{2^{k+1}} \le \frac{q^2}{2^{k+1}} .

Proof. There are q(q-1)/2 pairs, and each pair collides with probability 2^{-k}; the union bound concludes. ∎

With q = 10^9 files and k = 160, the bound is about 3 \times 10^{-31}: accidental collisions do not happen. Deliberate ones are discussed in ch. 7.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Each floating-point operation has a relative error at most u, but sums depend on the order of their terms.
  • Bayes’ formula turns the level and power of a test into the probability that a finding is true (ch. 5).
  • A finite directed graph has a topological order exactly when it is acyclic.
  • A collision-resistant hash gives a short, practically unique name to any file.

1. Free and open-source software: introduction

This chapter fixes the vocabulary of the course: what software is as a legal object, who controls it by default, and what “free software” and “open source” promise.

1.1 Source code and copyright

Definition 1.1 — Source code and object code The GNU GPL version 3 (section 1) defines the source code of a work as “the preferred form of the work for making modifications to it”. Object code is any other form, such as a compiled binary. For a trained model, which form is preferred for modification (weights, training code, data) is debated (ch. 9.6).

A computer program is protected by copyright as a literary work: under the Berne Convention as applied by the TRIPS agreement (article 10), the European Directive 2009/24/EC, and, in France, article L112-2 of the Code de la propriété intellectuelle.

Protection is automatic, without registration, and lasts in the European Union until 70 years after the author’s death. It covers the expression, that is the code, but not the ideas and principles behind it (Directive 2009/24/EC, article 1(2)), which include algorithms insofar as they are ideas.

Default rule Without a licence, all rights are reserved. Reading code on a public web page gives no right to copy, modify or redistribute it.

The rights holder is the author or, for software written by an employee in the course of their duties, the employer (ch. 9.2). A licence is the document by which the rights holder grants others some of these rights.

1.2 Free software and open source

Definition 1.2 — Free software The Free Software Foundation (FSF) calls a program free software when its users have four freedoms:

  • freedom 0, to run the program as they wish, for any purpose;
  • freedom 1, to study how the program works and change it, which requires the source code;
  • freedom 2, to redistribute copies;
  • freedom 3, to distribute copies of modified versions.

“Free” refers to liberty, not price: free software may be sold.

Definition 1.3 — Open source The Open Source Initiative (OSI) approves a licence as open source when it meets the ten criteria of the Open Source Definition: free redistribution; source code; derived works allowed; integrity of the author’s source code; no discrimination against persons or groups; no discrimination against fields of endeavour; distribution of the licence; licence not specific to a product; licence not restricting other software; licence technology-neutral.

The two definitions come from different philosophies, ethical for the FSF and practical for the OSI, but select almost the same licences, hence the umbrella term FOSS. Exceptions are rare: the OSI approves the NASA Open Source Agreement 1.3, which the FSF does not consider free.

Definition 1.4 — Neighbouring categories Freeware costs nothing but gives neither source nor freedoms. Source-available software shows its source but withholds a freedom, such as commercial use or hosting. A public domain dedication waives all rights as far as the law allows; CC0 adds a fallback licence. Everything else is proprietary.

Example 1.5 — Free or not?

  • CC BY-NC 4.0 forbids commercial use, a field of endeavour: neither free nor open source.
  • The JSON licence adds to an MIT text “The Software shall be used for Good, not Evil”; this restriction on use breaks freedom 0, and neither the FSF nor the OSI considers it free.
  • The Business Source License 1.1 restricts production use until a change date, at most four years after each release, when the code switches to an open licence: source-available.
  • The MIT licence only asks to keep the copyright and permission notice: free and open source.

1.3 A short history

  • 1983–1989: Richard Stallman announces the GNU project, creates the FSF (1985) and publishes the GNU General Public License (1989).
  • 1991–1992: Linus Torvalds releases Linux (1991) and places it under the GPL version 2 the following year.
  • 1998: the term “open source” is adopted and the OSI founded, after Netscape announces the release of its browser’s source code.
  • 2001–2007: Creative Commons is founded (2001) to license texts and images; the GPL version 3 appears (2007).

1.4 Why free software matters for research

A computational result can be checked only if its code can be run for any purpose, read and passed to a colleague: freedoms 0, 1 and 2. A proprietary dependency or a discontinued version can make a result impossible to regenerate. A free licence is necessary but not sufficient: the code must also be available, documented, runnable and faithful to the article, the subject of ch. 5–10.

Debate Is code released on a public platform without a licence “open”? Should the code of a thesis be released before the defence, and under whose name? Prepare one argument for each side, using ch. 1.1 and ch. 9.2.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Software is protected by copyright automatically; without a licence, nothing may be redistributed.
  • Free software guarantees four freedoms; open source is defined by ten criteria; both select nearly the same licences.
  • Restrictions on commercial use or on fields of use make a licence non-free.
  • Reproducibility needs free software, but also availability, documentation and faithfulness to the article.

2. Licensing

A licence turns the default “all rights reserved” into a list of what others may do. This chapter reads licences clause by clause, sorts them into families, and answers the question every project meets: can these components be distributed together?

2.1 Anatomy of a licence

Definition 2.1 — Permissions, conditions, limitations A free software licence is a permission granted by the rights holder to anyone who accepts its terms. Its clauses are of three kinds:

  • permissions: use, copy, modify, distribute, sublicense, sometimes use the contributors’ patents;
  • conditions: keep the notices, state the changes, provide the source, keep the same licence;
  • limitations: no warranty, no liability, no trademark rights.

If a condition is not met, the permission lapses and further use infringes copyright; the GPL version 3 (section 8) lets the violation be cured.

2.2 Families and copyleft

Definition 2.2 — Copyleft A copyleft licence requires that distributed modified versions, and, depending on its scope, larger works containing the code, be licensed under the same terms, with their source. A permissive licence only asks to keep the notices: the code may enter a work under any licence, proprietary included.

Licence (SPDX identifier) Family Copyleft scope Explicit patent grant
MIT, BSD-3-Clause permissive none no
Apache-2.0 (2004) permissive none yes, ended for whoever sues over patents
MPL-2.0 (2012) weak copyleft modified files yes
LGPL-2.1-only, LGPL-3.0-only weak copyleft the library, not programs linking to it in version 3
GPL-2.0-only (1991) strong copyleft whole program no
GPL-3.0-only (2007) strong copyleft whole program yes
AGPL-3.0-only (2007) network copyleft whole program, also used over a network yes

Copyleft is triggered by distribution: private use and modification carry no obligation. A service is not distributed, so the AGPL (section 13) obliges the operator to offer the source to remote users. The GPL does not extend to a mere aggregation of separate programs; the FSF holds that linking a library creates a combined work, and the LGPL was written to allow linking from programs under other licences.

2.3 When can licences be combined?

The following model makes the question precise; it simplifies legal reasoning and does not replace it.

Definition 2.3 — Compatibility model Describe a licence \ell by two sets of conditions on a distributed work that contains \ell-licensed code: the conditions O(\ell) it imposes, and the conditions A(\ell) \supseteq O(\ell) it tolerates. A permissive licence tolerates every condition; a copyleft licence only its own and those it explicitly allows.

Licences \ell_1, \dots, \ell_k are compatible if each tolerates all the imposed conditions: O := \bigcup_{i=1}^{k} O(\ell_i) \subseteq A(\ell_j) \quad \text{for every } j .

Proposition 2.4 — Compatibility is decided pair by pair In the model of Definition 2.3, a set of licences is compatible if and only if each pair of its licences is compatible.

Proof. (\Rightarrow) The conditions imposed by a pair satisfy O(\ell_i) \cup O(\ell_j) \subseteq O \subseteq A(\ell_i) \cap A(\ell_j).

(\Leftarrow) Pairwise compatibility gives O(\ell_i) \subseteq A(\ell_j) for all i \neq j, and O(\ell_j) \subseteq A(\ell_j) holds by definition.

Taking the union over i gives O \subseteq A(\ell_j) for every j. ∎

Compatibility is not transitive: MIT code is compatible with Apache-2.0 and with the GPL version 2, but, according to the FSF, Apache-2.0 is not compatible with the GPL version 2, whose ban on further restrictions excludes Apache’s patent termination clause. Compatible sets are the cliques of the compatibility graph.

The code encodes facts from the FSF’s licence list: MIT and BSD-3-Clause are compatible with both GPL versions; Apache-2.0 only with version 3; GPL-2.0-only and version 3 are incompatible, but “version 2 or later” code can be used under version 3; the GPL and AGPL version 3 allow combination (section 13 of both).

# Toy model of Definition 2.3: a licence = (imposed conditions O, tolerated conditions A).
ANY = None  # permissive: tolerates any condition on the combined work
GPL3_OK = {"notice", "no-endorsement", "state-changes", "patent-termination",
           "source", "under-GPL-3.0", "network-source"}
LIC = {
    "MIT":              ({"notice"}, ANY),
    "BSD-3-Clause":     ({"notice", "no-endorsement"}, ANY),
    "Apache-2.0":       ({"notice", "state-changes", "patent-termination"}, ANY),
    "GPL-2.0-only":     ({"notice", "source", "under-GPL-2.0"},
                         {"notice", "no-endorsement", "source", "under-GPL-2.0"}),
    "GPL-3.0-or-later": ({"notice", "source", "under-GPL-3.0"}, GPL3_OK),
    "AGPL-3.0-only":    ({"notice", "source", "under-GPL-3.0", "network-source"}, GPL3_OK),
}

def compatible(licences):
    O = set().union(*(LIC[l][0] for l in licences))
    return all(LIC[l][1] is ANY or O <= LIC[l][1] for l in licences)

names = list(LIC)
print([(a, b) for i, a in enumerate(names) for b in names[i + 1:] if not compatible([a, b])])
print(compatible(["MIT", "Apache-2.0", "GPL-3.0-or-later"]))   # True
print(compatible(["MIT", "Apache-2.0", "GPL-2.0-only"]))       # False

def spdx_ok(expr, accepted):
    """Evaluate an SPDX expression without parentheses: AND binds tighter than OR."""
    return any(all(t.strip() in accepted for t in conj.split(" AND "))
               for conj in expr.split(" OR "))

print(spdx_ok("MIT OR Apache-2.0", {"Apache-2.0"}))                 # True
print(spdx_ok("GPL-2.0-only AND BSD-3-Clause", {"BSD-3-Clause"}))   # False

The model finds three incompatible pairs: Apache-2.0 with GPL-2.0-only, and GPL-2.0-only with each version-3 licence.

Example 2.5 — A thesis repository A repository combines the student’s code, an MIT plotting helper, an Apache-2.0 data loader and a function copied from a GPL-2.0-only project. By Proposition 2.4 it is incompatible, because of the pair Apache-2.0 and GPL-2.0-only. Fixes: rewrite the function; call the GPL program as a separate executable (aggregation); or obtain “version 2 or later” and distribute everything under GPL-3.0-or-later.

The model ignores the kind of combination (copying, linking, separate processes), licence exceptions, and the fact that only courts settle interpretations. These notes are not legal advice.

2.4 Applying a licence: SPDX and REUSE

SPDX, a Linux Foundation project standardised as ISO/IEC 5962:2021, names every licence and combines names into expressions, which spdx_ok above evaluates:

  • MIT OR Apache-2.0: the recipient chooses either licence;
  • GPL-2.0-only AND BSD-3-Clause: both apply, for instance to two parts of a package;
  • GPL-2.0-only WITH Linux-syscall-note: a licence with an additional permission.

To license a project, put the licence text in a LICENSE file, start each source file with a copyright line and SPDX-License-Identifier: GPL-3.0-or-later, declare the expression in the package metadata (License-Expression for Python), and check the tree with the REUSE tool of the Free Software Foundation Europe.

2.5 French and European licences

  • CeCILL (CEA, CNRS, Inria), in French and English, governed by French law: CeCILL-2.1 (2013) is a copyleft that explicitly allows relicensing under the GNU GPL; CeCILL-B is permissive with a strong citation obligation; CeCILL-C is a library-level copyleft, close to the LGPL.
  • EUPL-1.2 (European Commission, 2017), a copyleft in the official languages of the European Union, whose appendix lists compatible licences, among them the GPL, AGPL, LGPL, MPL-2.0 and CeCILL-2.x.

2.6 Patents

Copyright protects the code; a patent protects an invention, possibly the method the code implements, whoever wrote it. A free licence does not guarantee freedom to use a method patented by a third party. The European Patent Convention (article 52) excludes programs for computers “as such”, but software-related inventions with a technical character can be patented in Europe.

Example 2.6 — The SIFT patent Lowe’s SIFT detector was covered by United States patent 6,711,293, held by the University of British Columbia. OpenCV kept it in a separate “non-free” module until the patent expired in March 2020; SIFT then joined the main library in release 4.4.0. Patent grants such as Apache-2.0’s would not have helped: they cover only the contributors’ own patents.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • A licence grants permissions under conditions; breaking a condition ends the permission.
  • Copyleft scope ranges from none to the file, the library, the program and network use.
  • In the model of Definition 2.3, compatibility is decided pair by pair but is not transitive.
  • Declare licences with SPDX expressions, a LICENSE file and per-file headers.
  • Patents are a separate layer from copyright licences.

3. Economic models of free software projects

Free software costs nothing to copy, yet years of work to write and maintain. This chapter explains why such a good is under-provided, how projects finance themselves, and how to measure their fragility.

3.1 Software as a public good

Definition 3.1 — Rivalry, excludability, public good A good is rival when one person’s use prevents another’s, and excludable when its owner can prevent use by those who do not pay. A public good is neither (Samuelson, 1954).

Copying software costs almost nothing, and a free licence gives up the right to exclude: free software is a public good. Its private provision is then insufficient, since each user prefers that another one pays (Bergstrom, Blume and Varian, 1986). The volunteer’s dilemma quantifies this.

3.2 The volunteer’s dilemma

Consider n \ge 2 users of a library. If at least one of them fixes a critical bug, each gains v > 0; fixing costs the volunteer c, with 0 < c < v.

The payoffs are:

  • v - c for a volunteer;
  • v for an abstainer when someone volunteers;
  • 0 for everyone when nobody does.

Theorem 3.2 — Volunteer’s dilemma (Diekmann, 1985)

    1. The pure Nash equilibria are the n profiles in which exactly one user volunteers.
    1. There is a unique symmetric equilibrium. In it, each user abstains with probability q = (c/v)^{1/(n-1)} .
    1. In that equilibrium, the probability that nobody volunteers is \mathbb{P}(\text{nobody}) = (c/v)^{n/(n-1)}, which increases with n and tends to c/v.

Proof.

  1. If nobody volunteers, anyone gains v - c > 0 by volunteering. If two or more volunteer, one of them gains c by abstaining. If exactly one volunteers, they would lose v - c by abstaining, and the others already get the maximum v.

  2. By (a), no symmetric pure profile is an equilibrium, so 0 < q < 1 and each user is indifferent between the two actions. Volunteering yields v - c; abstaining yields v unless the n - 1 others all abstain: v - c = v\,\big(1 - q^{n-1}\big) \iff q^{n-1} = c/v .

  3. Nobody volunteers with probability q^n = (c/v)^{n/(n-1)}; as 0 < c/v < 1 and the exponent decreases to 1, this increases to c/v. ∎

Example 3.3 — More users, less maintenance With v = 10 and c = 4:

Users n Abstention probability q \mathbb{P}(\text{nobody})
2 0.400 0.160
5 0.795 0.318
10 0.903 0.361
100 0.991 0.396

From 2 to 100 users, the risk that the bug is never fixed more than doubles.

The pure equilibria show the remedy: coordination that designates the volunteer, as maintainers paid by a company, a foundation or a research institute do.

3.3 Why people contribute

Lerner and Tirole (2002) distinguish immediate benefits, such as needing the fix oneself (the “itch” of Raymond, 1999) or being paid for it, from delayed ones: public contributions signal skill to future employers and peers. In research, code is part of the output, but the credit it receives remains weak compared with articles (ch. 8.4).

3.5 Relicensing and forks

Rule — What a relicensing can and cannot do A project can change the licence of its future versions alone only if it holds the rights on every contribution, directly or through CLAs. Free licences are generally irrevocable (GPL version 3, section 2; Apache-2.0, section 2), so released versions stay under their licence, and anyone may fork the last of them, that is, continue it independently.

Three recent cases follow one pattern: cloud competition, a move to a source-available licence, a community fork.

  • Elasticsearch left Apache-2.0 in 2021; the OpenSearch fork kept it. Elastic added the AGPL version 3 as an option in 2024.
  • Terraform moved from MPL-2.0 to the Business Source License in 2023; the OpenTofu fork is hosted by the Linux Foundation.
  • Redis left BSD-3-Clause in 2024; the Valkey fork is hosted by the Linux Foundation. Redis added the AGPL version 3 as an option in 2025.

3.6 Measuring fragility: the truck factor

Definition 3.4 — Truck factor (Avelino et al., 2016) Let F be the files of a project and D its developers, file f having authors a(f) \subseteq D. A file is orphaned by the departure of S \subseteq D when a(f) \subseteq S. The truck factor is the smallest number of departures that orphan more than half of the files: \mathrm{TF} = \min \big\{ |S| : |\{ f \in F : a(f) \subseteq S \}| > |F|/2 \big\} .

Avelino and coauthors compute it greedily, removing at each step the developer whose departure orphans the most files. The code compares this heuristic with exhaustive search, and checks Theorem 3.2 by simulation.

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

# Volunteer's dilemma (Theorem 3.2): value v, cost c, n users
v, c = 10.0, 4.0
for n in [2, 5, 10, 100]:
    q = (c / v) ** (1 / (n - 1))          # probability of abstaining
    print(f"n={n:3d}  q={q:.3f}  P(nobody)={q**n:.3f}")
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 10; q = (c / v) ** (1 / (n - 1))
print("simulated, n=10:", (rng.random((200_000, n)) < q).all(axis=1).mean())

# Truck factor (Definition 3.4) on a synthetic authorship relation
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_files, n_dev = 60, 8
p = 1 / np.arange(1, n_dev + 1); p /= p.sum()          # a few prolific authors
authors = [set(rng.choice(n_dev, size=rng.integers(1, 3), replace=False, p=p))
           for _ in range(n_files)]
orphaned = lambda S: sum(a <= S for a in authors)

def truck_factor_greedy():
    S = set()
    while orphaned(S) <= n_files / 2:
        S.add(max(set(range(n_dev)) - S, key=lambda d: orphaned(S | {d})))
    return len(S)

def truck_factor_exact():
    return next(k for k in range(1, n_dev + 1)
                if any(orphaned(set(S)) > n_files / 2 for S in combinations(range(n_dev), k)))

print("greedy:", truck_factor_greedy(), " exact:", truck_factor_exact())   # 3 and 3

Both give 3 here, but the greedy heuristic can overestimate the truck factor (Ex. 9).

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Free software is a public good, hence privately under-provided.
  • In the symmetric volunteer’s dilemma, the risk that nobody does the work grows with the number of users.
  • Projects live on support, dual licensing, open core, hosting, foundations, grants and research institutions.
  • Released versions keep their licence; relicensing needs all the rights and often triggers a fork.
  • The truck factor counts the departures that would orphan most of the code.

4.1 The Linux kernel

  • Licence: GPL-2.0-only, with a note excluding from the copyleft the user programs that only use system calls (GPL-2.0-only WITH Linux-syscall-note).
  • Rights holders: thousands of contributors keep their copyright, without CLA; each patch certifies the Developer Certificate of Origin (2004) by a “Signed-off-by” line. A move to the GPL version 3 would need all their consents (ch. 3.5): practically impossible.
  • Economic model: most changes are written by developers employed by companies; the Linux Foundation provides neutral governance.

4.2 scikit-learn

  • Type and licence: a Python machine-learning library under BSD-3-Clause, started as a Google Summer of Code project (2007) and first released publicly by Inria researchers in 2010; Pedregosa et al. (2011) is its reference article.
  • Economic model: the permissive licence lets companies embed it in proprietary products, which brings users and sponsors; a consortium hosted by the Inria Foundation has funded maintainers since 2018.
  • Reproducibility: the random_state parameter of randomised estimators, deprecation cycles, extensive tests, documentation for each version.

4.3 OpenCV and the SIFT patent

  • Licence: BSD-3-Clause until release 4.4, Apache-2.0 from release 4.5.0 (2020), which adds a patent grant from contributors.
  • Patents: SIFT and SURF lived in a non-free module enabled at build time (Example 2.6).
  • Lesson: the copyright licence, third-party patents and the build options that select what is distributed are three layers to check separately.

4.4 Dual licensing: MySQL, Qt and CGAL

  • MySQL: the GPL version 2 or a commercial licence. When Oracle announced in 2009 the acquisition of Sun, owner of MySQL, the original author of MySQL started the MariaDB fork.
  • Qt: the GPL, the LGPL version 3, or commercial licences sold by The Qt Company.
  • CGAL, the Computational Geometry Algorithms Library: most packages under GPL-3.0-or-later, the kernel under LGPL-3.0-or-later, commercial licences sold by GeometryFactory. Academic users get free software; industrial users pay.

5. Reproducible research: introduction

A computational article makes claims that depend on code, data and an environment that the reader rarely sees. This chapter defines what reproducing such a claim means, reviews the evidence that many claims cannot be reproduced, and quantifies the statistical side of the problem.

5.1 Origins and vocabulary

In the early 1990s, Jon Claerbout’s group at Stanford arranged its books and theses so that every figure could be rebuilt by one command (Claerbout and Karrenbach, 1992). Buckheit and Donoho (1995) summarised the idea:

An article about computational science in a scientific publication is not the scholarship itself, it is merely advertising of the scholarship. The actual scholarship is the complete software development environment and the complete set of instructions which generated the figures.

Definition 5.1 — Reproducibility and replicability (National Academies, 2019)

  • Reproducibility: obtaining consistent computational results using the same input data, computational steps, methods, code and conditions of analysis.
  • Replicability: obtaining consistent results across studies aimed at answering the same scientific question, each of which has obtained its own data.

The Turing Way adds: a result is robust when a different analysis of the same data gives it, and generalisable when it survives new data and a new analysis.

Some communities swap the two words (Barba, 2018; Plesser, 2018); the ACM swapped the meanings of its badges in 2020 to align with the definitions above. Always state yours. Peng (2011) describes a spectrum: publication only; with code; with code and data; with linked, executable code and data; full replication.

5.2 Evidence

  • Psychology: the Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 studies; 97 % of the originals had significant results, against 36 % of the replications.
  • All sciences: among 1,576 researchers surveyed by Nature (Baker, 2016), more than 70 % had failed to reproduce another scientist’s experiments.
  • Machine learning: Raff (2019) attempted to reimplement 255 papers without their code and succeeded for about 63.5 %; Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) traced many failures to data leakage.
  • Notebooks: of about 1.4 million Jupyter notebooks collected by Pimentel et al. (2019), about a quarter of those re-executed ran without error, and about 4 % reproduced their stored results.

5.4 The statistics of findings that do not replicate

A perfectly reproducible computation can still support a claim that does not replicate.

Theorem 5.2 — Positive predictive value of a finding (Ioannidis, 2005) Among the hypotheses tested in a field, let R be the ratio of true to false ones, tested at level \alpha with power 1 - \beta. The probability that a significant finding is true is \mathrm{PPV} = \frac{(1 - \beta)\,R}{(1 - \beta)\,R + \alpha} .

Proof. A tested hypothesis is true with probability R/(1+R). Bayes’ formula (ch. 0.2), with A = “true” and B = “significant”, gives \mathrm{PPV} = \frac{(1-\beta)\,\frac{R}{1+R}}{(1-\beta)\,\frac{R}{1+R} + \alpha\,\frac{1}{1+R}} , and the factor 1/(1+R) cancels. ∎

Example 5.3 — Exploratory fields With \alpha = 0.05 and R = 0.1, the PPV is 0.08 / 0.13 \approx 0.615 at power 0.8, and 0.02 / 0.07 \approx 0.286 at power 0.2: in an underpowered field, most significant findings are false, without any error in the code.

Proposition 5.4 — Replicating a just-significant result (Goodman, 1992) A study observes a standardised statistic z_{\mathrm{obs}} > 0. If the true effect equals the observed one, a replication of the same size is significant at level \alpha, in the same direction, with probability \Phi\big(z_{\mathrm{obs}} - z_{1-\alpha/2}\big), which equals 1/2 at p = 0.05 exactly.

Proof. The replication statistic is then Z' \sim \mathcal{N}(z_{\mathrm{obs}}, 1), significant in the same direction when Z' > z_{1-\alpha/2}, so with probability 1 - \Phi\big(z_{1-\alpha/2} - z_{\mathrm{obs}}\big) = \Phi\big(z_{\mathrm{obs}} - z_{1-\alpha/2}\big). At p = 0.05, z_{\mathrm{obs}} = z_{0.975} and \Phi(0) = 1/2. ∎

Proposition 5.5 — Multiple testing Among m independent tests of true null hypotheses at level \alpha, at least one is significant with probability 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m, about 0.64 for m = 20 and \alpha = 0.05.

Proposition 5.6 — The best of n seeds If the scores of n runs with different seeds are independent and \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2), then \mathbb{E}\big[\max_i X_i\big] = \mu + e_n\,\sigma, \qquad e_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \approx 0.564,\quad e_{10} \approx 1.539, and e_n grows like \sqrt{2 \ln n}.

Proof. For n = 2, write \max(X_1, X_2) = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{2} + \frac{|X_1 - X_2|}{2} .

The difference X_1 - X_2 is \mathcal{N}(0, 2\sigma^2), and \mathbb{E}|Y| = s\sqrt{2/\pi} when Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2), so the second term has mean \sigma/\sqrt{\pi}. The value of e_{10} is computed below; the growth rate is the classical behaviour of Gaussian maxima. ∎

Reporting the best of ten seeds inflates the result by about 1.5 standard deviations of the seed noise. Henderson et al. (2018) showed that, in deep reinforcement learning, averages over two small sets of seeds of the same algorithm can differ significantly.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def ppv(R, power, alpha=0.05):                     # Theorem 5.2
    return power * R / (power * R + alpha)

for R in [1.0, 0.25, 0.1]:
    print(f"R={R:4.2f}  PPV(power .8)={ppv(R, .8):.3f}  PPV(power .2)={ppv(R, .2):.3f}")

z_crit = norm.ppf(0.975)                           # Proposition 5.4
for p in [0.05, 0.01, 0.005]:
    print(f"p={p:5.3f}  P(replication significant)={norm.cdf(norm.ppf(1 - p/2) - z_crit):.3f}")

print("P(at least one of 20 tests)", round(1 - 0.95**20, 3))   # Proposition 5.5

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                     # Proposition 5.6
for n in [2, 5, 10]:
    e_n = rng.standard_normal((100_000, n)).max(axis=1).mean()
    print(f"n={n:2d}  e_n = {e_n:.3f}")

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Reproducibility reruns the same analysis on the same data; replicability obtains the result again with new data.
  • Psychology, surveys, machine learning and notebooks all show widespread failures.
  • Low prior odds and low power make most significant findings false (Theorem 5.2).
  • A just-significant result replicates half the time; the best of ten seeds overstates the mean by about 1.5\sigma.

6. Numerical determinism: floating point and pseudo-random numbers

Rerunning the same code on the same data does not guarantee the same numbers. This chapter bounds the effect of the order of floating-point operations, lists the sources of nondeterminism, and makes pseudo-random numbers reproducible.

6.1 Rounding errors in sums

For an integer m with mu < 1, write \gamma_m = \frac{m u}{1 - m u} .

Lemma 6.1 — Products of rounding factors If |\delta_k| \le u for k = 1, \dots, m and mu < 1, then \Big|\prod_{k=1}^{m} (1 + \delta_k) - 1\Big| \le \gamma_m .

Proof. The product lies between (1-u)^m and (1+u)^m. Above, (1+u)^m \le e^{mu} \le 1/(1 - mu) = 1 + \gamma_m, since e^x \le 1/(1-x) for 0 \le x < 1. Below, (1-u)^m \ge 1 - mu \ge 1 - \gamma_m by Bernoulli’s inequality. ∎

Theorem 6.2 — Error of recursive summation Let s = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i and let \hat s be computed from left to right in floating point, with (n-1)u < 1. Then |\hat s - s| \le \gamma_{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i| .

Proof. Let \hat s_1 = x_1 and \hat s_k = (\hat s_{k-1} + x_k)(1 + \delta_k) for k \ge 2, with |\delta_k| \le u by Proposition 0.2. Unrolling the recursion, \hat s = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \prod_{k=\max(i,2)}^{n} (1 + \delta_k) .

Each product has at most n - 1 factors, hence differs from 1 by at most \gamma_{n-1} (Lemma 6.1). Subtracting s and using the triangle inequality concludes. ∎

Corollary 6.3 — Two orders, two results Any two summation orders, for instance those of two thread counts, give results within 2\,\gamma_{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i| of each other, but generally not equal bit for bit: bitwise reproducibility requires a fixed order of operations.

The bound is a worst case; rounding errors of random sign partly cancel (Ex. 11). Compensated summation carries the rounding error of each addition into the next.

Algorithm 6.4 — Kahan summation (Kahan, 1965)

  1. s \leftarrow 0, c \leftarrow 0.
  2. For i = 1, \dots, n: y \leftarrow x_i - c; t \leftarrow s + y; c \leftarrow (t - s) - y; s \leftarrow t.
  3. Return s.

Its error is at most (2u + O(nu^2)) \sum_i |x_i|, almost independent of n (Higham, 2002, section 4.3).

import math
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
xs = rng.standard_normal(100_000).astype(np.float32)
ref = math.fsum(xs.astype(np.float64))            # correctly rounded reference

def naive(v):                                      # left-to-right summation
    s = np.float32(0)
    for t in v:
        s = np.float32(s + t)
    return s

def kahan(v):                                      # Algorithm 6.4
    s, c = np.float32(0), np.float32(0)
    for t in v:
        y = np.float32(t - c); t2 = np.float32(s + y)
        c = np.float32((t2 - s) - y); s = t2
    return s

u32 = np.finfo(np.float32).eps / 2
n = len(xs)
bound = (n - 1) * u32 / (1 - (n - 1) * u32) * np.abs(xs).astype(np.float64).sum()
for name, val in [("left to right", naive(xs)), ("right to left", naive(xs[::-1])),
                  ("numpy pairwise", xs.sum()), ("kahan", kahan(xs))]:
    print(f"{name:15s} {float(val):.5f}  error {abs(float(val) - ref):.1e}")
print(f"worst-case bound of Theorem 6.2: {bound:.1e}")

The order or rounding of operations also changes with the number of threads of a reduction or of a BLAS library, with atomic additions on GPUs, with compiler options such as -ffast-math or fused multiply–add, and with library versions. PyTorch’s torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True) raises an error when an operation has no deterministic implementation. Python also randomises string hashes in each process, so the iteration order of a set of strings may vary unless PYTHONHASHSEED is fixed.

6.2 Pseudo-random numbers

Definition 6.5 — Pseudo-random generator A pseudo-random generator is a finite state set S, a transition f : S \to S and an output map g. A seed fixes the initial state s_0, then s_{k+1} = f(s_k), \qquad u_k = g(s_k) . As S is finite, the states are eventually periodic; the period is the length of the cycle. The whole sequence is a deterministic function of the seed.

Theorem 6.6 — Full period of a linear congruential generator (Hull and Dobell, 1962) The generator x_{k+1} = (a x_k + c) \bmod m has period m for every seed if and only if:

  • c and m are coprime;
  • a - 1 is divisible by every prime factor of m;
  • a - 1 is divisible by 4 if m is.

A proof is in Knuth (1997, section 3.2.1.2). Necessity of the first condition is immediate: if d = \gcd(c, m) > 1 and x_0 = 0, every term is a multiple of d, so at most m/d values are visited.

For m = 16: (a, c) = (5, 3) has period 16; (7, 3) fails the third condition and has period 4; (5, 2) fails the first and has period 8.

Python’s random module uses the Mersenne Twister, of period 2^{19937} - 1 (Matsumoto and Nishimura, 1998); NumPy’s default_rng uses PCG64 (O’Neill, 2014) and spawns independent streams from one master seed with SeedSequence. Four rules follow:

  • choose one master seed per experiment and record it;
  • derive one stream per worker, split or repetition, never seeding from the clock;
  • seed every library involved: NumPy, PyTorch (torch.manual_seed), data-loader workers;
  • report results over several seeds (Proposition 5.6).
import numpy as np

def period(a, c, m, x0=0):
    seen, x, k = {}, x0, 0
    while x not in seen:
        seen[x] = k
        x, k = (a * x + c) % m, k + 1
    return k - seen[x]

print([period(5, 3, 16), period(7, 3, 16), period(5, 2, 16)])   # [16, 4, 8]

def first_draws(master_seed):                       # one master seed, three streams
    children = np.random.SeedSequence(master_seed).spawn(3)
    return [float(np.random.default_rng(s).random()) for s in children]

print([round(x, 4) for x in first_draws(2026)])     # three different streams
print(first_draws(2026) == first_draws(2026))      # True: the same seed gives the same streams

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Recursive summation errs by at most \gamma_{n-1} \sum_i |x_i|; two orders agree within twice that, but not bit for bit.
  • Threads, GPUs, compiler options and library versions change the order of operations.
  • Kahan summation makes the error almost independent of n.
  • A pseudo-random sequence is a function of its seed: record one master seed, spawn streams, report over seeds.

7. Software environments, version control and provenance

Code that ran last year may not install today, and a figure may come from a version of the code that nobody can identify. This chapter explains why environments are hard to rebuild, how version control names every state of a project, and how a pipeline knows exactly what to recompute.

7.1 Software environments

Definition 7.1 — Software environment The environment of a computation is everything besides code and data that can change its result: interpreter, libraries and their exact versions, system libraries, compiler, operating system, drivers and hardware.

With semantic versioning, a version MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH increases MAJOR for incompatible changes, MINOR for compatible additions and PATCH for fixes. Constraints such as numpy>=1.24,<2 leave the installer a choice, and choosing well is hard in a precise sense.

Theorem 7.2 — Installability is NP-complete (Mancinelli et al., 2006) A repository is a finite set of packages, each with dependencies given as a conjunction of disjunctions of packages, and with pairs of conflicting packages. An installation contains the dependencies of its members and no conflicting pair. Deciding whether a given package belongs to some installation is NP-complete.

Proof. In NP: an installation is a certificate, checked in polynomial time.

NP-hard, by reduction from 3-SAT: for each variable x_i, create conflicting packages x_i^{+} and x_i^{-}, like two versions of one package.

For each clause C_j, create a package c_j depending on the disjunction of the packages of its literals; a target t depends on all the c_j.

A satisfying assignment gives an installation: t, the c_j, and the package matching each variable’s value.

Conversely, an installation containing t contains a literal package for each clause. Since x_i^{+} and x_i^{-} conflict, setting x_i true exactly when x_i^{+} is installed satisfies every clause. ∎

Installers therefore use SAT solvers or heuristics, whose answer depends on the solver and on the packages published since: the same requirement file can give different environments on different days. Remedies, from the weakest to the strongest:

  • pin exact versions, ideally with file hashes (pip install --require-hashes);
  • commit the lock file of the package manager, which records the resolved set;
  • ship a container image (Docker, Apptainer), which also freezes system libraries;
  • use a functional package manager (Nix, Guix), where each package is a function of its exact inputs; guix time-machine rebuilds an environment as it was at a past commit of the package collection.

7.2 Version control as a Merkle graph

Git names objects by their hash. A blob is a file’s content; a tree lists names, permissions and hashes of blobs and sub-trees; a commit holds the hash of a tree, the hashes of its parents, an author, a date and a message.

Definition 7.3 — Merkle DAG A Merkle DAG is a DAG whose nodes carry content and are named by the hash of their content together with the names of their children w_1, \dots, w_r, in a fixed order (Merkle, 1988): \mathrm{name}(v) = H\big(\mathrm{content}(v),\ \mathrm{name}(w_1), \dots, \mathrm{name}(w_r)\big).

Proposition 7.4 — A name commits to a whole history If two nodes of Merkle DAGs have the same name, either the sub-DAGs below them are identical, or a collision of H can be read off them.

Proof. By induction on the height. If the strings hashed for the two nodes differ, they form a collision. Otherwise the nodes have the same content and the same child names, and the induction hypothesis applies to each pair of children. ∎

A commit hash quoted in an article thus identifies every file and the whole history behind a result. Accidental collisions are excluded by Proposition 0.7. Deliberate collisions of SHA-1, git’s hash, became feasible in 2017 (Stevens et al.); git now detects the known attacks and supports SHA-256 repositories.

import hashlib
from graphlib import TopologicalSorter

def git_blob(data: bytes) -> str:
    """Name of a file in git: SHA-1 of a header and the content."""
    return hashlib.sha1(b"blob %d\0" % len(data) + data).hexdigest()

print(git_blob(b""))           # e69de29bb2d1d6434b8b29ae775ad8c2e48c5391, git's empty file
print(git_blob(b"hello\n"))    # equals: echo hello | git hash-object --stdin

def h(*parts: str) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256("\x1f".join(parts).encode()).hexdigest()[:12]

# A pipeline (Definition 7.5): each step = (code, parameters, inputs)
steps = {
    "data":    ("load v1",    "seed=0",  []),
    "clean":   ("drop NaN",   "",        ["data"]),
    "train":   ("fit ridge",  "lam=0.1", ["clean"]),
    "figure2": ("plot curve", "",        ["train"]),
    "table1":  ("summary",    "",        ["clean"]),
}

def keys(steps):                               # Proposition 7.6: hash in topological order
    order = TopologicalSorter({s: set(d[2]) for s, d in steps.items()}).static_order()
    k = {}
    for s in order:
        code, params, inputs = steps[s]
        k[s] = h(code, params, *[k[i] for i in inputs])
    return k

before = keys(steps)
steps["train"] = ("fit ridge", "lam=0.2", ["clean"])      # change one parameter
after = keys(steps)
print("recompute:", sorted(s for s in steps if before[s] != after[s]))   # ['figure2', 'train']

Commit often, tag the version used for each article (git tag paper-v1), never rewrite a history others have fetched, and write the commit hash into every result file.

7.3 Pipelines and provenance

Definition 7.5 — Pipeline A pipeline is a DAG of steps; each step s has code, parameters and input steps i_1, \dots, i_r, and produces a target that is a deterministic function of them. Its key is computed in a topological order (Proposition 0.5): \mathrm{key}(s) = H\big(\mathrm{code}_s,\ \mathrm{params}_s,\ \mathrm{key}(i_1), \dots, \mathrm{key}(i_r)\big).

Proposition 7.6 — Content-addressed recomputation is exact Change the code or parameters of one step s. Unless H has a collision, the keys that change are exactly those of s and its descendants; every other target can be reused from a cache indexed by keys.

Proof. In topological order: a step that is neither s nor a descendant of s hashes the same string, so its key is unchanged. The key of s hashes a different string, and so does each descendant, one of whose input keys changed; their keys change unless a collision occurs. ∎

The script checks it: changing the regularisation of train recomputes train and figure2, not table1. The classical make compares modification times, a weaker criterion; DVC, Nix and Guix index outputs by hashes of their inputs. Next to each result, store a provenance record, in the spirit of the W3C PROV model (2013): commit hash, lock file or image digest, command line, seeds, data checksums, hardware and date.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Choosing compatible versions is NP-complete: pin, lock, containerise or use a functional package manager.
  • Git is a Merkle DAG: a commit hash identifies a whole history unless a collision is exhibited.
  • Hashing each step with its inputs’ keys recomputes exactly what changed.
  • Store a provenance record with every result.

8. Publishing reproducible research: the editorial process

Publishing means passing an editorial process designed before code and data became part of research. This chapter follows a manuscript through it, models the noise of peer review, and describes the venues and identifiers that make code part of the publication.

8.1 The editorial process

  1. Submission, often after a preprint on arXiv or HAL.
  2. Desk check by the editor: scope, format, plagiarism; possible rejection without review.
  3. Peer review by two to four reviewers, single-anonymous, double-anonymous or open; conferences add area chairs, a rebuttal and sometimes public reviews on OpenReview.
  4. Decision and revision: accept, revise or reject; a revision comes with a response letter and a new round.
  5. Acceptance and contract: a copyright transfer or a licence to publish (ch. 9.3).
  6. Production and afterwards: proofs, the version of record with a DOI, then errata or, for serious errors, retraction.

8.2 Peer review as a noisy measurement

Definition 8.1 — A Gaussian model of review The quality of a submission is Q \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). Two independent committees observe S_A = Q + \varepsilon_A, \qquad S_B = Q + \varepsilon_B, \qquad \varepsilon_A, \varepsilon_B \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2), with Q, \varepsilon_A, \varepsilon_B independent, and each accepts the fraction a of best-scored submissions.

Proposition 8.2 — Agreement between committees Let \rho = 1/(1 + \tau^2), z_a = \Phi^{-1}(1 - a), and \Phi_2(\cdot, \cdot; \rho) the distribution function of a standard bivariate normal pair with correlation \rho. Then \mathbb{P}(B \text{ accepts} \mid A \text{ accepts}) = \frac{\Phi_2(-z_a, -z_a; \rho)}{a}.

Proof. S_A and S_B have variance 1 + \tau^2 and covariance \mathrm{Var}(Q) = 1.

Divided by \sqrt{1 + \tau^2}, they form a standard bivariate normal pair (U, V) with correlation \rho.

Each committee accepts above z_a, with probability a. By the symmetry (U, V) \mapsto (-U, -V), both accept with probability \Phi_2(-z_a, -z_a; \rho); divide by a. ∎

Example 8.3 — How consistent is acceptance? With an acceptance rate a = 0.25:

Noise \tau Correlation \rho \mathbb{P}(B \text{ accepts} \mid A \text{ accepts})
0.25 0.94 0.826
0.5 0.80 0.676
1.0 0.50 0.481
2.0 0.20 0.335

When review noise equals the spread of quality (\tau = 1), the other committee would reject more than half of the accepted papers.

In the 2014 NeurIPS experiment, part of the submissions went to two independent committees; Cortes and Lawrence (2021) report disagreement on a large share of each committee’s accepted papers, of the order of the case \tau = 1. Review filters for plausibility and seldom re-executes code: reproducibility must be built into the artefacts.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, multivariate_normal

def consistency(tau, a):                    # Proposition 8.2
    rho = 1 / (1 + tau**2)
    z = norm.ppf(1 - a)
    both = multivariate_normal(mean=[0, 0], cov=[[1, rho], [rho, 1]]).cdf([-z, -z])
    return both / a

for tau in [0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    print(f"tau={tau:4.2f}  rho={1/(1+tau**2):.2f}  P(B | A)={consistency(tau, 0.25):.3f}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)              # Monte Carlo check for tau = 1
Q = rng.standard_normal(200_000)
SA, SB = Q + rng.standard_normal(Q.size), Q + rng.standard_normal(Q.size)
accA, accB = SA > np.quantile(SA, 0.75), SB > np.quantile(SB, 0.75)
print(f"simulated: {(accA & accB).sum() / accA.sum():.3f}")

8.3 Venues that review the code

  • Artifact evaluation: the ACM awards badges after reviewing artefacts: Artifacts Available, Artifacts Evaluated (functional or reusable), Results Reproduced, Results Replicated.
  • Checklists: since its 2019 reproducibility programme (Pineau et al., 2021), NeurIPS asks authors for a checklist on code, data, hyperparameters and statistics.
  • ReScience C (Rougier et al., 2017) publishes replications of computational results, with open review of article and code on GitHub.
  • IPOL, Image Processing On Line (Limare and Morel, 2011), publishes each article with its source code and an online demonstrator, reviewed together: the reviewer checks that the code implements the algorithm described, and any reader can run it on their own data.

The object under review becomes a triple, text, code and demonstration, and the editorial process gains a step: reviewing the code against the pseudo-code, which ch. 10 prepares.

8.4 Identifiers and archives

  • A DOI names articles, data and code persistently; Zenodo, run by CERN, archives GitHub releases with a DOI.
  • Software Heritage archives public source code under intrinsic, git-compatible SWHIDs: the empty file is swh:1:cnt:e69de29bb2d1d6434b8b29ae775ad8c2e48c5391.
  • HAL, the French national open archive, accepts software deposits linked to Software Heritage.
  • ORCID identifies authors; a CITATION.cff file says how to cite software, following the principles of Smith et al. (2016).
  • The FAIR principles (Wilkinson et al., 2016) ask data and software to be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • A manuscript goes through submission, desk check, review, decision, revision, contract and production.
  • When review noise matches the spread of quality, acceptance is close to a coin flip among good papers.
  • Artifact evaluation, checklists, ReScience C and IPOL review code and data.
  • DOIs, SWHIDs, HAL and CITATION.cff make code citable.

9. Legal aspects of the code, the article and the data

A reproducibility package bundles code, text and data, which the law treats differently. This chapter explains who owns each, under which terms each can be shared, and when the whole package can be redistributed.

9.1 Three objects, three regimes

Object Main protection Typical terms
Code copyright (ch. 1.1), possibly patents (ch. 2.6) free software licences (ch. 2)
Article authors’ copyright, often transferred to the publisher copyright transfer, licence to publish, Creative Commons
Data facts are free; databases have specific rights; personal data are regulated CC0, CC BY, ODbL, Licence Ouverte, terms of use

9.2 The code: who owns it

  • In France, the economic rights on software created by employees in the exercise of their duties, or following their employer’s instructions, belong to the employer (article L113-9 of the Code de la propriété intellectuelle): releasing research code is the institution’s decision, often delegated to the laboratory.
  • Unless agreed otherwise, the author of software cannot oppose modifications that harm neither their honour nor their reputation (article L121-7).
  • For students, interns and visitors, it depends on their status and hosting agreement: check it early.

9.3 The article

Definition 9.1 — Versions of an article The preprint is the manuscript as submitted; the accepted manuscript is the authors’ final version after peer review, before the publisher’s formatting; the version of record is the version published by the publisher.

At acceptance, authors transfer their copyright to the publisher or grant it a licence to publish. In France, the law for a digital republic of 7 October 2016 created a right of open-access deposit, now article L533-4 of the Code de la recherche: for an article from research funded at least half by public funds, the author may make the accepted manuscript freely available after at most 6 months in science, technology and medicine, or 12 months in the humanities and social sciences, even after transferring their rights.

With the rights retention strategy of cOAlition S, authors announce at submission that CC BY applies to any resulting accepted manuscript. Creative Commons licences combine BY (attribution), SA (share alike), NC (non-commercial) and ND (no derivatives); only CC BY, CC BY-SA and CC0 allow every reuse, and none is meant for software.

9.4 The data

  • Facts are not protected; an original selection or arrangement of them can be.
  • Databases in the European Union have a sui generis right for 15 years when their making required a substantial investment (Directive 96/9/EC).
  • Text and data mining is allowed by Directive (EU) 2019/790 for research organisations on lawfully accessed works (article 3), and for others unless rights holders opt out (article 4).
  • Personal data fall under the GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), with health data as a special category (article 9) and research rules requiring safeguards such as pseudonymisation (article 89); pseudonymised data remain personal data.
  • Terms of use are a contract: data released “for non-commercial research only” cannot be redistributed by their users.

Common data licences are CC0, CC BY 4.0, the Open Database License (a copyleft) and Etalab’s Licence Ouverte 2.0.

9.5 A redistributable reproducibility package

Stodden (2009) proposed a Reproducible Research Standard: text and figures under CC BY, code under a permissive licence such as the modified BSD, data in the public domain, so that the package can be reused with attribution only.

Proposition 9.2 — When a package can be redistributed Model a package as separately licensed parts placed side by side: code components, texts and data sets. It can be redistributed as a whole if and only if each part may be redistributed under its own terms, and the code components combined into one program are compatible (Definition 2.3).

Proof. Parts placed side by side form an aggregation: each remains governed by its own terms, which must each allow redistribution. Only code combined into one program forms a combined work, whose distribution must satisfy all its licences at once; by Definition 2.3 and Proposition 2.4, this is the compatibility of its components. ∎

Example 9.3 — A package with restricted images A package holds MIT code, CC BY figures and images whose terms forbid redistribution. The code and figures may be shared; the images may not. Ship instead a script that downloads them from the official source and verifies their checksums (ch. 7.3), so that others obtain the same files under the original terms.

9.6 Models and weights

The legal status of trained weights is unsettled. The OSI’s Open Source AI Definition 1.0 (October 2024) requires the freedoms of Definition 1.2 for an AI system, with access to the complete code, the parameters, and sufficiently detailed information about the training data. Models whose weights are published with restrictions of use, often called open-weight, are not open source in this sense. The European AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) lightens some obligations for models under free and open-source licences, except general-purpose models with systemic risk; see Regulating AI.

Debate Should publicly funded research software be copyleft, so that improvements return to the public, or permissive, so that industry adopts it? Ex. 15 asks for a dissertation on this question.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Code, articles and data have different regimes, and a package must respect each.
  • In France the employer owns the economic rights on employees’ software.
  • French law lets authors deposit the accepted manuscript of a publicly funded article after at most 6 or 12 months.
  • Facts are free, databases may carry a 15-year right, personal data fall under the GDPR, terms of use bind users.
  • A package is redistributable when each part allows it and the combined code is compatible.

10. Writing a reproducible scientific article

This chapter turns the previous ones into a method for writing. Its central idea: the pseudo-code, the code, the numbers and the figures of an article should be checkable against each other, by the authors first and by any reader afterwards.

10.1 Anatomy of a reproducible article

  • Method: definitions, then the algorithm in pseudo-code, with every parameter named and given a default value.
  • Implementation notes: every deviation of the code from the pseudo-code, such as vectorisation or numerical safeguards.
  • Experiments: data and their provenance, protocol, seeds, statistics over repetitions, and the command producing each figure and table.
  • Limitations, with failure examples, and an availability statement with identifiers (ch. 8.4) and licences (ch. 9).

10.2 From pseudo-code to code

Definition 10.1 — Faithful implementation An implementation is faithful to a pseudo-code when each line of the pseudo-code is mapped to the code implementing it, every deviation is listed and justified in the article, and a test checks that the implementation and a literal transcription of the pseudo-code agree on several inputs.

Otsu’s method thresholds a grey-level image into two classes. Let p_i be the proportion of pixels of level i \in \{0, \dots, 255\}.

For a threshold t, class 0 holds the levels below t, with weight \omega_0 and mean \mu_0; class 1 the others, with weight \omega_1 = 1 - \omega_0 and mean \mu_1.

Algorithm 10.2 — Otsu’s threshold

  1. For t = 1, \dots, 255:
  2. split the pixels into levels < t and \ge t;
  3. compute the between-class variance \sigma_B^2(t) = \omega_0 \omega_1 (\mu_0 - \mu_1)^2;
  4. keep the first t that maximises \sigma_B^2(t).

Proposition 10.3 — Two forms of Otsu’s criterion Let \sigma^2 be the variance of all pixels, \mu_T their mean, and \mu(t) = \sum_{i<t} i\,p_i. Define the within-class variance \sigma_W^2(t) = \omega_0 \sigma_0^2 + \omega_1 \sigma_1^2 .

    1. \sigma^2 = \sigma_W^2(t) + \sigma_B^2(t), so maximising \sigma_B^2 minimises \sigma_W^2.
    1. Whenever 0 < \omega_0 < 1, \sigma_B^2(t) = \frac{\big(\mu_T\,\omega_0(t) - \mu(t)\big)^2}{\omega_0(t)\,\big(1 - \omega_0(t)\big)} .

Proof.

  1. is the law of total variance for the pixel level given its class: mean of the class variances plus variance of the class means, the latter being \omega_0 \omega_1 (\mu_0 - \mu_1)^2.

  2. Since \mu_T = \omega_0 \mu_0 + \omega_1 \mu_1 and \mu(t) = \omega_0 \mu_0, \mu_T\,\omega_0 - \mu(t) = \omega_0(\omega_0 - 1)\mu_0 + \omega_0 \omega_1 \mu_1 = \omega_0 \omega_1 (\mu_1 - \mu_0); square and divide by \omega_0 \omega_1. ∎

Form (b) needs only cumulative sums of the histogram, so all thresholds are evaluated in one pass: a deviation from the literal loop, to be documented and tested.

import numpy as np

def otsu_reference(img):
    """Algorithm 10.2 transcribed literally."""
    best_t, best_var = 0, -1.0
    for t in range(1, 256):                                  # line 1
        low, high = img[img < t], img[img >= t]              # line 2
        if low.size == 0 or high.size == 0:
            continue
        w0, w1 = low.size / img.size, high.size / img.size
        var_b = w0 * w1 * (low.mean() - high.mean()) ** 2    # line 3
        if var_b > best_var:                                 # line 4
            best_t, best_var = t, var_b
    return best_t

def otsu_fast(img):
    """Lines 2-3 for every t at once, by Proposition 10.3 (b): a documented deviation."""
    p = np.bincount(img.ravel(), minlength=256) / img.size
    w0 = np.cumsum(p)[:-1]                                   # omega_0(t), t = 1..255
    m0 = np.cumsum(p * np.arange(256))[:-1]                  # mu(t)
    mT = m0[-1] + 255 * p[255]
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        var_b = (mT * w0 - m0) ** 2 / (w0 * (1 - w0))
    var_b[~np.isfinite(var_b)] = -1.0                        # empty classes are skipped
    return int(np.argmax(var_b)) + 1                         # first maximiser (line 4)

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
for trial in range(3):                                       # test of Definition 10.1
    img = np.concatenate([rng.normal(70 + 20 * trial, 15, 6000), rng.normal(170, 25, 4000)])
    img = np.clip(img, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8).reshape(100, 100)
    print(otsu_reference(img), otsu_fast(img))
    assert otsu_reference(img) == otsu_fast(img), "implementation deviates from the pseudo-code"

10.3 Tests with tolerances

Floating-point tests must allow for rounding, and no more. By Corollary 6.3, a reference and an implementation summing the same n terms in two orders never differ by more than 2\gamma_{n-1} \sum_i |x_i|; a larger difference is a bug.

Example 10.4 — A tolerance that rounding cannot break For n = 10^6 binary64 terms with \sum_i |x_i| = 8 \times 10^5: 2\gamma_{n-1} \sum_i |x_i| \approx 2 \times 10^6 \times 1.11 \times 10^{-16} \times 8 \times 10^5 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-4} . Safe but pessimistic: a tighter tolerance must be justified by experiment and stated.

NumPy’s isclose(a, b) tests |a - b| \le \mathrm{atol} + \mathrm{rtol}\,|b|: it is not symmetric, and its default atol of 10^{-8} is meaningless near zero, so set both tolerances. Add regression tests comparing the article’s main numbers with stored values.

10.4 Reporting over seeds

Proposition 10.5 — How many seeds? If seed-to-seed scores have standard deviation \sigma, the 95 % normal confidence interval of the mean has half-width 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}, at most h as soon as n \ge \left(\frac{1.96\,\sigma}{h}\right)^2 . With \sigma = 0.5 and h = 0.2 point, n \ge 24.01: 25 seeds. For two methods on the same seeds, \mathrm{Var}(X - Y) = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y), which pairing reduces when the scores are positively correlated.

10.6 Auditing someone else’s article

Definition 10.6 — Audit levels This page proposes a cumulative scale for a published article, inspired by Peng (2011):

  • L0: the article only.
  • L1: the code is available under a licence allowing reuse.
  • L2: the code builds and runs in a documented environment.
  • L3: the published numbers and figures are regenerated within a stated tolerance.
  • L4: the code is faithful to the pseudo-code (Definition 10.1), deviations listed.
  • L5: an independent reimplementation from the article alone agrees.

An audit reports the level reached and each discrepancy, with whether it supports or weakens the article’s claims: the reproducibility part of the official project.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • A reproducible article names every parameter, documents every deviation, and says where everything is archived.
  • A faithful implementation is tested against a literal transcription of its pseudo-code.
  • Tolerances are explicit and justified by error bounds or experiments.
  • Report means and standard errors over enough seeds, on paired seeds.
  • The audit levels L0–L5 grade the reproducibility of an article.

11. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It points to active research on the questions of the course, in a neutral voice, and ends with open problems suitable for a dissertation or an internship.

11.1 Long-term reproducibility

Code decays even when nobody touches it: compilers, libraries, operating systems and hardware change around it. The Ten Years Reproducibility Challenge organised by ReScience C asked researchers to run again the code of their own articles published before 2010; Perkel (2020) reports on its lessons, among them missing dependencies and obsolete platforms.

Two lines of work address the problem at its root.

  • Archiving the source code itself. Software Heritage, described by Di Cosmo and Zacchiroli (2017), archives publicly available source code and names it by intrinsic identifiers (ch. 8.4).
  • Rebuilding environments bit for bit. Functional package managers make every package a function of its exact inputs. Dolstra and coauthors introduced Nix (2004); Courtès and Wurmus (2015) showed how Guix brings user-controlled, reproducible environments to high-performance computing.

Open questions remain for hardware: GPU generations, proprietary drivers and the emulation of obsolete platforms.

11.2 Reproducible arithmetic at scale

Corollary 6.3 shows that parallel sums depend on the order of operations. Demmel and Nguyen (2013) designed summation algorithms whose result is independent of the order and of the number of processors, at a moderate cost; such ideas underlie the ReproBLAS library.

Low precision makes the question sharper. The bfloat16 format used to train large networks has precision p = 8, so u = 2^{-8}; for a dot product of length n = 4096, nu = 16 and the bound of Theorem 6.2 no longer applies. This is why accumulations are done in higher precision.

The outputs of large language model inference servers can depend on the batch in which a request is processed, when kernels change their reduction order with the batch size. Making such systems deterministic without a large loss of speed is an open engineering and research problem.

11.3 Variance and comparisons in machine learning

  • Bouthillier and coauthors (2021) decomposed the variance of machine-learning benchmarks into its sources (data sampling, initialisation, data order, augmentation, hyperparameter search) and recommended randomising as many of them as possible when comparing methods.
  • Dodge and coauthors (2019) proposed to report the expected validation performance as a function of the tuning budget, rather than a single best score, an answer to the bias of Proposition 5.6.
  • Melis, Dyer and Blunsom (2018) found that well-tuned standard recurrent language models matched more recent architectures, and Lucic and coauthors (2018) found no evidence that the generative adversarial networks they compared consistently beat the original one under a fair budget.
  • Steegen and coauthors (2016) proposed the multiverse analysis: run every reasonable variant of an analysis and report the whole distribution of results.

11.4 Automated reproduction

Benchmarks now ask AI agents to reproduce research. PaperBench (Starace and coauthors, 2025) asks agents to replicate recent machine-learning papers from scratch, graded by detailed rubrics written with the papers’ authors. CORE-Bench (Siegel and coauthors, 2024) asks them to reproduce the results of published code capsules. Both show agents far from reliable. Whether automated checks of levels L2 to L4 (Definition 10.6) could become part of the editorial process is open.

11.5 Sustainability, security and law

  • Critical infrastructure maintained by few people. In 2024 a backdoor was found in the xz compression library (CVE-2024-3094), inserted by a contributor who had gained the trust of its single overloaded maintainer: a truck factor of 1 and an instance of the volunteer’s dilemma.
  • Regulation. The European Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) sets security obligations for products with digital elements and a lighter regime for open-source software stewards.
  • Value. Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou (2024) estimated that firms would have to spend several times more on software if open-source software did not exist.
  • Law and AI. Whether training on copyleft code creates obligations, whether model weights are protected, and how licences with use restrictions should be classified are open questions, now examined by courts and regulators.

11.6 Open problems

  • A measure of reproducibility that is cheap to compute, robust to gaming, and predictive of replicability.
  • Deterministic training and inference of large models at a negligible cost in speed.
  • Fair comparison of methods under different computational budgets.
  • Durable credit for research software and for maintenance, comparable to that given to articles.
  • Licences and definitions of openness adapted to models, data and their combination.

Key takeaways — chapter 11

  • Long-term reproducibility needs archived source code and environments rebuilt from exact inputs.
  • Order-independent summation exists; low precision and batched inference make determinism harder.
  • Fair comparisons in machine learning randomise every source of variance and account for the tuning budget.
  • Agents that reproduce papers are being benchmarked, and are not yet reliable.
  • The sustainability and security of critical free software are now matters of regulation.

Exercises

  1. ★ Free or not? Classify each case as free software (open source), source-available or proprietary, and name the freedom or criterion at stake: (a) a licence allowing use “for academic purposes only”; (b) GPL-3.0; (c) a repository on a public platform without a licence; (d) Apache-2.0 with the Commons Clause, which removes the right to sell the software; (e) CC BY-SA 4.0 for a data set.

    Solution: (a) not free: it discriminates against a field of endeavour and breaks freedom 0. (b) Free and open source. (c) Proprietary by default: all rights reserved (ch. 1.1), whatever its visibility.

    (d) Not open source: selling is a commercial use, so the restriction breaks freedoms 0 and 2. (e) A free licence for a non-software work: it allows every reuse under attribution and share-alike.

  2. ★ Reading a licence. For MIT and Apache-2.0, list the permissions, conditions and limitations of Definition 2.1. What must you do when you redistribute a modified Apache-2.0 file?

    Solution: MIT permits everything, under the single condition of keeping the copyright and permission notice; it excludes warranty and liability. Apache-2.0 adds an explicit patent licence (section 3), which ends for whoever sues over patents, and excludes trademark rights (section 6).

    To redistribute a modified file: give recipients a copy of the licence, mark the modified files with prominent notices, keep the copyright and attribution notices, and include the attribution notices of a NOTICE file if the project has one (section 4).

  3. ★ Combining licences. With the facts of ch. 2.3, decide whether each set can be distributed as one program, and under which licence: (a) MIT, BSD-3-Clause and GPL-2.0-only; (b) Apache-2.0 and GPL-3.0-or-later; (c) GPL-2.0-only and GPL-3.0-only; (d) GPL-3.0-only and AGPL-3.0-only.

    Solution: (a) yes, as a whole under GPL-2.0-only, keeping the MIT and BSD notices; the SPDX expression of the source is MIT AND BSD-3-Clause AND GPL-2.0-only. (b) Yes, under the GPL version 3. (c) No. (d) Yes, by section 13 of both licences; the AGPL conditions continue to apply to the AGPL part.

  4. ★ The volunteer’s dilemma in numbers. With v = 6 and c = 3, compute the abstention probability q, the probability that nobody volunteers, and the expected number of volunteers for n = 2 and n = 3. What is the limit as n \to \infty?

    Solution: here c/v = 0.5. For n = 2: q = 0.5, nobody with probability 0.25, expected volunteers 2 \times 0.5 = 1.

    For n = 3: q = 0.5^{1/2} \approx 0.707, nobody with probability 0.5^{3/2} \approx 0.354, expected volunteers 3(1 - q) \approx 0.879. The probability that nobody volunteers tends to c/v = 0.5.

  5. ★ Positive predictive value and replication. (a) Compute the PPV for R = 0.25, \alpha = 0.05 and power 0.5. (b) A study reports p = 0.01; under the assumption of Proposition 5.4, what is the probability that an identical replication is significant? (c) What is the probability of at least one false positive among 10 independent tests at level 0.05?

    Solution: (a) 0.125 / (0.125 + 0.05) \approx 0.714. (b) \Phi(2.576 - 1.960) = \Phi(0.616) \approx 0.731. (c) 1 - 0.95^{10} \approx 0.401.

  6. ★ Floating point by hand. (a) In binary64, compare (0.1 + 0.2) + 0.3 and 0.1 + (0.2 + 0.3). (b) Find the smallest positive integer N such that float32(N) + 1 equals float32(N).

    Solution: (a) Python prints 0.6000000000000001 and 0.6: each sum is correctly rounded (Proposition 0.2), but the intermediate roundings differ, so associativity fails.

    (b) N = 2^{24} = 16{,}777{,}216. Binary32 has p = 24, so integers up to 2^{24} are exact. Beyond it, consecutive floats are 2 apart, and 2^{24} + 1 is a tie, rounded to the even neighbour 2^{24}.

  7. ★ Names in git (Python). Compute the git name of a file containing hello\n, then of the same file with one character changed. Build a tiny tree object from two files and check that renaming a file changes the tree’s name but not the blobs’ names.

    Solution:

    import hashlib

def obj(kind: bytes, data: bytes) -> bytes:          # raw 20-byte git name
    return hashlib.sha1(kind + b" %d\0" % len(data) + data).digest()

def tree(entries):                                   # entries: {filename: content}
    body = b"".join(b"100644 " + name.encode() + b"\0" + obj(b"blob", data)
                    for name, data in sorted(entries.items()))
    return obj(b"tree", body).hex()

print(obj(b"blob", b"hello\n").hex())   # ce013625030ba8dba906f756967f9e9ca394464a
print(obj(b"blob", b"hellp\n").hex())   # a completely different name
t1 = tree({"a.py": b"x = 1\n", "b.py": b"y = 2\n"})
t2 = tree({"a.py": b"x = 1\n", "c.py": b"y = 2\n"})  # b.py renamed to c.py
print(t1 != t2, obj(b"blob", b"y = 2\n").hex())      # True, and the blob name is unchanged

    The tree hashes the file names together with the blob names (Definition 7.3), so a rename changes the tree, and therefore every commit that contains it.

  8. ★★ Compatibility graphs (Python). In the model of Definition 2.3, prove that compatibility of two licences is reflexive and symmetric, show with ch. 2.3 that it is not transitive, and prove that the compatible sets are exactly the cliques of the compatibility graph. Compute the maximal compatible sets of the six licences of the code of ch. 2.3.

    Solution: reflexivity holds because O(\ell) \subseteq A(\ell); symmetry because the definition of a compatible set does not depend on the order. MIT is compatible with Apache-2.0 and with GPL-2.0-only, which are not compatible with each other. By Proposition 2.4, a set is compatible exactly when all its pairs are, that is, when it is a clique.

    import networkx as nx
from itertools import combinations

incompatible = {("Apache-2.0", "GPL-2.0-only"), ("GPL-2.0-only", "GPL-3.0-or-later"),
                ("GPL-2.0-only", "AGPL-3.0-only")}
names = ["MIT", "BSD-3-Clause", "Apache-2.0", "GPL-2.0-only", "GPL-3.0-or-later",
         "AGPL-3.0-only"]
G = nx.Graph()
G.add_nodes_from(names)
G.add_edges_from(p for p in combinations(names, 2)
                 if p not in incompatible and p[::-1] not in incompatible)
for clique in nx.find_cliques(G):
    print(sorted(clique))

    The two maximal compatible sets are {MIT, BSD-3-Clause, GPL-2.0-only} and {MIT, BSD-3-Clause, Apache-2.0, GPL-3.0-or-later, AGPL-3.0-only}.

  9. ★★ When the greedy truck factor fails. A project has 10 files: 3 written by X alone, 5 written jointly by A and B, one by Y alone and one by Z alone. Compute the truck factor exactly, then run the greedy heuristic of ch. 3.6 by hand.

    Solution: more than half means at least 6 orphaned files. No pair suffices: the best pairs, \{A, B\} and \{X, Y\}, orphan 5 and 4 files. The triple \{A, B, X\} orphans 8 files, so \mathrm{TF} = 3.

    The greedy heuristic first removes X (3 files), then Y (4 files), then Z (5 files), since removing A or B alone orphans nothing.

    It then needs both A and B, and returns 5. The heuristic misses departures that only matter together.

  10. ★★ The best of n seeds. Let X_1, X_2, X_3 be independent standard normal variables, with density \varphi and distribution function \Phi. (a) Show that \mathbb{E}\big[\max_i X_i\big] = 3 \int_{\mathbb{R}} x\,\varphi(x)\,\Phi(x)^2\,dx .

    1. Using \varphi'(x) = -x\,\varphi(x), deduce that e_3 = 3/(2\sqrt{\pi}) \approx 0.846, and check the value e_2 = 1/\sqrt{\pi} of Proposition 5.6 by the same method.

    Hints and expected result: (a) the maximum is X_1 with probability \Phi(X_1)^2 given X_1, and the three variables play symmetric roles. (b) An integration by parts turns \int x\,\varphi\,\Phi^2 into 2 \int \varphi^2\,\Phi.

    Then \varphi(x)^2 equals \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} times the density of \mathcal{N}(0, 1/2). If Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/2) is independent of Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), the remaining integral is \mathbb{P}(Z \le Y) = 1/2.

    Hence \int x\,\varphi\,\Phi^2 = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} and e_3 = \frac{3}{2\sqrt{\pi}}. For two seeds, the same steps give e_2 = 2\int x\,\varphi\,\Phi\,dx = 2\int \varphi^2\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} .

  11. ★★ How large are summation errors really? (Python) Sum n standard normal numbers in binary32 from left to right, in 20 random orders, for n = 10^3, 10^4, 10^5. Compare the standard deviation of the results with the bound of Corollary 6.3, and with the estimate obtained by treating the rounding errors as independent, of mean 0 and standard deviation about u/\sqrt{3}: \frac{u}{\sqrt{3}} \Big( \sum_{k=1}^{n} s_k^2 \Big)^{1/2}, \qquad s_k = x_1 + \dots + x_k .

    Solution:

    import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
u = np.finfo(np.float32).eps / 2
for n in [1_000, 10_000, 100_000]:
    x = rng.standard_normal(n).astype(np.float32)
    sums = [np.cumsum(x[rng.permutation(n)], dtype=np.float32)[-1] for _ in range(20)]
    A = np.abs(x).astype(np.float64).sum()
    bound = 2 * (n - 1) * u / (1 - (n - 1) * u) * A               # Corollary 6.3
    partial = np.cumsum(x.astype(np.float64))
    typical = u / np.sqrt(3) * np.sqrt((partial**2).sum())        # random-sign estimate
    print(f"n={n:6d}  std={np.std(sums):.1e}  estimate={typical:.1e}  bound={bound:.1e}")

    The standard deviation (about 2 \times 10^{-5}, 10^{-4} and 8 \times 10^{-4}) is within a factor 1.5 of the random-sign estimate.

    For these zero-mean data the partial sums grow like \sqrt{k}, so the typical error grows roughly like n, while the worst-case bound grows like n^2: at n = 10^5 they differ by six orders of magnitude. The bound remains the only guarantee.

  12. ★★ From 3-SAT to packages (Python). Encode the formula (x_1 \lor \lnot x_2 \lor x_3) \land (\lnot x_1 \lor x_2) \land (\lnot x_3) as a repository, following the proof of Theorem 7.2. Find an installation of the target by exhaustive search, and read a satisfying assignment from it.

    Solution:

    from itertools import product

clauses = [[1, -2, 3], [-1, 2], [-3]]                 # literal +i is x_i, -i is not x_i
pkg = lambda lit: f"x{abs(lit)}{'+' if lit > 0 else '-'}"
depends = {"t": [[f"c{j}"] for j in range(len(clauses))]}   # conjunction of disjunctions
depends.update({f"c{j}": [[pkg(l) for l in C]] for j, C in enumerate(clauses)})
conflicts = {(f"x{i}+", f"x{i}-") for i in (1, 2, 3)}

def is_installation(S):
    deps_ok = all(any(d in S for d in disj) for p in S for disj in depends.get(p, []))
    return deps_ok and not any(a in S and b in S for a, b in conflicts)

base = {"t", "c0", "c1", "c2"}
for signs in product("+-", repeat=3):
    S = base | {f"x{i + 1}{s}" for i, s in enumerate(signs)}
    if is_installation(S):
        print(sorted(S), {f"x{i + 1}": s == "+" for i, s in enumerate(signs)})

    Two installations exist, with x_3 false and x_1 = x_2; for example x_1 = x_2 = true satisfies the three clauses.

  13. ★★ Review noise and a redistributable package. (a) In the model of Definition 8.1 with a = 0.25, find numerically the noise \tau for which \mathbb{P}(B \text{ accepts} \mid A \text{ accepts}) = 0.6. (b) A package combines into one program MIT code, an Apache-2.0 module and a GPL-2.0-only module, and ships CC BY figures beside it. Can it be redistributed, and what is the smallest change that makes it redistributable?

    Hints and expected result: (a) the function consistency of ch. 8.2 decreases in \tau; a bisection between 0.5 and 1 (for instance scipy.optimize.brentq) gives \tau \approx 0.66.

    (b) No: by Proposition 9.2 the figures are fine, but the combined program contains the incompatible pair Apache-2.0 and GPL-2.0-only. The smallest change is to replace one of these two modules, or to obtain “version 2 or later” for the GPL module and distribute the program under the GPL version 3.

  14. ★★ Otsu and seeds. (a) Prove the decomposition \sigma^2 = \sigma_W^2 + \sigma_B^2 of Proposition 10.3 from the definitions of the class weights and means. (b) A method’s score has seed-to-seed standard deviation 0.8 point. How many seeds give a 95 % interval of half-width 0.25 point? (c) Two methods run on the same seeds have equal standard deviations 0.8 and correlation 0.9; how many seeds are needed for the same precision on their difference?

    Solution: (a) Write \sum_i p_i (i - \mu_T)^2 as the sum over the two classes, and expand i - \mu_T = (i - \mu_k) + (\mu_k - \mu_T) in class k. The cross terms vanish by definition of \mu_k, which leaves \omega_0 \sigma_0^2 + \omega_1 \sigma_1^2 + \omega_0 (\mu_0 - \mu_T)^2 + \omega_1 (\mu_1 - \mu_T)^2 . The last two terms equal \omega_0 \omega_1 (\mu_0 - \mu_1)^2.

    (b) (1.96 \times 0.8 / 0.25)^2 \approx 39.3, so 40 seeds.

    (c) The variance of the difference is 0.64 + 0.64 - 2 \times 0.9 \times 0.64 = 0.128, a standard deviation of about 0.358. Then (1.96 \times 0.358 / 0.25)^2 \approx 7.9, so 8 seeds suffice: pairing divides the cost by five.

  15. ★★★ Individual dissertation. Write a two-page dissertation on the question of ch. 9: should publicly funded research software be released under a copyleft or a permissive licence?

  16. ★★★ Audit an IPOL article. Choose an article of the journal Image Processing On Line, for instance “Anatomy of the SIFT Method” (Rey-Otero and Delbracio, 2014).
