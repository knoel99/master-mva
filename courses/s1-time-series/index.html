0. Review: probability, least squares and Fourier analysis

Three M1 tools carry most of the course: conditioning of Gaussian vectors (prediction, Kalman filtering, imputation), least squares (autoregressions, dictionaries, features) and the discrete Fourier transform (spectra, convolutions, filters). This chapter states them in the form used later, with short proofs.

Definition 0.1 — Mean, covariance, Gaussian vector For a random vector X \in \mathbb{R}^d with finite second moments, the mean is \mu = \mathbb{E}[X] and the covariance is \Sigma = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)(X - \mu)^\top\big], a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix.

X is Gaussian, X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma), if every linear form a^\top X is a real Gaussian variable. When \Sigma is invertible, X has the density p(x) = (2\pi)^{-d/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\Big).

Proposition 0.2 — Best linear predictor and Gaussian conditioning Let (X, Y) be a random vector with means \mu_X, \mu_Y, covariance blocks \Sigma_{XX}, \Sigma_{XY}, \Sigma_{YY}, and \Sigma_{YY} invertible.

Among all affine functions of Y, the one that minimises the mean squared error of prediction of X is

Key result — best linear predictor \hat X = \mu_X + \Sigma_{XY} \Sigma_{YY}^{-1} (Y - \mu_Y),

with error covariance \Sigma_{XX} - \Sigma_{XY} \Sigma_{YY}^{-1} \Sigma_{YX}.

If (X, Y) is Gaussian, then \hat X = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y], and the conditional law of X given Y is Gaussian with this mean and this covariance.

Proof. Orthogonality. The error E = X - \hat X has mean zero and \mathrm{Cov}(E, Y) = \Sigma_{XY} - \Sigma_{XY} \Sigma_{YY}^{-1} \Sigma_{YY} = 0.

Optimality. Any other affine predictor writes \hat X + a + B(Y - \mu_Y). Its error is E - a - B(Y - \mu_Y), whose second moment is that of E plus a non-negative term, because E is centred and uncorrelated with Y.

Gaussian case. (E, Y) is Gaussian and uncorrelated, hence independent. So X = \hat X + E given Y is \hat X plus an independent Gaussian of covariance \mathrm{Cov}(E), which expands to the stated formula. ∎

The same orthogonality principle — the error is orthogonal to everything the predictor may use — gives the Yule–Walker equations (ch. 2), the Kalman filter (ch. 2) and the Wiener filter (ch. 6).

Proposition 0.3 — Least squares and ridge regression Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} and b \in \mathbb{R}^n. Every minimiser of \|Ax - b\|^2 solves the normal equations A^\top A x = A^\top b.

For \lambda > 0 the ridge problem \min_x \|Ax - b\|^2 + \lambda \|x\|^2 has the unique solution x_\lambda = (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1} A^\top b.

Proof. The objective is a convex quadratic with gradient 2A^\top(Ax - b) + 2\lambda x. Setting it to zero gives the two formulas. For \lambda > 0 the matrix A^\top A + \lambda I is positive definite, hence invertible, and the objective is strictly convex. ∎

Definition 0.4 — Discrete Fourier transform For x = (x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}) \in \mathbb{C}^n, the DFT is \hat x_k = \sum_{t=0}^{n-1} x_t\, e^{-2i\pi k t / n}, \qquad k = 0, \dots, n - 1.

The index k corresponds to the frequency k/n cycles per sample, or k f_s / n hertz for a sampling rate f_s. The FFT computes all coefficients in \mathcal{O}(n \log n) operations.

Theorem 0.5 — Convolution theorem and Parseval Let the circular convolution be (x \circledast y)_t = \sum_{s=0}^{n-1} x_s\, y_{(t - s) \bmod n}.

Then, for every k, \widehat{x \circledast y}_k = \hat x_k\, \hat y_k, \qquad \sum_{t} |x_t|^2 = \frac1n \sum_k |\hat x_k|^2.

Proof. Convolution. Expand and substitute u = t - s \bmod n: \sum_t \sum_s x_s y_{t-s} e^{-2i\pi k t/n} = \sum_s x_s e^{-2i\pi k s/n} \sum_u y_u e^{-2i\pi k u/n}.

Parseval. The vectors e_k = (e^{2i\pi k t/n})_t are orthogonal with \|e_k\|^2 = n, and \hat x_k = \langle x, e_k \rangle. Expanding x in this basis gives the identity. ∎

A linear filtering, a cross-correlation or a sliding inner product therefore costs \mathcal{O}(n \log n) instead of \mathcal{O}(n^2). This speeds up the matrix profile (ch. 3) and convolutional sparse coding (ch. 4).

Note 0.6 — Sampling and aliasing A continuous signal whose spectrum vanishes above B hertz is determined by its samples taken at f_s > 2B (Shannon–Nyquist). Below that rate, a frequency f is indistinguishable from f - f_s: it aliases.

In practice, sensors filter before sampling, and a series analysed here is a sequence x_t, t = 1, \dots, n, with a known sampling rate. Irregular sampling is discussed in ch. 10.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The best linear predictor makes the error orthogonal to the data; for Gaussian vectors it is the conditional expectation.
  • Least squares and ridge regression have closed forms; ridge is always well posed.
  • The DFT diagonalises circular convolution and preserves energy up to the factor n.

1. Representing a time series: stationarity, spectra and scales

Before learning anything from a series, one must decide what to look at: the raw samples, their correlations across lags, their frequencies, or their variations at several scales. This chapter defines these representations, says when they are meaningful (stationarity) and how to estimate them.

Time series as data

Definition 1.1 — Time series A (regularly sampled) time series is a finite sequence x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) with x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d. The sample x_t is observed at time t / f_s, for a sampling rate f_s.

The series is univariate when d = 1 and multivariate when d > 1, with one coordinate per channel or sensor.

The statistical model is a stochastic process (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{Z}} of which x is a finite realisation. A learning problem uses either one long series (forecasting, segmentation) or a data set of series x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}, possibly of different lengths (classification, clustering).

Note 1.2 — The tasks of the course The official themes correspond to a handful of tasks:

  • modelling and representing a series — this chapter, ch. 2 and ch. 4;
  • forecasting its future values — ch. 2;
  • recognising and extracting patterns, classifying series — ch. 3, ch. 4 and ch. 7;
  • detecting changes and anomalies — ch. 5;
  • repairing a series: denoising and imputing missing values — ch. 6;
  • exploiting the structure between channels — ch. 8.

One warning holds for all of them: samples are dependent. A random train/test split mixes past and future, and statistics computed on the whole series leak information. Every evaluation in this course respects the order of time.

Stationarity and autocorrelation

Definition 1.3 — Weak stationarity, autocovariance A process (X_t) with finite second moments is weakly stationary if its mean \mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu does not depend on t and its covariance depends only on the lag: \gamma(h) = \mathrm{Cov}(X_{t+h}, X_t) \quad \text{for all } t, h \in \mathbb{Z}.

The function \gamma is the autocovariance, and \rho(h) = \gamma(h) / \gamma(0) the autocorrelation (ACF).

Proposition 1.4 — Properties of an autocovariance For a weakly stationary process:

  • \gamma(-h) = \gamma(h) and |\gamma(h)| \le \gamma(0);

  • \gamma is positive semi-definite: for all n, all times t_1, \dots, t_n and all a \in \mathbb{R}^n,

    \sum_{i,j} a_i a_j\, \gamma(t_i - t_j) \ \ge\ 0.

In particular the Toeplitz matrix \Gamma_n = [\gamma(i - j)]_{i,j = 1}^{n} is positive semi-definite.

Proof. Symmetry: \gamma(-h) = \mathrm{Cov}(X_{t-h}, X_t), and shifting both times by h gives \mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \gamma(h).

Bound: by Cauchy–Schwarz, |\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t+h}, X_t)| \le \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{t+h}) \mathrm{Var}(X_t)} = \gamma(0).

Positivity: the double sum is \mathrm{Var}\big(\sum_i a_i X_{t_i}\big) \ge 0. ∎

Definition 1.5 — Sample autocovariance and autocorrelation For observations x_1, \dots, x_n with mean \bar x, and 0 \le h < n, \hat\gamma(h) = \frac1n \sum_{t=1}^{n-h} (x_{t+h} - \bar x)(x_t - \bar x), \qquad \hat\rho(h) = \frac{\hat\gamma(h)}{\hat\gamma(0)}.

The factor 1/n (not 1/(n-h)) keeps the estimated Toeplitz matrix positive semi-definite. Under mild conditions \hat\rho(h) is consistent. For a white noise, \hat\rho(h) is approximately \mathcal{N}(0, 1/n), which gives the bands \pm 1.96/\sqrt n drawn on ACF plots.

Example 1.6 — Three reference processes Let (\varepsilon_t) be i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance \sigma^2.

  • White noise X_t = \varepsilon_t: \gamma(0) = \sigma^2 and \gamma(h) = 0 for h \ne 0.

  • Random walk S_t = \varepsilon_1 + \dots + \varepsilon_t: \mathrm{Var}(S_t) = t\sigma^2 grows with t, so S is not stationary. Its increments S_t - S_{t-1} = \varepsilon_t are.

  • AR(1) X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t with |\phi| < 1: the stationary solution (ch. 2) has

    \gamma(h) = \frac{\sigma^2 \phi^{|h|}}{1 - \phi^2}, \qquad \rho(h) = \phi^{|h|}.

For the AR(1), the stationary solution X_t = \sum_{j \ge 0} \phi^j \varepsilon_{t-j} (proved in Proposition 2.2) makes \varepsilon_t uncorrelated with every earlier value X_{t-h}, h \ge 1.

Multiplying the equation by X_{t-h} and taking expectations gives \gamma(h) = \phi\,\gamma(h-1) for h \ge 1. Taking variances gives \gamma(0) = \phi^2 \gamma(0) + \sigma^2. The two formulas above follow.

With \phi = 0.7: \rho(1) = 0.7, \rho(2) = 0.49, \rho(3) = 0.343. The code below recovers these values from 4096 samples.

Spectral representation

The autocovariance describes dependence across lags; its Fourier transform describes how the variance is spread over frequencies.

Theorem 1.7 — Spectral density Let (X_t) be weakly stationary with \sum_h |\gamma(h)| < \infty. The function f(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{h \in \mathbb{Z}} \gamma(h)\, e^{-ih\omega}, \qquad \omega \in [-\pi, \pi], is continuous, even and non-negative. It determines the autocovariance:

Key result — Wiener–Khinchin pair \gamma(h) = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{ih\omega} f(\omega)\, d\omega, \qquad f(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{h} \gamma(h) e^{-ih\omega}.

Proof. Inversion. The series converges uniformly, so it may be integrated term by term. Since \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{i(h-k)\omega}\, d\omega = 2\pi\, \mathbb{1}[h = k], only the term k = h survives.

Non-negativity. Assume \mu = 0 and set f_n(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi n}\, \mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{t=1}^n X_t e^{-it\omega}\Big|^2 \ \ge\ 0.

Expanding the square and grouping the pairs (s, t) by lag h = s - t gives f_n(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{|h| < n} \Big(1 - \frac{|h|}{n}\Big) \gamma(h)\, e^{-ih\omega}.

By dominated convergence (the weights are bounded by 1 and \gamma is summable), f_n(\omega) \to f(\omega), which is therefore \ge 0. Evenness follows from \gamma(-h) = \gamma(h). ∎

White noise has a flat spectrum f = \sigma^2 / (2\pi). The AR(1) of Example 1.6 has f(\omega) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi\,(1 - 2\phi\cos\omega + \phi^2)}, which concentrates the variance at low frequencies when \phi > 0. It follows by summing the two geometric series of \sum_h \phi^{|h|} e^{-ih\omega}.

Definition 1.8 — Periodogram At the Fourier frequencies \omega_k = 2\pi k / n, the periodogram of x_1, \dots, x_n is I_n(\omega_k) = \frac{1}{2\pi n} \Big| \sum_{t=1}^n (x_t - \bar x)\, e^{-it\omega_k} \Big|^2 .

It is the squared modulus of the DFT (Definition 0.4), rescaled. For k \ne 0 it equals I_n(\omega_k) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{|h| < n} \hat\gamma(h)\, e^{-ih\omega_k}, so it is the plug-in estimator of f.

Proposition 1.9 — The periodogram is not consistent Under the assumptions of Theorem 1.7, the bias \mathbb{E}[I_n(\omega_k)] - f(\omega_k) tends to 0 uniformly over the Fourier frequencies \omega_k \ne 0. However, for an i.i.d. \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) white noise and 0 < k < n/2, I_n(\omega_k) \sim \frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{\chi^2_2}{2}, whose variance (\sigma^2 / 2\pi)^2 does not decrease with n.

Proof. Bias. At a Fourier frequency \omega_k \ne 0, \sum_t e^{-it\omega_k} = 0, so subtracting \bar x changes nothing and \mathbb{E}[I_n(\omega_k)] = f_n(\omega_k), with f_n as in the proof of Theorem 1.7. The bound |f_n(\omega) - f(\omega)| \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_h \min\Big(1, \frac{|h|}{n}\Big)\, |\gamma(h)| does not depend on \omega and tends to 0.

Variance. For 0 < k < n/2, the real and imaginary parts of \sum_t \varepsilon_t e^{-it\omega_k} are Gaussian, centred, uncorrelated (orthogonality of cosines and sines at Fourier frequencies), each of variance n\sigma^2/2. Their squared sum divided by n\sigma^2/2 is a \chi^2_2, and I_n = \frac{\sigma^2}{4\pi}\chi^2_2. ∎

The periodogram must therefore be smoothed. Welch’s method cuts the series into K overlapping windowed segments and averages their periodograms. The variance is divided by about K, and the frequency resolution, now set by the segment length, is coarser by the same factor.

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import welch

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, phi = 4096, 0.7
eps = rng.standard_normal(n)
ar = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):                       # AR(1): x_t = 0.7 x_{t-1} + eps_t
    ar[t] = phi * ar[t - 1] + eps[t]
x = ar + 1.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.1 * np.arange(n))   # + a period-10 cycle

def acf(x, max_lag):                        # sample ACF (Definition 1.5)
    xc = x - x.mean()
    g = np.array([xc[: len(x) - h] @ xc[h:] for h in range(max_lag + 1)]) / len(x)
    return g / g[0]

print("sample ACF, lags 0-3:", acf(ar, 3).round(2))
print("theory 0.7^h        :", (phi ** np.arange(4)).round(2))
f, P = welch(x, fs=1.0, nperseg=250)        # averaged periodogram
print("frequency of the spectral peak:", f[np.argmax(P)], "cycle/sample")

The output gives the ACF (1, 0.70, 0.49, 0.35) against the theoretical (1, 0.70, 0.49, 0.34), and a spectral peak at f = 0.1 \text{ cycle per sample}, the hidden period of 10 samples, invisible on the raw ACF of the noisy mixture.

Time–frequency and multiscale representations

A spectrum averages over the whole series. Speech, gait or an electrocardiogram change over time, so one needs representations that are localised both in time and in frequency.

Definition 1.10 — Short-time Fourier transform, spectrogram Let w be a window of length M (Hann, for instance) and H a hop size. The STFT of x is X(m, k) = \sum_{t} x_t\, w(t - mH)\, e^{-2i\pi k (t - mH) / M}, and the spectrogram is |X(m, k)|^2: a periodogram of each windowed frame, with frames indexed by m.

A long window resolves close frequencies but blurs fast events; a short window does the opposite. The Gabor–Heisenberg inequality makes the trade-off unavoidable: the product of the time spread and the frequency spread of a window is bounded below by a universal constant.

Wavelets resolve the trade-off differently: short windows for high frequencies, long ones for low frequencies. The simplest wavelet basis, Haar’s, is enough to state the main facts.

Definition 1.11 — Haar wavelet transform Let n = 2^J and a^{(0)} = x. For j = 1, \dots, J and k = 0, \dots, n 2^{-j} - 1, set a^{(j)}_k = \frac{a^{(j-1)}_{2k} + a^{(j-1)}_{2k+1}}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad d^{(j)}_k = \frac{a^{(j-1)}_{2k} - a^{(j-1)}_{2k+1}}{\sqrt 2}.

The Haar transform of x is the vector W x = (a^{(J)}, d^{(J)}, \dots, d^{(1)}). The coefficient d^{(j)}_k measures the variation of x at scale 2^j around the time k 2^j.

Proposition 1.12 — The Haar transform is orthonormal and fast W is an orthogonal matrix, so \|Wx\| = \|x\|, \qquad x = W^\top (Wx), and both Wx and W^\top c cost \mathcal{O}(n) operations: fewer than 4n additions and multiplications.

Proof. Each level applies the orthogonal matrix \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} to disjoint pairs of entries. Level j is thus orthogonal on the current approximation and the identity on the details already computed. A product of orthogonal maps is orthogonal.

Level j processes n 2^{-j} pairs, so there are \sum_{j \ge 1} n 2^{-j} = n - 1 pairs in all. Each pair costs two additions and two multiplications by 1/\sqrt 2, hence fewer than 4n operations. ∎

A piecewise-constant signal has nonzero Haar details only near its jumps: few large coefficients carry it. This sparsity is what wavelet denoising exploits in ch. 6.

Trend and seasonality

Many series are a slow trend plus a periodic seasonal component plus a stationary remainder, x_t = m_t + s_t + r_t. Differencing removes the first two.

Proposition 1.13 — Differencing Let \nabla x_t = x_t - x_{t-1} and \nabla_p x_t = x_t - x_{t-p}.

  • If m_t is a polynomial of degree k in t, then \nabla^k m_t is constant and \nabla^{k+1} m_t = 0.

  • If s_t is p-periodic, then \nabla_p s_t = 0.

  • If r is stationary with spectral density f_r, then \nabla r is stationary with density

    f_{\nabla r}(\omega) = |1 - e^{-i\omega}|^2 f_r(\omega) = (2 - 2\cos\omega)\, f_r(\omega).

Proof. Polynomials. t^k - (t-1)^k = k t^{k-1} + (\text{lower degree}), so \nabla lowers the degree by exactly one; after k steps a constant remains, killed by one more step.

Periodic part. s_t - s_{t-p} = 0 by definition.

Spectrum. Expanding the covariance of the differences, \gamma_{\nabla r}(h) = 2\gamma_r(h) - \gamma_r(h+1) - \gamma_r(h-1). Inserting \gamma_r(h) = \int e^{ih\omega} f_r gives the factor 2 - e^{i\omega} - e^{-i\omega}. ∎

Differencing thus removes trends but amplifies high frequencies: it should be used once or twice, not by reflex.

Example 1.14 — Removing a trend and a season Let x_t = 1 + 0.5\,t + s_t with the period-4 season s = (2, 0, -2, 0, 2, 0, -2, 0, \dots). For t = 0, \dots, 7: x = (3,\ 1.5,\ 0,\ 2.5,\ 5,\ 3.5,\ 2,\ 4.5).

Seasonal differencing gives, for t = 4, \dots, 7, \nabla_4 x_t = (2, 2, 2, 2) = 4 \times 0.5, a constant, and one more ordinary difference gives 0. On real data the remainder r_t survives these operations and is what the models of ch. 2 describe.

Robust alternatives estimate the trend by a centred moving average of length p and the season by averaging each phase, or use the loess-based STL procedure of Cleveland and coauthors (1990).

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Stationarity makes the autocovariance a function of the lag only; it is even, bounded by \gamma(0) and positive semi-definite.
  • The spectral density is the Fourier transform of the autocovariance and is non-negative.
  • The raw periodogram is asymptotically unbiased but not consistent; average it (Welch).
  • Spectrograms and wavelets localise frequencies in time; the Haar transform is orthonormal and costs \mathcal{O}(n).
  • Differencing removes polynomial trends and periodic seasons but amplifies high frequencies.

2. Linear models, state space and forecasting

To forecast a series, or to fill its gaps, one needs a model of how the present depends on the past. This chapter builds the linear one: autoregressions, their optimal predictors, the state-space form with the Kalman filter, and then forecasting as ordinary supervised learning with an honest evaluation.

Autoregressive and moving-average models

Definition 2.1 — AR, MA and ARMA models Let (\varepsilon_t) be a white noise of variance \sigma^2 and B the backshift operator, B x_t = x_{t-1}. With the polynomials \phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \dots - \phi_p z^p, \qquad \theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1 z + \dots + \theta_q z^q, an ARMA(p, q) process satisfies \phi(B) X_t = \theta(B) \varepsilon_t .

The case q = 0 is the autoregression AR(p), the case p = 0 the moving average MA(q).

The process is causal if X_t = \sum_{j \ge 0} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j} with \sum_j |\psi_j| < \infty: it depends on present and past shocks only. When \phi and \theta have no common zeros, this holds if and only if \phi(z) \ne 0 for |z| \le 1 (Brockwell and Davis, 1991). Its spectral density is then f(\omega) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi} \frac{|\theta(e^{-i\omega})|^2}{|\phi(e^{-i\omega})|^2}.

Proposition 2.2 — The AR(1) model The equation X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t has a stationary solution if and only if |\phi| \ne 1. For |\phi| < 1 the solution is causal and unique: X_t = \sum_{j \ge 0} \phi^j \varepsilon_{t-j}, \qquad \gamma(h) = \frac{\sigma^2 \phi^{|h|}}{1 - \phi^2}.

Proof. Uniqueness. Iterating the equation k times gives X_t = \phi^k X_{t-k} + \sum_{j < k} \phi^j \varepsilon_{t-j}. For a stationary solution, the second moment of \phi^k X_{t-k} is \phi^{2k}\, \mathbb{E}[X_0^2] \to 0, so X_t is the L^2 limit of the partial sums.

Existence and covariance. The series converges in L^2 since \sum_j \phi^{2j} < \infty. For h \ge 0, independence of the shocks gives \gamma(h) = \sigma^2 \sum_{j \ge 0} \phi^j \phi^{j+h} = \frac{\sigma^2 \phi^h}{1 - \phi^2}.

The case \phi = \pm 1. Then X_t - \phi^k X_{t-k} has variance k\sigma^2, while for a stationary process it is at most 4\gamma(0): impossible for large k. (For |\phi| > 1 a stationary but non-causal solution exists, written with future shocks.) ∎

Theorem 2.3 — Yule–Walker equations Let X be a causal AR(p). Then, for every h \ge 1,

Key result — Yule–Walker equations \gamma(h) = \sum_{j=1}^p \phi_j\, \gamma(h - j), \qquad \sigma^2 = \gamma(0) - \sum_{j=1}^p \phi_j \gamma(j).

In matrix form, \Gamma_p \boldsymbol\phi = \boldsymbol\gamma_p, with the Toeplitz matrix \Gamma_p = [\gamma(i - j)]_{i,j=1}^p and the vector \boldsymbol\gamma_p of the autocovariances at lags 1 to p.

Proof. Multiply X_t = \sum_j \phi_j X_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t by X_{t-h} and take expectations (centred process).

For h \ge 1, causality makes X_{t-h} a function of \varepsilon_s, s \le t - h, hence uncorrelated with \varepsilon_t: the last term vanishes.

For h = 0, \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t X_t] = \sigma^2, because X_t - \varepsilon_t depends only on past shocks. ∎

Replacing \gamma by the sample autocovariance (Definition 1.5) gives the Yule–Walker estimator \hat{\boldsymbol\phi} = \hat\Gamma_p^{-1} \hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_p .

The 1/n normalisation keeps \hat\Gamma_p positive definite, and the fitted model is always causal. The estimator is consistent and asymptotically Gaussian; least squares on the lagged values is asymptotically equivalent.

Theorem 2.4 — Optimal linear prediction of an AR(p) For a causal AR(p) and any k \ge p, the best linear predictor of X_{t+1} from X_t, \dots, X_{t-k+1} is \hat X_{t+1 \mid t} = \sum_{j=1}^p \phi_j X_{t+1-j}, with mean squared error \sigma^2. The h-step predictor follows the recursion \hat X_{t+h \mid t} = \sum_{j=1}^p \phi_j \hat X_{t+h-j \mid t}, \qquad \hat X_{s \mid t} = X_s \ \text{for } s \le t.

Proof. The error of the proposed predictor is \varepsilon_{t+1}, uncorrelated with every X_s, s \le t, by causality. By the orthogonality principle (Proposition 0.2), the predictor is optimal, and its error is \mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{t+1}) = \sigma^2.

For h > 1, project the model equation at time t + h on the past: projection is linear, and the projection of \varepsilon_{t+h} is zero. ∎

Example 2.5 — Forecasting an AR(1) Take \phi = 0.8, \sigma^2 = 1 and the last observation x_t = 2. The recursion gives \hat x_{t+h \mid t} = 0.8^h \times 2, and the error of the h-step forecast is \mathrm{MSE}_h = \sigma^2 \sum_{j < h} \phi^{2j} = \sigma^2 \frac{1 - \phi^{2h}}{1 - \phi^2}.

Horizon h 1 2 5 \infty
Forecast 1.6 1.28 0.66 0
MSE 1 1.64 2.48 2.78

The forecast decays to the mean and its error grows to the variance \gamma(0) = 1/0.36: beyond a few correlation times, the model knows nothing more than the marginal law. A Gaussian 95 % interval at h = 1 is 1.6 \pm 1.96.

Note 2.6 — ARIMA, seasonal models, exponential smoothing An ARIMA(p, d, q) model says that \nabla^d X is a causal ARMA(p, q): differencing (Proposition 1.13) handles polynomial trends. Seasonal ARIMA adds \nabla_s and polynomials in B^s. The book of Bisgaard and Kulahci (2011), in the official references, works through such models on real data.

Simple exponential smoothing forecasts with the level \ell_t = \alpha x_t + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{t-1}. It is the optimal predictor of an ARIMA(0, 1, 1) model, and the Holt–Winters variants add a trend and a season. Orders are chosen by an information criterion (AIC) on the Gaussian likelihood, which the Kalman filter below computes.

State-space models and the Kalman filter

The state-space form writes every linear model — AR, ARMA, trend plus season, several sensors — as a hidden Markov chain observed with noise. One algorithm then forecasts, filters, smooths and handles missing values (ch. 6).

Definition 2.7 — Linear Gaussian state-space model A hidden state s_t \in \mathbb{R}^m and an observation y_t \in \mathbb{R}^d follow s_{t+1} = F s_t + w_t, \qquad y_t = H s_t + v_t, with w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q), v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R) and s_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(m_0, P_0), all independent.

An AR(p) is the case s_t = (x_t, \dots, x_{t-p+1}), with the companion matrix F (first row \phi_1, \dots, \phi_p, ones below the diagonal), H = (1, 0, \dots, 0) and R = 0.

The local level model s_{t+1} = s_t + w_t, y_t = s_t + v_t is a random walk seen through noise.

Theorem 2.8 — Kalman filter Let m_{t \mid t-1}, P_{t \mid t-1} be the mean and covariance of s_t given y_{1:t-1}, and m_{t \mid t}, P_{t \mid t} those given y_{1:t}. The conditional laws are Gaussian, and:

  • prediction: m_{t \mid t-1} = F m_{t-1 \mid t-1} and P_{t \mid t-1} = F P_{t-1 \mid t-1} F^\top + Q;

  • update: with the innovation covariance S_t = H P_{t \mid t-1} H^\top + R and the gain K_t = P_{t \mid t-1} H^\top S_t^{-1},

    m_{t \mid t} = m_{t \mid t-1} + K_t\,(y_t - H m_{t \mid t-1}), \qquad P_{t \mid t} = (I - K_t H)\, P_{t \mid t-1}.

Proof. By induction on t. Given y_{1:t-1}, s_{t-1} is Gaussian with the filtered moments, and s_t = F s_{t-1} + w_{t-1} with w_{t-1} independent of everything before: this gives the prediction step.

Still given y_{1:t-1}, the pair (s_t, y_t) is Gaussian with \mathrm{Cov}(s_t, y_t) = P_{t \mid t-1} H^\top and \mathrm{Var}(y_t) = S_t, because v_t is independent. Proposition 0.2 applied to this pair is exactly the update step. ∎

The innovations y_t - H m_{t \mid t-1} are independent \mathcal{N}(0, S_t) variables, so the likelihood of the whole series is a product of n Gaussian densities; maximising it fits F, Q and R. The Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother adds a backward pass that conditions on the whole series: m_{t \mid n} = m_{t \mid t} + J_t\,(m_{t+1 \mid n} - m_{t+1 \mid t}), \qquad J_t = P_{t \mid t} F^\top P_{t+1 \mid t}^{-1}.

It follows from Proposition 0.2 applied to (s_t, s_{t+1}) given y_{1:t}, and from the Markov property: given s_{t+1}, the state s_t is independent of y_{t+1:n}. Chapter 6 uses it to impute gaps.

Forecasting as supervised learning

Definition 2.9 — Lag embedding, recursive and direct strategies Fix a number of lags L. The lag embedding turns a series into a regression data set with inputs z_t = (x_{t-1}, \dots, x_{t-L}) and targets x_t. Calendar variables and exogenous series can be appended to z_t.

Any regressor (ridge, gradient boosting, a neural network) then forecasts one step ahead. For a horizon h, the recursive strategy feeds its own forecasts back as inputs. The direct strategy trains one model per horizon on the targets x_{t+h-1}. The recursive strategy accumulates its errors; the direct one ignores the dependence between horizons and needs h models.

Definition 2.10 — Rolling-origin evaluation and forecast metrics Choose forecast origins T_1 < T_2 < \dots < T_K. For each k, fit the model on x_{1:T_k} only and score its forecasts of x_{T_k + 1 : T_k + H}. The average score over origins is the rolling-origin (or time-series cross-validation) estimate.

Usual scores are the MAE, the RMSE and the scaled error MASE: the MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of the naive forecast \hat x_{t+1} = x_t (Hyndman and Koehler, 2006). MASE below 1 beats the naive forecast. The percentage error MAPE explodes near zero values and should be avoided there.

Note 2.11 — Why a shuffled split is optimistic With a random split, a test point has its neighbours x_{t-1} and x_{t+1} in the training set; when residuals are correlated, they carry part of its value, and the score is optimistic. Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo (2018) show that ordinary K-fold cross-validation remains valid for purely autoregressive models with uncorrelated errors. Outside that case, use a rolling origin.

Always report the naive and the seasonal naive forecasts (\hat x_{t+h} = x_{t+h-s}) as baselines.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import toeplitz, solve
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 2000
x = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(2, n):                       # AR(2) with phi = (0.6, -0.3)
    x[t] = 0.6 * x[t - 1] - 0.3 * x[t - 2] + rng.standard_normal()

def yule_walker(x, p):                      # Theorem 2.3 with sample autocovariances
    xc = x - x.mean()
    g = np.array([xc[: len(x) - h] @ xc[h:] for h in range(p + 1)]) / len(x)
    phi = solve(toeplitz(g[:p]), g[1:])
    return phi, g[0] - phi @ g[1:]

phi_hat, s2_hat = yule_walker(x[:1000], 2)
print("Yule-Walker on the first 1000 points:", phi_hat.round(3), round(s2_hat, 3))

L = 5                                       # lag embedding: row t holds x_{t-1}, ..., x_{t-5}
X = np.column_stack([x[L - k - 1 : n - k - 1] for k in range(L)])
y = x[L:]
mse = {"naive (last value)": [], "AR(2) Yule-Walker": [], "ridge on 5 lags": []}
for origin in range(1000, len(y), 100):     # rolling origin (Definition 2.10)
    te = slice(origin, origin + 100)
    phi, _ = yule_walker(x[: origin + L], 2)            # refit on the past only
    ridge = Ridge(alpha=1.0).fit(X[:origin], y[:origin])
    mse["naive (last value)"].append(np.mean((y[te] - X[te, 0]) ** 2))
    mse["AR(2) Yule-Walker"].append(np.mean((y[te] - X[te, :2] @ phi) ** 2))
    mse["ridge on 5 lags"].append(np.mean((y[te] - ridge.predict(X[te])) ** 2))
for name, v in mse.items():
    print(f"{name:20s} one-step MSE = {np.mean(v):.3f}")

The estimate is \hat{\boldsymbol\phi} = (0.578, -0.297) for the true (0.6, -0.3). The one-step errors are 1.567 for the naive forecast, 1.055 for the AR(2) model and 1.060 for the ridge regression on 5 lags.

Both models come close to the floor \sigma^2 = 1 of Theorem 2.4. The remaining gap of about 0.05 is within the sampling error of an average over about 1000 test points, whose standard deviation is close to \sqrt{2/1000} \approx 0.045. On a linear process, a flexible regressor cannot beat the right linear model; it can only match it.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • A causal AR(p) has autocovariances given by the Yule–Walker equations, and its optimal one-step predictor is the model equation itself, with error \sigma^2.
  • Forecast errors grow with the horizon up to the variance of the series.
  • The Kalman filter is Gaussian conditioning applied recursively; the RTS smoother conditions on the whole series.
  • Forecasting is supervised learning on lag embeddings, evaluated with a rolling origin against naive baselines.

3. Extracting and recognising patterns

Two heartbeats, two steps of a walker or two spoken words are “the same pattern” even when one is shifted, slower or locally stretched. This chapter builds distances that tolerate such deformations, uses them to classify series, and then searches long series for repeated patterns (motifs) and for short discriminative ones (shapelets).

Comparing two series

Definition 3.1 — z-normalisation and lock-step distances The z-normalisation of x \in \mathbb{R}^n is \hat x = (x - \bar x \mathbf{1}) / s_x, where \bar x is the mean and s_x the standard deviation of its entries. It removes offset and amplitude, which are rarely what defines a pattern.

The Euclidean distance \|x - y\| is a lock-step distance: it compares x_t with y_t only. A pattern shifted by a few samples, or played slightly faster, can be far from itself in this distance.

Definition 3.2 — Warping path and dynamic time warping Let x \in \mathbb{R}^n and y \in \mathbb{R}^m. A warping path is a sequence of index pairs (i_1, j_1), \dots, (i_K, j_K) such that:

  • it starts at (1, 1) and ends at (n, m);
  • each step (i_{k+1} - i_k,\ j_{k+1} - j_k) is (1, 0), (0, 1) or (1, 1).

The DTW distance is the cost of the best path: \mathrm{DTW}(x, y) = \min_{\text{paths}} \Big( \sum_{k=1}^{K} (x_{i_k} - y_{j_k})^2 \Big)^{1/2}.

The Sakoe–Chiba band of half-width w restricts paths to |i_k - j_k| \le w; it forbids pathological alignments and speeds up the computation.

Theorem 3.3 — Dynamic programming for DTW Let D(0, 0) = 0, D(i, 0) = D(0, j) = +\infty for i, j \ge 1, and

Key result — the DTW recursion D(i, j) = (x_i - y_j)^2 + \min\big\{ D(i-1, j),\ D(i, j-1),\ D(i-1, j-1) \big\}.

Then D(i, j) is the smallest cost of a warping path from (1, 1) to (i, j), and \mathrm{DTW}(x, y)^2 = D(n, m), computed in \mathcal{O}(nm) time, or \mathcal{O}(nw) with a band.

Proof. By induction on i + j. A path ending at a pair (i, j) \neq (1, 1) has its last-but-one pair among the three predecessors (i-1, j), (i, j-1) and (i-1, j-1). Its cost is the cost of its prefix plus (x_i - y_j)^2.

The prefix is a path to that predecessor, so its cost is at least D of the predecessor; and the optimal path to the best predecessor, extended by one step, attains the bound. Each of the nm cells is filled in constant time. ∎

Example 3.4 — A shifted bump Let x = (0, 0, 1, 2, 1, 0) and y = (0, 1, 2, 1, 0, 0), the same bump one sample earlier. The Euclidean distance is \|x - y\| = \sqrt{0 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0} = 2.

The path (1,1), (2,1), (3,2), (4,3), (5,4), (6,5), (6,6) matches equal values everywhere, so \mathrm{DTW}(x, y) = 0 .

Proposition 3.5 — DTW is not a metric DTW is symmetric and non-negative, but \mathrm{DTW}(x, y) = 0 does not imply x = y, and the triangle inequality fails.

Proof. Zero distance. (0, 1) and (0, 1, 1) are at DTW distance 0: the last value of the first series is matched twice.

Triangle inequality. Take x = (0), y = (1), z = (1, 1). The only path from x to z matches 0 with both ones, so \mathrm{DTW}(x, z) = \sqrt 2 \ >\ \mathrm{DTW}(x, y) + \mathrm{DTW}(y, z) = 1 + 0.

∎

Metric indexing structures therefore do not apply directly; fast search relies on lower bounds instead.

Proposition 3.6 — The LB_Keogh lower bound Let x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n and a band of half-width w. Define the envelope of y by U_i = \max_{|k - i| \le w} y_k, \qquad L_i = \min_{|k - i| \le w} y_k, and let \mathrm{LB}(x, y)^2 be the sum over i of the squared distance from x_i to the interval [L_i, U_i]. Then \mathrm{LB}(x, y) \ \le\ \mathrm{DTW}_w(x, y), and \mathrm{LB} costs \mathcal{O}(n).

Proof. Every index i appears in at least one pair (i, j) of the optimal banded path, with |i - j| \le w, hence y_j \in [L_i, U_i]. The cost of that pair is at least the squared distance from x_i to the interval.

Choosing one such pair for each i selects distinct pairs of the path, so the sum of these lower bounds is at most the total cost. Running minima and maxima give the envelope in linear time. ∎

In a nearest-neighbour search, a candidate whose lower bound exceeds the best distance found so far is discarded without computing DTW. The bound is presented in Keogh and Ratanamahatana (2005); cascades of such bounds let DTW search scale to trillions of subsequences (Rakthanmanon and coauthors, 2012).

Note 3.7 — Averaging and differentiating DTW Clustering with k-means needs an average under DTW. DTW barycentre averaging (Petitjean and coauthors, 2011) alternates between aligning every series to the current average and averaging the aligned values.

Soft-DTW (Cuturi and Blondel, 2017) replaces the \min of Theorem 3.3 by the smooth minimum -\gamma \log \sum_i e^{-a_i / \gamma}. The result is differentiable, its gradient is computed by a backward recursion of the same cost, and it can serve as a training loss.

Nearest-neighbour classification

Theorem 3.8 — Cover and Hart (1967) Let the training pairs be i.i.d. in a separable metric space, with K classes, and let R^* be the Bayes error. Under mild regularity of the class-conditional laws, the error R of the 1-nearest-neighbour rule tends, as the sample grows, to a limit that satisfies R^* \ \le\ R \ \le\ R^*\Big(2 - \frac{K}{K-1} R^*\Big) \ \le\ 2R^*.

Proof. Out of scope; see Cover and Hart (1967). The key step is that the nearest neighbour converges to the query point, so its label is an independent draw from the same conditional law. ∎

DTW is not a metric, so the theorem is only a guide. Empirically, 1-NN with a DTW whose band is tuned by cross-validation was long the baseline to beat on the UCR archive of time-series classification data sets (Dau and coauthors, 2019). The large comparison of Bagnall and coauthors (2017) found that many published methods were not significantly more accurate than this baseline.

The Cylinder–Bell–Funnel data set (Saito, 1994) is synthetic: a plateau, a ramp up or a ramp down, of random start, length and height, in Gaussian noise. The code generates it and compares the two distances.

import numpy as np

def cbf(n_per_class, rng, length=128):      # Cylinder-Bell-Funnel (Saito, 1994)
    t, X, y = np.arange(length), [], []
    for label in range(3):
        for _ in range(n_per_class):
            a = rng.integers(16, 33)
            b = a + rng.integers(32, 97)
            chi = ((t >= a) & (t <= b)).astype(float)
            shape = [chi, chi * (t - a) / (b - a), chi * (b - t) / (b - a)][label]
            X.append((6 + rng.standard_normal()) * shape + rng.standard_normal(length))
            y.append(label)
    return np.array(X), np.array(y)

def dtw_to_all(q, C, w):
    """DTW between q and every row of C (squared costs, band half-width w)."""
    n, N = len(q), len(C)
    D = np.full((n + 1, n + 1, N), np.inf)
    D[0, 0] = 0.0
    for i in range(1, n + 1):                # recursion of Theorem 3.3
        for j in range(max(1, i - w), min(n, i + w) + 1):
            best = np.minimum(np.minimum(D[i - 1, j], D[i, j - 1]), D[i - 1, j - 1])
            D[i, j] = (q[i - 1] - C[:, j - 1]) ** 2 + best
    return np.sqrt(D[n, n])

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
Xtr, ytr = cbf(10, rng)                      # 30 training series
Xte, yte = cbf(30, rng)                      # 90 test series
z = lambda X: (X - X.mean(1, keepdims=True)) / X.std(1, keepdims=True)
Xtr, Xte = z(Xtr), z(Xte)

d_euc = ((Xte[:, None, :] - Xtr[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
d_dtw = np.array([dtw_to_all(q, Xtr, w=12) for q in Xte])
for name, d in [("Euclidean", d_euc), ("DTW, 10% band", d_dtw)]:
    print(f"1-NN {name:14s} error = {np.mean(ytr[d.argmin(1)] != yte):.3f}")

With 30 training series, the Euclidean 1-NN errs on 13.3 % of the test series and the DTW 1-NN on none: \text{error}_{\text{Euclid}} = 0.133, \qquad \text{error}_{\text{DTW}} = 0.000 .

Motifs and discords: the matrix profile

In a single long series, one asks which pattern repeats (a motif) and which stretch resembles nothing else (a discord). The matrix profile answers both questions at once.

Definition 3.9 — Matrix profile, motif, discord Let T be a series of length n and T_{i,m} = (T_i, \dots, T_{i+m-1}) its subsequences of length m.

Let d_{i,j} be the Euclidean distance between the z-normalised subsequences T_{i,m} and T_{j,m}.

The matrix profile is P_i = \min_{j\,:\,|i - j| \ge m/2} d_{i,j}, the distance from each subsequence to its nearest non-trivial match. The exclusion zone |i - j| < m/2 discards a subsequence’s overlapping copies of itself.

The pair (i, j) that attains \min_i P_i is the top motif. The index \arg\max_i P_i is the top discord, a natural anomaly candidate (ch. 5).

Proposition 3.10 — Distance and correlation For subsequences a, b \in \mathbb{R}^m with Pearson correlation \rho(a, b), z-normalised with the population standard deviation, \|\hat a - \hat b\|^2 = 2m\,\big(1 - \rho(a, b)\big).

Proof. A z-normalised vector has mean 0 and \|\hat a\|^2 = m. Hence \|\hat a - \hat b\|^2 = 2m - 2\langle \hat a, \hat b \rangle, and \langle \hat a, \hat b \rangle = m\,\rho(a, b) by definition of the correlation. ∎

All distances therefore follow from the sliding dot products \langle T_{i,m}, T_{j,m} \rangle and from sliding means and standard deviations (cumulative sums). For a fixed i, the dot products with all j form one cross-correlation, computed by FFT (Theorem 0.5). Along a diagonal they update in constant time: \langle T_{i+1,m}, T_{j+1,m} \rangle = \langle T_{i,m}, T_{j,m} \rangle - T_i T_j + T_{i+m} T_{j+m}.

This gives the exact matrix profile in \mathcal{O}(n^2) time and \mathcal{O}(n) memory (Yeh and coauthors, 2016; Zhu and coauthors, 2016). The block below uses the \mathcal{O}(n^2)-memory version, which is enough for 2000 samples.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, m = 2000, 50
T = np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal(n)) * 0.1 + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal(n)
motif = 2 * np.sin(np.linspace(0, 3 * np.pi, m))
for pos in (300, 1400):                      # plant the same pattern twice
    T[pos : pos + m] += motif

S = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(T, m)        # all subsequences
Z = (S - S.mean(1, keepdims=True)) / S.std(1, keepdims=True)
corr = Z @ Z.T / m                                         # Pearson correlations
dist = np.sqrt(np.maximum(2 * m * (1 - corr), 0))          # Proposition 3.10
idx = np.arange(len(Z))
dist[np.abs(idx[:, None] - idx[None, :]) < m // 2] = np.inf   # exclusion zone
P = dist.min(1)                                            # the matrix profile
i = int(np.argmin(P))
j = int(np.argmin(dist[i]))
print("motif pair (start indices):", sorted((i, j)), " distance:", round(P[i], 2))
print("discord (most isolated subsequence) starts at:", int(np.argmax(P)))

The top motif is the pair of subsequences starting at 302 and 1402, two samples after the planted copies (the z-normalised pattern matches best with a small offset). The discord, at index 63, is simply the most unusual stretch of the random walk: on a series without a planted anomaly, the top discord is not an anomaly.

Shapelets

A class can be defined by a short pattern that appears somewhere in the series. Shapelets are such patterns, cut out of the training data.

Definition 3.11 — Shapelet and shapelet distance A shapelet is a short series s \in \mathbb{R}^L. Its distance to a series x \in \mathbb{R}^n, n \ge L, is the distance to the best-matching window: \mathrm{sd}(s, x) = \min_{1 \le t \le n - L + 1} \frac1L \sum_{k=1}^{L} (x_{t+k-1} - s_k)^2 .

A small \mathrm{sd}(s, x) means that s occurs in x, wherever it is: the distance is invariant to the position of the pattern.

Definition 3.12 — Shapelet transform (Hills and coauthors, 2014) Given labelled training series:

  1. take as candidates the subsequences of the training series, for a range of lengths;
  2. score each candidate by how well its distances \mathrm{sd}(s, x^{(i)}) separate the classes — information gain, or an F-statistic;
  3. keep the k best candidates, discarding those that overlap a better one;
  4. map every series x to the vector of its k shapelet distances, \mathrm{sd}(s_j, x) for j = 1, \dots, k, and train any classifier on these vectors.

With N series of length n, there are about N n^2 / 2 candidates, each compared with N series at a cost of order n^2. Exhaustive search is thus of order N^2 n^4 operations, which is why practical versions subsample the candidates or learn the shapelets by gradient descent (Grabocka and coauthors, 2014). The original shapelets of Ye and Keogh (2009) were used inside a decision tree.

The reward is interpretability: a shapelet is a piece of real data that one can plot and show to a domain expert.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
tri = np.r_[np.linspace(0, 2, 10), np.linspace(2, 0, 10)]   # class-1 pattern
sq = np.r_[np.zeros(3), 2 * np.ones(14), np.zeros(3)]      # class-0 pattern

def make(n, length=100):                    # pattern at a random position + noise
    X, y = 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((n, length)), rng.integers(0, 2, n)
    for k in range(n):
        p = rng.integers(0, length - 20)
        X[k, p : p + 20] += tri if y[k] else sq
    return X, y

def sdist(s, X):                            # shapelet distance (Definition 3.11)
    W = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(X, len(s), axis=1)
    return ((W - s) ** 2).mean(-1).min(-1)

def quality(d, y):                          # separation of the two classes
    a, b = d[y == 0], d[y == 1]
    return (a.mean() - b.mean()) ** 2 / (a.var() + b.var())

Xtr, ytr = make(40)
Xte, yte = make(300)
cands = [x[p : p + 20] for x in Xtr[:10] for p in range(0, 81, 2)]
scores = [quality(sdist(s, Xtr), ytr) for s in cands]
best = [cands[i] for i in np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:5]]   # keep the 5 best
F = lambda X: np.column_stack([sdist(s, X) for s in best]) # shapelet transform
clf = LogisticRegression().fit(F(Xtr), ytr)
print("shapelet transform + logistic regression, accuracy:", round(clf.score(F(Xte), yte), 3))
d = ((Xte[:, None, :] - Xtr[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
print("Euclidean 1-NN on the raw series, accuracy:      ", round(np.mean(ytr[d.argmin(1)] == yte), 3))

The patterns sit at random positions, so the lock-step 1-NN reaches only 0.67, while five shapelets learned from 40 series give \text{accuracy} = 0.917 .

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • DTW aligns two series by the cheapest monotone warping path, computed by dynamic programming in \mathcal{O}(nm), or \mathcal{O}(nw) within a band.
  • DTW is not a metric; lower bounds such as LB_Keogh make nearest-neighbour search fast.
  • 1-NN with DTW is a strong classification baseline.
  • The matrix profile finds motifs and discords from z-normalised distances, which reduce to correlations.
  • Shapelets classify by the presence of a local pattern anywhere in the series, and remain interpretable.

4. Sparse representations and dictionary learning

A recurring pattern — a heartbeat, a step, a spike — can be seen as an atom that the signal uses a few times. This chapter represents signals as sparse combinations of atoms, learns the atoms from the data, and then makes the representation shift-invariant with convolutions, which turns it into a pattern-extraction tool.

Sparse coding

Definition 4.1 — Dictionary and sparse coding A dictionary is a matrix D = [d_1, \dots, d_K] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K} whose columns, the atoms, have unit norm; often K > n (redundant). The number of nonzero entries of z is written \|z\|_0.

The sparse coding of y \in \mathbb{R}^n seeks a code z \in \mathbb{R}^K with few nonzeros such that y \approx Dz, for instance \min_{z} \|y - Dz\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|z\|_0 \le s.

This problem is NP-hard in general (Natarajan, 1995). Two families of methods approximate it: greedy pursuits and convex relaxation by the \ell_1 norm.

Algorithm 4.2 — Orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) Start from the residual r = y and the empty support S. Repeat s times:

  1. select the atom most correlated with the residual, k^* = \arg\max_k |\langle d_k, r \rangle|, and add it to S;
  2. recompute the coefficients on S by least squares, z_S = \arg\min_u \|y - D_S u\|^2 (Proposition 0.3);
  3. update the residual r = y - D_S z_S, which is orthogonal to every selected atom.

Each iteration costs one product D^\top r and one small least-squares problem (Pati and coauthors, 1993).

Definition 4.3 — Mutual coherence and spark The mutual coherence of a dictionary with unit-norm atoms is the largest correlation between two distinct atoms: \mu(D) = \max_{k \ne l} |\langle d_k, d_l \rangle| .

The spark of D is the smallest number of columns of D that are linearly dependent.

Theorem 4.4 — Uniqueness and recovery under low coherence Let y = Dx with \|x\|_0 < \frac12 \Big( 1 + \frac{1}{\mu(D)} \Big).

Then x is the unique sparsest representation of y. Moreover, two algorithms recover x exactly (Donoho and Elad, 2003; Tropp, 2004):

  • OMP run for \|x\|_0 steps;
  • the \ell_1 problem \min \|z\|_1 subject to Dz = y.

Proof. Spark bound. Take any k columns; their Gram matrix has unit diagonal and off-diagonal entries of modulus at most \mu. By Gershgorin’s theorem its eigenvalues are at least 1 - (k-1)\mu, positive when k < 1 + 1/\mu. Such columns are independent, so \mathrm{spark}(D) \ge 1 + 1/\mu.

Uniqueness. If Dx' = y with \|x'\|_0 \le \|x\|_0, then D(x - x') = 0 with \|x - x'\|_0 \le 2\|x\|_0 < 1 + 1/\mu \le \mathrm{spark}(D), so the columns used by x - x' are independent and x = x'.

Recovery. The recovery of OMP and \ell_1 minimisation is proved in the cited papers; the key step for OMP is that, under the condition, the atom most correlated with the residual always belongs to the true support. ∎

For the union of the identity and the orthonormal Fourier basis in dimension n, \mu = 1/\sqrt n. With n = 64 the theorem guarantees recovery of any combination of at most 4 spikes and sinusoids (Exercise 10).

Proposition 4.5 — Soft thresholding, ISTA and FISTA The proximal operator of \tau \|\cdot\|_1 is the soft thresholding

Key result — soft thresholding S_\tau(u)_k = \mathrm{sign}(u_k)\, \max(|u_k| - \tau,\, 0).

For the lasso F(z) = \frac12 \|y - Dz\|^2 + \lambda \|z\|_1 and a step 1/L with L \ge \|D\|_2^2, the ISTA iteration is z^{k+1} = S_{\lambda / L}\Big( z^k - \tfrac1L D^\top (D z^k - y) \Big).

It satisfies F(z^k) - F^* \le L \|z^0 - z^*\|^2 / (2k). Its accelerated version FISTA, which adds a momentum step, reaches 2L \|z^0 - z^*\|^2 / (k+1)^2 (Beck and Teboulle, 2009).

Proof. Proximal operator. The problem \min_v \frac12 \|v - u\|^2 + \tau \|v\|_1 separates over coordinates.

For one coordinate, a minimiser v > 0 satisfies v - u + \tau = 0, so v = u - \tau, which is valid when u > \tau. Symmetrically, v = u + \tau when u < -\tau.

Otherwise 0 is optimal, since |u| \le \tau means that 0 \in -u + \tau\,\partial|\cdot|(0).

Iteration and rates. ISTA is the proximal gradient method on F: a gradient step on the smooth part, whose gradient is L-Lipschitz, then the proximal operator of the \ell_1 part. The rates are those of the proximal gradient method and of its accelerated version; see Beck and Teboulle (2009) and Convex Optimization. ∎

Dictionary learning

Fixed bases (Fourier, wavelets) are universal but not adapted to a given signal. Dictionary learning fits the atoms to the data (Tošić and Frossard, 2011, in the official references).

Definition 4.6 — Dictionary learning Given training signals Y = [y_1, \dots, y_N] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times N} — for instance windows cut from long series — solve \min_{D,\ Z} \ \frac12 \|Y - DZ\|_F^2 + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^N \|z_i\|_1 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|d_k\| \le 1 .

The norm constraint removes the scale ambiguity (D, Z) \mapsto (\alpha D, Z / \alpha), which would otherwise drive Z to zero. The problem is not jointly convex, but it is convex in Z for fixed D and in D for fixed Z: one alternates between sparse coding and dictionary update.

Proposition 4.7 — Two dictionary updates Fix the codes Z.

  • MOD (Engan and coauthors, 1999): if ZZ^\top is invertible, the unconstrained minimiser of \|Y - DZ\|_F^2 is

    D = Y Z^\top (Z Z^\top)^{-1},

    whose columns are then renormalised.

  • K-SVD (Aharon and coauthors, 2006): updating only atom k and the row z^k of its coefficients, with the error E_k = Y - \sum_{j \ne k} d_j z^j, the best choice is the leading singular pair of E_k:

    d_k = u_1, \qquad z^k = \sigma_1 v_1^\top .

Proof. MOD. The objective separates over the rows of Y and D: each row of D solves a least-squares problem whose normal equations (Proposition 0.3) read D\, ZZ^\top = YZ^\top.

K-SVD. The objective restricted to atom k is \|E_k - d_k z^k\|_F^2, the error of a rank-one approximation of E_k. By the Eckart–Young theorem, the best rank-one approximation is \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top, and u_1 has unit norm as required.

In practice E_k is restricted to the signals that already use atom k, which keeps the codes sparse. ∎

For millions of windows, the online algorithm of Mairal and coauthors (2010) updates the dictionary from mini-batches.

Convolutional sparse coding

Cutting a long series into windows creates an artefact: a pattern that straddles two windows is coded twice, at arbitrary shifts. The convolutional model codes the whole series at once, with atoms that may appear at any time.

Definition 4.8 — Convolutional sparse coding (CSC) Let d_1, \dots, d_K \in \mathbb{R}^L be short atoms, L \ll n. A series x \in \mathbb{R}^n is modelled as x \approx \sum_{k=1}^K d_k * z_k, where the activation signals z_k \in \mathbb{R}^n are sparse: a spike of height a at time \tau in z_k places a copy of a\, d_k starting at \tau. The coding problem is \min_{z_1, \dots, z_K} \ \frac12 \Big\| x - \sum_k d_k * z_k \Big\|^2 + \lambda \sum_k \|z_k\|_1 .

It is a lasso whose dictionary contains every shift of every atom: Kn columns, highly coherent, but with a convolution structure that makes it tractable. Shifting x shifts the activations: the representation is shift-equivariant.

Proposition 4.9 — CSC in the Fourier domain With circular convolutions of length n, let \mathcal{D}(z) = \sum_k d_k \circledast z_k and let \hat d_k(\omega) be the DFT of the zero-padded atom d_k.

  • \mathcal{D} acts frequency by frequency:

    \widehat{\mathcal{D}(z)}(\omega) = \sum_k \hat d_k(\omega)\, \hat z_k(\omega).

  • Its squared operator norm, the Lipschitz constant needed by ISTA and FISTA, is

    \|\mathcal{D}\|^2 = \max_\omega \sum_{k=1}^K |\hat d_k(\omega)|^2 .

  • The linear systems (\mathcal{D}^* \mathcal{D} + \rho I) z = b of ADMM split into n systems of size K, each with an identity-plus-rank-one matrix, solved in \mathcal{O}(K) by the Sherman–Morrison formula (Wohlberg, 2015, in the official references).

Proof. Diagonalisation. Theorem 0.5 applied to each term.

Norm. By Parseval and Cauchy–Schwarz at each frequency, \|\mathcal{D}(z)\|^2 = \frac1n \sum_\omega \Big| \sum_k \hat d_k \hat z_k \Big|^2 \le \max_\omega \Big(\sum_k |\hat d_k(\omega)|^2\Big) \sum_k \|z_k\|^2 .

Equality holds when every \hat z_k is supported at the maximising frequency (and its mirror frequency, for real signals) and proportional to \overline{\hat d_k} there.

Systems. At frequency \omega, with a = \overline{\hat d(\omega)} \in \mathbb{C}^K, the matrix is \rho I + a a^{*}, and (\rho I + a a^*)^{-1} = \frac1\rho \Big( I - \frac{a a^*}{\rho + a^* a} \Big).

∎

One FISTA or ADMM iteration therefore costs a few FFTs, \mathcal{O}(K n \log n), instead of \mathcal{O}(K n L) for direct convolutions. The block below codes a synthetic signal built from two atoms with FISTA.

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import find_peaks

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n, L, K = 1024, 32, 2
t = np.linspace(-1, 1, L)
atoms = np.stack([np.exp(-8 * t**2), np.sin(3 * np.pi * t) * np.exp(-3 * t**2)])
atoms /= np.linalg.norm(atoms, axis=1, keepdims=True)      # unit-norm atoms
Z = np.zeros((K, n))
for k in range(K):                                         # 5 activations per atom
    Z[k, rng.choice(n - L, 5, replace=False)] = rng.uniform(1, 3, 5)

Dh = np.fft.rfft(atoms, n)                                 # atoms in the Fourier domain
synth = lambda Z: np.fft.irfft((Dh * np.fft.rfft(Z, n)).sum(0), n)   # sum_k d_k * z_k
adj = lambda r: np.fft.irfft(np.conj(Dh) * np.fft.rfft(r, n), n)     # its adjoint
x = synth(Z) + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal(n)

lam, Lip = 0.2, (np.abs(Dh) ** 2).sum(0).max()            # Lipschitz constant (Prop. 4.9)
z = v = np.zeros((K, n))
tk = 1.0
for it in range(300):                                      # FISTA iterations
    u = v - adj(synth(v) - x) / Lip                        # gradient step
    z_new = np.sign(u) * np.maximum(np.abs(u) - lam / Lip, 0)   # soft threshold
    t_new = (1 + np.sqrt(1 + 4 * tk**2)) / 2
    v = z_new + (tk - 1) / t_new * (z_new - z)
    z, tk = z_new, t_new
for k in range(K):
    found, _ = find_peaks(z[k], height=0.5)
    print(f"atom {k}: true {np.flatnonzero(Z[k])}, found {found}")
print("relative residual:", round(np.linalg.norm(x - synth(z)) / np.linalg.norm(x), 3))

The ten activation times are recovered exactly, for both atoms. The relative residual of 0.23 reflects the shrinkage of the amplitudes by the \ell_1 penalty; refitting the amplitudes by least squares on the detected support removes this bias.

Note 4.10 — Convolutional dictionary learning in practice Learning the atoms alternates CSC with an update of the atoms under the constraint \|d_k\| \le 1, by projected gradient or ADMM. Initialising the atoms with random windows of the signal helps.

The learned atoms are prototypes of the recurring patterns, and the activations say when they occur. Pattern extraction and recognition (ch. 3) are thus solved together. For multichannel recordings such as MEG, Dupré la Tour and coauthors (2018) constrain each atom to be rank one across channels — a spatial pattern times a temporal waveform — which makes the atoms physically interpretable.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Sparse coding is NP-hard in general, but OMP and \ell_1 minimisation recover sparse codes exactly when the dictionary has low coherence.
  • The proximal operator of the \ell_1 norm is soft thresholding; ISTA converges at rate 1/k and FISTA at rate 1/k^2.
  • Dictionary learning alternates sparse coding with MOD or K-SVD updates.
  • Convolutional sparse coding models a series as a few shifted atoms; the Fourier domain makes it cost \mathcal{O}(Kn\log n) per iteration.

5. Detecting changes and anomalies

A gait recording switches from walking to running, a machine drifts before failing, a sensor freezes for an hour. The first two are changes: the law of the series is different after some times. The last one is an anomaly: a value or a stretch that does not conform to normal behaviour. This chapter treats change-point detection as an optimisation problem, following the official review of Truong, Oudre and Vayatis (2020), and then the detection of anomalies.

Change-point detection as penalised optimisation

Definition 5.1 — Segmentation and penalised criterion Write y_{a..b} = (y_{a+1}, \dots, y_b) for a segment of a series y_1, \dots, y_n. A segmentation with K change points is a set \mathcal{T} = \{t_1 < \dots < t_K\}, with the conventions t_0 = 0 and t_{K+1} = n.

Given a cost function c that measures how homogeneous a segment is, the penalised criterion is V(\mathcal{T}) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} c\big(y_{t_k..t_{k+1}}\big) + \beta K .

Change-point detection minimises V. A method is the choice of three elements: the cost, the search method and the penalty \beta (or a known number K of changes).

Definition 5.2 — Three cost functions For a segment of length m = b - a with mean \bar y_{a..b}:

  • quadratic (changes in the mean):

    c_{L_2}(y_{a..b}) = \sum_{t=a+1}^{b} \|y_t - \bar y_{a..b}\|^2 ;

  • Gaussian (changes in mean and covariance): c(y_{a..b}) = m \log\det \hat\Sigma_{a..b}, with \hat\Sigma_{a..b} the empirical covariance of the segment;

  • kernel (changes in distribution), for a positive definite kernel k:

    c_k(y_{a..b}) = \sum_{t=a+1}^{b} k(y_t, y_t) - \frac1m \sum_{s,t=a+1}^{b} k(y_s, y_t).

The first two are, up to constants, minus twice the Gaussian log-likelihood of the segment. With cumulative sums of y_t and y_t^2, c_{L_2} of any segment costs \mathcal{O}(1).

Proposition 5.3 — One change in the mean For 0 < \tau < n, splitting y_{0..n} at \tau decreases the quadratic cost by c_{L_2}(y_{0..n}) - c_{L_2}(y_{0..\tau}) - c_{L_2}(y_{\tau..n}) = \frac{\tau (n - \tau)}{n} \big(\bar y_{0..\tau} - \bar y_{\tau..n}\big)^2 \ \ge\ 0 .

Divided by the noise variance \sigma^2, this is twice the log-likelihood ratio of “one change at \tau” against “no change” for Gaussian data. It is also the square of the standardised CUSUM statistic at \tau, so its maximum over \tau is the squared maximal CUSUM statistic of the classical offline test.

Proof. This is the analysis-of-variance identity: total sum of squares = within-segment sum + between-segment sum. With \bar y = (\tau \bar y_1 + (n - \tau)\bar y_2)/n, the between part is \tau(\bar y_1 - \bar y)^2 + (n - \tau)(\bar y_2 - \bar y)^2.

Now \bar y_1 - \bar y = \frac{n - \tau}{n}(\bar y_1 - \bar y_2) and \bar y_2 - \bar y = \frac{\tau}{n}(\bar y_2 - \bar y_1). The sum is therefore \frac{\tau (n-\tau)^2 + (n - \tau)\tau^2}{n^2} (\bar y_1 - \bar y_2)^2 = \frac{\tau(n - \tau)}{n} (\bar y_1 - \bar y_2)^2.

∎

Example 5.4 — Locating one change by hand Let y = (0.1, -0.2, 0.0, 2.1, 1.9, 2.0). The gains of Proposition 5.3 for \tau = 1, \dots, 5 are 0.94, \quad 3.20, \quad 6.20, \quad 2.80, \quad 1.24 .

The maximum is at \tau = 3, between the third and fourth samples. The split is kept if its gain exceeds the penalty \beta; with a noise level \sigma = 0.15 and the BIC-type penalty 2\sigma^2 \log n \approx 0.08 (justified in Note 5.9 below), it is kept by a wide margin.

Search methods

Proposition 5.5 — Optimal partitioning (Jackson and coauthors, 2005) Let F(0) = -\beta and, for t = 1, \dots, n,

Key result — optimal partitioning F(t) = \min_{0 \le s < t} \Big[ F(s) + c(y_{s..t}) + \beta \Big].

Then F(n) = \min_{\mathcal{T}} V(\mathcal{T}), and the optimal segmentation is recovered by storing the minimising s at each t and backtracking from n. The cost is \mathcal{O}(n^2) evaluations of c.

Proof. By induction, F(t) is the optimal penalised cost of y_{0..t}. Let s be the last change point of an optimal segmentation of y_{0..t}, or s = 0 if there is none.

The part of that segmentation before s must be optimal for y_{0..s}; otherwise, replacing it would lower the total.

The total is therefore F(s) + c(y_{s..t}) + \beta, where the convention F(0) = -\beta cancels the penalty of the first segment, and minimising over s gives F(t). ∎

Theorem 5.6 — PELT pruning (Killick, Fearnhead and Eckley, 2012) Assume there is a constant \kappa such that, for all a < t < b, c(y_{a..t}) + c(y_{t..b}) + \kappa \le c(y_{a..b}).

If, at some time t, a candidate s < t satisfies F(s) + c(y_{s..t}) + \kappa > F(t), then s is never the optimal last change point before any later time T > t, and can be removed from the candidate set.

Proof. For T > t, the assumption with (a, t, b) = (s, t, T) gives F(s) + c(y_{s..T}) + \beta \ \ge\ F(s) + c(y_{s..t}) + \kappa + c(y_{t..T}) + \beta \ >\ F(t) + c(y_{t..T}) + \beta .

The candidate t therefore does strictly better than s at time T, so s is never the minimiser. ∎

For c_{L_2} one can take \kappa = 0, since splitting never increases the cost (Proposition 5.3). The result is exact: PELT returns the same segmentation as optimal partitioning. When the number of changes grows linearly with n, its expected cost is \mathcal{O}(n) under mild conditions (Killick and coauthors, 2012).

Note 5.7 — Approximate searches Binary segmentation finds the best single split (Proposition 5.3 for c_{L_2}), then recurses on both sides while the gain exceeds \beta. It costs about \mathcal{O}(n \log n) but can miss a short segment squeezed between two large changes. Wild binary segmentation (Fryzlewicz, 2014) searches random sub-intervals to fix this.

Window-based methods slide two adjacent windows along the series and compare them. They are fast and easy to run online, but each change is judged locally. The official review of Truong, Oudre and Vayatis (2020) organises all these methods by cost, search and constraint.

Proposition 5.8 — The kernel cost in feature space Let \Phi be the feature map of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space \mathcal{H} of k, so that k(u, v) = \langle \Phi(u), \Phi(v) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}. For a segment of length m with mean embedding \bar\mu = \frac1m \sum_t \Phi(y_t), c_k(y_{a..b}) = \sum_{t=a+1}^{b} \big\| \Phi(y_t) - \bar\mu \big\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 .

Proof. Expand the square: \|\Phi(y_t) - \bar\mu\|^2 = k(y_t, y_t) - \frac2m \sum_s k(y_t, y_s) + \frac{1}{m^2}\sum_{s,s'} k(y_s, y_{s'}).

Summing over the m values of t, the last two terms combine into -\frac1m \sum_{s,t} k(y_s, y_t). ∎

The kernel cost is therefore the quadratic cost of the data mapped into \mathcal{H}. With a characteristic kernel, such as the Gaussian kernel, mean embeddings differ whenever distributions differ: the cost detects changes in variance or shape, not only in the mean (Harchaoui and Cappé, 2007; Arlot, Celisse and Harchaoui, 2019).

Note 5.9 — Choosing the penalty and scoring a segmentation For c_{L_2} and Gaussian noise of variance \sigma^2, each change adds two parameters (a location and a mean). A BIC-type penalty is therefore \beta = 2\sigma^2 \log n .

The noise level can be estimated robustly from the differences: \hat\sigma is the median absolute deviation of y_{t+1} - y_t divided by 0.6745\sqrt 2. The official review discusses other calibrations, including from annotated signals.

A segmentation is scored by the error on the number of changes, the Hausdorff distance between true and estimated change points, and a precision and recall in which an estimated change counts as correct within a margin of M samples of an unmatched true one.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
means, lengths = [0, 2, 0.5, 3, 1], [200, 150, 250, 100, 300]
y = np.concatenate([m + rng.standard_normal(l) for m, l in zip(means, lengths)])
n = len(y)
S1, S2 = np.r_[0, np.cumsum(y)], np.r_[0, np.cumsum(y**2)]
cost = lambda a, b: S2[b] - S2[a] - (S1[b] - S1[a]) ** 2 / (b - a)  # c_L2(y[a:b])

def pelt(beta, min_size=5, prune=True):
    F = np.full(n + 1, np.inf)
    F[0] = -beta
    last, R, evals = np.zeros(n + 1, int), [0], 0
    for t in range(min_size, n + 1):
        ok = [s for s in R if t - s >= min_size]
        vals = np.array([F[s] + cost(s, t) + beta for s in ok])
        evals += len(ok)
        F[t], last[t] = vals.min(), ok[int(vals.argmin())]
        if prune:                            # Theorem 5.6 with kappa = 0
            R = [s for s in R if t - s < min_size] + \
                [s for s, v in zip(ok, vals) if v - beta <= F[t]]
        R.append(t)
    cps, t = [], n
    while t > 0:                             # backtrack the optimal segmentation
        t = last[t]
        cps.append(t)
    return [int(c) for c in sorted(cps)[1:]], evals

beta = 2 * np.log(n)                         # BIC-type penalty, sigma = 1
print("true change points:", np.cumsum(lengths)[:-1].tolist())
for prune in (False, True):
    cps, evals = pelt(beta, prune=prune)
    print(f"{'PELT' if prune else 'optimal partitioning':20s} {cps}  cost evaluations: {evals}")

Both searches return the change points (201, 350, 600, 700) for the true (200, 350, 600, 700). Optimal partitioning needs 492 532 cost evaluations, PELT 100 618: the same exact answer, about five times cheaper here, and the gap grows with n.

Anomaly detection

Definition 5.10 — Point, contextual and collective anomalies Following the official survey of Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar (2009), an anomaly is a pattern that does not conform to the expected normal behaviour.

  • A point anomaly is a single value that is abnormal with respect to the whole series (a spike).
  • A contextual anomaly is abnormal only in its context — a temperature normal in July but not in January, a value at the wrong phase of a cycle.
  • A collective anomaly is a subsequence that is abnormal as a whole, although each of its values may be normal (a rhythm that changes for a few seconds).

Detection is supervised when labelled anomalies exist, semi-supervised when only normal data are available, and unsupervised otherwise. A detector outputs a score s_t and raises an alarm when s_t exceeds a threshold.

The main families reuse the previous chapters. Prediction-based detectors score the residual of a forecaster (ch. 2). Distance-based ones score the distance to the nearest neighbour, such as the matrix-profile discords of ch. 3. Reconstruction-based ones score the error of a dictionary or a low-rank model (ch. 4).

Proposition 5.11 — False alarms of a threshold rule Let the residuals of normal data be i.i.d. \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) and raise an alarm when |r_t| > k\sigma. The false-alarm probability per sample is 2(1 - \Phi(k)), where \Phi is the standard Gaussian distribution function.

Over n normal samples, the expected number of false alarms is 2n(1 - \Phi(k)). The probability of at least one false alarm stays below \alpha if k = \Phi^{-1}\Big(1 - \frac{\alpha}{2n}\Big).

Proof. The first claim is the Gaussian tail, the second linearity of expectation, the third the union bound over the n samples. ∎

With k = 3 the per-sample rate is 0.0027, that is 27 false alarms expected in 10^4 normal samples. To keep a 5 % chance of any false alarm over 10^4 samples, the threshold must rise to k \approx 4.56 .

Definition 5.12 — Isolation forest (Liu, Ting and Zhou, 2008) An isolation tree splits a random subsample of \psi points recursively, each time on a random feature at a random threshold, until every point is alone. Anomalies are few and different, so they are isolated after few splits.

With h(x) the depth at which x is isolated, averaged over the trees, the score is s(x) = 2^{-\mathbb{E}[h(x)] / c(\psi)}, \qquad c(\psi) = 2H(\psi - 1) - \frac{2(\psi - 1)}{\psi}, where H(i) \approx \ln i + 0.5772 is the harmonic number and c(\psi) the average depth of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree. Scores close to 1 indicate anomalies.

Applied to sliding windows of a series, the forest turns collective anomalies into point anomalies of the window space.

Proposition 5.13 — CUSUM, the online detector of a change Let the law of the samples switch from p_0 to p_1 at an unknown time, and set \ell_t = \log\big(p_1(y_t) / p_0(y_t)\big). Page’s statistic S_0 = 0, S_t = \max(0,\ S_{t-1} + \ell_t) satisfies S_t = \max_{1 \le k \le t+1} \ \sum_{i=k}^{t} \ell_i , with the empty sum (for k = t + 1) equal to 0. It is the log-likelihood ratio of the most likely change time against no change; an alarm is raised when S_t > h.

Proof. By induction: if the formula holds at t - 1, then S_{t-1} + \ell_t = \max_{k \le t} \sum_{i=k}^{t} \ell_i, and taking the maximum with 0 adds the case k = t + 1. ∎

For a Gaussian mean shift from \mu_0 to \mu_1, \ell_t is proportional to y_t - (\mu_0 + \mu_1)/2. The threshold h trades the mean time between false alarms against the detection delay; Lorden (1971) proved that CUSUM is asymptotically optimal for this trade-off. The theory is developed in Detection Theory and Industrial Applications.

Note 5.14 — Evaluating a detector honestly Anomalies are rare, so accuracy is meaningless; report precision and recall at the event level, the detection delay and how the threshold was chosen. The popular point-adjust protocol counts a whole anomalous segment as detected as soon as one of its points is flagged; Kim and coauthors (2022) show that it lets even random scores look competitive.

Wu and Keogh (2023) also found that several public benchmarks contain trivial or mislabelled anomalies. Synthetic anomalies with known type and position, as below, are a useful complement.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n, p = 3000, 50                              # period-50 cycle + AR(1) noise
noise = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    noise[t] = 0.5 * noise[t - 1] + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal()
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(n) / p) + noise
x[700] += 3.0                                # point anomaly
x[1500:1525] = x[1475:1500]                  # contextual: half a cycle repeated
x[2300:2400] = np.sin(4 * np.pi * np.arange(100) / p) + noise[2300:2400]  # collective

# (a) residuals of a seasonal-naive forecast, standardised by a robust scale
r = x[p:] - x[:-p]
z = np.abs(r - np.median(r)) / (1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(r - np.median(r))))
def regions(idx, gap=10):                    # group flagged times into intervals
    cuts = np.flatnonzero(np.diff(idx) > gap)
    return [(int(a), int(b)) for a, b in zip(idx[np.r_[0, cuts + 1]], idx[np.r_[cuts, -1]])]
print("(a) residual |z| > 4 in:", regions(np.flatnonzero(z > 4) + p))

# (b) isolation forest on sliding windows of one period
W = np.lib.stride_tricks.sliding_window_view(x, p)[::5]
s = -IsolationForest(random_state=0).fit(W).score_samples(W)
print("(b) isolation forest, 5 most anomalous windows start at:", np.sort(np.argsort(s)[-5:] * 5))

The residual detector (a) flags the spike at 700, the contextual anomaly around 1508–1519 and parts of the collective one (2315–2335). It also flags 750, 1560–1567 and 2418–2433: these are echoes, because the seasonal forecast \hat x_t = x_{t-50} reuses the anomalous values one period later. The isolation forest (b) flags only windows that overlap the collective anomaly (starts 2285 to 2345).

Each detector sees the anomalies its representation makes visible; combining a residual score with a window score covers the three types.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Change-point detection minimises a sum of segment costs plus a penalty; the cost decides which changes are visible (mean, covariance, distribution).
  • Optimal partitioning is exact in \mathcal{O}(n^2); PELT prunes candidates and stays exact, often in linear time.
  • A BIC-type penalty 2\sigma^2\log n calibrates the number of changes for mean shifts.
  • Anomalies are point, contextual or collective; residual, distance, reconstruction and isolation scores each see some of them.
  • Thresholds must account for the number of tests; CUSUM detects changes online.

6. Repairing data: denoising and missing values

Real recordings are noisy and incomplete: sensors add measurement noise, connections drop, batteries die. This chapter removes noise by filtering and by thresholding in a sparse basis, then fills missing values by interpolation, by the Kalman smoother of ch. 2, and by low-rank completion when several channels are recorded together.

Denoising

Throughout, y_t = x_t + \varepsilon_t: a signal of interest plus noise, and the goal is to estimate x from y.

Theorem 6.1 — The Wiener filter Let x and \varepsilon be centred, uncorrelated, weakly stationary, with spectral densities f_x and f_\varepsilon. Among the estimators \hat x = h * y with absolutely summable h, the mean squared error is minimal for the frequency response

Key result — the Wiener filter H(\omega) = \frac{f_x(\omega)}{f_x(\omega) + f_\varepsilon(\omega)},

and the minimal error is \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \frac{f_x(\omega) f_\varepsilon(\omega)}{f_x(\omega) + f_\varepsilon(\omega)}\, d\omega .

Proof. Filtering a stationary process by h multiplies its spectral density by |H(\omega)|^2, where H is the transfer function of h (the same computation as in Proposition 1.13).

The error x - h * y = (\delta - h) * x - h * \varepsilon is a sum of two uncorrelated filtered processes. Its density is |1 - H(\omega)|^2 f_x(\omega) + |H(\omega)|^2 f_\varepsilon(\omega), and its variance is the integral of this density (Theorem 1.7).

At each frequency, a|1 - H|^2 + b|H|^2 with a, b \ge 0 is minimised by H = a / (a + b), with value ab / (a + b). Minimising the integrand pointwise minimises the integral. ∎

The filter passes the frequencies where the signal dominates and attenuates those where the noise dominates. In practice f_y is estimated by Welch’s method, f_\varepsilon = \sigma^2 / (2\pi) for a white noise, and f_x = \max(f_y - f_\varepsilon, 0). The Wiener filter is optimal among linear filters only: it blurs the jumps of a piecewise-constant signal, whose energy spreads over all frequencies.

Definition 6.2 — Thresholding estimators Let W be an orthonormal transform in which the signal is sparse, for instance the Haar transform (Proposition 1.12). A thresholding estimator computes the coefficients u = Wy, applies a scalar rule \rho_\lambda to each of them, and transforms back: \hat x = W^\top \rho_\lambda(W y).

Since W is orthonormal, the noise coefficients W\varepsilon are again i.i.d. \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) when \varepsilon is. A few large coefficients carry the signal, and many small ones carry only noise.

Theorem 6.3 — Soft thresholding is \ell_1-penalised least squares For W orthonormal, \arg\min_x \ \frac12 \|y - x\|^2 + \lambda \|Wx\|_1 = W^\top S_\lambda(W y), with S_\lambda the soft thresholding of Proposition 4.5.

Proof. Set \theta = Wx. Orthonormality gives \|y - x\| = \|Wy - \theta\|, so the problem becomes \min_\theta \ \frac12 \|Wy - \theta\|^2 + \lambda\|\theta\|_1 .

This is the proximal operator of \lambda\|\cdot\|_1 at Wy, which is S_\lambda(Wy) by Proposition 4.5. ∎

Proposition 6.4 — The universal threshold Let \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_n) with n \ge 2. Then \mathbb{P}\Big( \max_i |\varepsilon_i| > \sigma \sqrt{2 \log n} \Big) \ \le\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi \log n}} \ \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{}\ 0 .

Proof. For a standard Gaussian Z and u > 0, the Mills inequality gives \mathbb{P}(|Z| > u) \le \sqrt{2/\pi}\, e^{-u^2/2} / u. With u = \sqrt{2 \log n}, e^{-u^2/2} = 1/n. The union bound over n coordinates gives n \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \cdot \frac{1}{n \sqrt{2\log n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi \log n}}.

∎

With the universal threshold \lambda = \sigma\sqrt{2\log n}, every pure-noise coefficient is set to zero with high probability, so the estimate does not invent oscillations. Donoho and Johnstone (1994) proved that its risk is within a factor of order 2\log n of an oracle that knows which coefficients to keep. The noise level is estimated from the finest wavelet details, \hat\sigma = \mathrm{median}(|d^{(1)}|) / 0.6745, which the few signal coefficients barely affect.

Proposition 6.5 — Hard thresholding is \ell_0-penalised least squares For every u \in \mathbb{R}^n, \arg\min_\theta \ \frac12 \|u - \theta\|^2 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \|\theta\|_0 \quad \text{is} \quad \theta_i = u_i\, \mathbb{1}\big[|u_i| > \lambda\big].

Proof. The problem separates. For one coordinate, keeping \theta_i \ne 0 costs at least \lambda^2/2, attained at \theta_i = u_i; setting \theta_i = 0 costs u_i^2 / 2. Keep the coefficient if and only if u_i^2 > \lambda^2. ∎

Soft thresholding shrinks every kept coefficient by \lambda, which biases large ones; hard thresholding keeps them intact but is discontinuous. The code compares both with a moving average on a piecewise-constant signal with a bump.

import numpy as np

def haar(x):                                 # orthonormal Haar transform (Prop. 1.12)
    coeffs, a = [], x.copy()
    while len(a) > 1:
        coeffs.append((a[0::2] - a[1::2]) / np.sqrt(2))   # details
        a = (a[0::2] + a[1::2]) / np.sqrt(2)              # approximation
    return a, coeffs

def ihaar(a, coeffs):
    for d in reversed(coeffs):
        out = np.empty(2 * len(a))
        out[0::2], out[1::2] = (a + d) / np.sqrt(2), (a - d) / np.sqrt(2)
        a = out
    return a

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 1024
t = np.arange(n) / n
clean = 2 * (t > 0.2) - 3 * (t > 0.45) + 1.5 * (t > 0.7) + np.exp(-((t - 0.85) / 0.03) ** 2)
y = clean + 0.4 * rng.standard_normal(n)

a, c = haar(y)
sigma = np.median(np.abs(c[0])) / 0.6745     # noise level from the finest details
lam = sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.log(n))         # universal threshold (Prop. 6.4)
soft = lambda d: np.sign(d) * np.maximum(np.abs(d) - lam, 0)   # Theorem 6.3
hard = lambda d: d * (np.abs(d) > lam)                          # Proposition 6.5
x_soft = ihaar(a, [soft(d) for d in c])
x_hard = ihaar(a, [hard(d) for d in c])
x_ma = np.convolve(y, np.ones(9) / 9, mode="same")       # moving average, width 9
rmse = lambda u: np.sqrt(np.mean((u - clean) ** 2))
print(f"sigma estimate {sigma:.3f} (true 0.4), threshold {lam:.2f}")
for name, u in [("noisy", y), ("moving average", x_ma), ("Haar, soft", x_soft), ("Haar, hard", x_hard)]:
    print(f"RMSE {name:15s} {rmse(u):.3f}")

The estimated noise level is 0.376 for a true 0.4. The errors (RMSE) are 0.389 for the noisy signal, 0.167 for the moving average, 0.234 for soft thresholding and 0.131 for hard thresholding. Soft thresholding pays for its bias on the large jump coefficients; hard thresholding keeps the jumps sharp and wins.

For signals that are exactly piecewise constant, total-variation denoising solves \min_x \frac12\|y - x\|^2 + \lambda \sum_t |x_{t+1} - x_t| (Rudin, Osher and Fatemi, 1992). Its solution is piecewise constant, so it is a convex relative of the change-point detection of ch. 5; in one dimension it is computed exactly by a direct algorithm (Condat, 2013).

Missing values

Definition 6.6 — Missingness mechanisms (Rubin, 1976) Let M_t = 1 when x_t is observed and 0 otherwise. The data are:

  • missing completely at random (MCAR) if the law of M does not depend on x;
  • missing at random (MAR) if it depends only on the observed values;
  • missing not at random (MNAR) otherwise — a sensor that saturates when the value is high.

Under MCAR and MAR, likelihood-based methods may ignore the mechanism; under MNAR it must be modelled. In time series the pattern matters as much: isolated samples, long gaps when a sensor is down, or blocks missing on several channels at once.

Theorem 6.7 — Natural cubic splines minimise curvature Let t_1 < \dots < t_m be the observed times with values x_1, \dots, x_m.

Among the functions f with square-integrable second derivative that interpolate the data, the natural cubic spline s — cubic on each interval, twice continuously differentiable, with s'' = 0 at both ends — minimises \int_{t_1}^{t_m} f''(t)^2\, dt .

Proof. Let g = f - s, which vanishes at every t_j. Then \int f''^2 = \int s''^2 + 2\int s'' g'' + \int g''^2, and it suffices to show that the middle term vanishes.

On [t_j, t_{j+1}], integrate by parts: \int s'' g'' = [s'' g'] - \int s''' g'. The boundary terms telescope to the end points, where s'' = 0. Since s''' is a constant c_j on each interval, the remaining term is \sum_j c_j\,(g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j)) = 0. ∎

Splines and linear interpolation are smooth and cheap, but they ignore the dynamics: across a long gap they draw a smooth curve, not a plausible piece of the series. A model of the dynamics does better.

Proposition 6.8 — The Kalman filter with missing observations In the state-space model of Definition 2.7, if y_t is missing, the update step of Theorem 2.8 is skipped: m_{t \mid t} = m_{t \mid t-1}, \qquad P_{t \mid t} = P_{t \mid t-1}, which is the update with gain K_t = 0, or with infinite observation noise. The smoother is unchanged, and m_{t \mid n} is the conditional expectation of the state given all the observed values.

Proof. Without y_t, the conditioning information at time t is the same as at time t - 1, so the filtering law equals the predicted law. The proofs of Theorem 2.8 and of the smoother only use Gaussian conditioning on the observed values, so they carry over with the reduced information. If only some coordinates of y_t are missing, keep the observed rows of H and the matching block of R. ∎

Under MAR, m_{t \mid n} is the best imputation in mean square for a linear Gaussian model, and P_{t \mid n} says how uncertain it is. The code imputes an AR(2) series with scattered missing values and one gap of 20 samples.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n, phi = 1000, np.array([1.6, -0.8])         # quasi-periodic AR(2), period about 13
x = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(2, n):
    x[t] = phi @ x[t - 2 : t][::-1] + rng.standard_normal()
miss = np.zeros(n, bool)
miss[700:720] = True                         # a gap of 20 samples
miss[500:] |= rng.random(n - 500) < 0.15     # + 15% missing at random
y = np.where(miss, np.nan, x)

def kalman_smoother(y, F, Q, H, R):          # filter (Theorem 2.8) + RTS smoother
    n, d = len(y), F.shape[0]
    m, P = np.zeros(d), np.eye(d) * 10.0
    ms, Ps, mp, Pp = np.zeros((n, d)), np.zeros((n, d, d)), np.zeros((n, d)), np.zeros((n, d, d))
    for t in range(n):
        m, P = F @ m, F @ P @ F.T + Q        # predict
        mp[t], Pp[t] = m, P
        if not np.isnan(y[t]):               # update only when observed (Prop. 6.8)
            S = H @ P @ H.T + R
            G = P @ H.T / S
            m, P = m + (G * (y[t] - H @ m)).ravel(), P - G @ H @ P
        ms[t], Ps[t] = m, P
    for t in range(n - 2, -1, -1):           # backward pass
        J = Ps[t] @ F.T @ np.linalg.inv(Pp[t + 1])
        ms[t] = ms[t] + J @ (ms[t + 1] - mp[t + 1])
        Ps[t] = Ps[t] + J @ (Ps[t + 1] - Pp[t + 1]) @ J.T
    return ms[:, 0]

F = np.array([phi, [1.0, 0.0]])              # state (x_t, x_{t-1})
Q = np.diag([1.0, 0.0])
H = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])
x_kal = kalman_smoother(y, F, Q, H, R=np.array([[1e-6]]))
obs = np.flatnonzero(~miss)
x_lin = np.interp(np.arange(n), obs, x[obs])
for name, u in [("linear interpolation", x_lin), ("AR(2) Kalman smoother", x_kal)]:
    gap, scattered = np.arange(700, 720), np.flatnonzero(miss & (np.arange(n) < 700))
    print(f"{name:22s} RMSE gap {np.sqrt(np.mean((u - x)[gap] ** 2)):.2f}"
          f" | scattered {np.sqrt(np.mean((u - x)[scattered] ** 2)):.2f}")
print("standard deviation of the series:", round(x.std(), 2))

On the scattered missing values, the smoother’s error is 0.46 against 0.79 for linear interpolation. In the 20-sample gap both errors (3.50 and 3.63) are close to the standard deviation of the series, 3.61. The gap is longer than the memory of the process, so no method can know the values in its middle; the smoother at least reports this through its variance P_{t \mid n}.

When several channels are recorded, they often share a few latent signals. The matrix of channels \times time is then nearly low rank, and the observed channels inform the missing ones.

Definition 6.9 — Low-rank completion Let X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T} be observed on a set \Omega of entries, and P_\Omega the operator that zeroes the other entries. Nuclear-norm completion solves \min_Z \ \frac12 \|P_\Omega(X - Z)\|_F^2 + \lambda \|Z\|_* , where \|Z\|_*, the sum of the singular values, is the convex surrogate of the rank.

The proximal operator of \lambda\|\cdot\|_* is the singular-value soft thresholding Z = U S_\lambda(\Sigma) V^\top of the SVD U\Sigma V^\top (Cai, Candès and Shen, 2010). Soft-impute iterates Z \leftarrow \mathrm{SVT}_\lambda\big(P_\Omega(X) + P_\Omega^\perp(Z)\big) (Mazumder, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2010). Candès and Recht (2009) proved exact recovery of incoherent low-rank matrices from enough randomly sampled entries.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
T, d, r = 400, 20, 3                         # 20 sensors driven by 3 latent signals
t = np.arange(T)
latent = np.stack([np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 50), np.sign(np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 120)),
                   np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal(T)) / 10])
X = rng.standard_normal((d, r)) @ latent + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal((d, T))
mask = rng.random((d, T)) > 0.3              # 30% of the entries are missing

def soft_impute(Y, mask, lam, iters=200):    # Mazumder, Hastie and Tibshirani (2010)
    Z = np.zeros_like(Y)
    for _ in range(iters):
        U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(np.where(mask, Y, Z), full_matrices=False)
        Z = (U * np.maximum(s - lam, 0)) @ Vt    # singular-value soft thresholding
    return Z

Z = soft_impute(X, mask, lam=2.0)
lin = np.array([np.interp(t, t[m], row[m]) for row, m in zip(X, mask)])
err = lambda A: np.sqrt(np.mean((A - X)[~mask] ** 2))
print(f"RMSE on missing entries: per-sensor linear interpolation {err(lin):.3f}, "
      f"low-rank soft-impute {err(Z):.3f}")
print("top singular values:", [round(float(v), 1) for v in np.linalg.svd(Z, compute_uv=False)[:5]])

Soft-impute reaches an error of 0.140 against 0.256 for per-sensor interpolation, and the completed matrix has three dominant singular values (440.4, 92.6, 51.5) followed by values near 0: the rank 3 of the generating model is recovered. Chapter 8 adds a graph between the sensors to the same idea.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • The Wiener filter is the optimal linear denoiser, with response f_x / (f_x + f_\varepsilon); it blurs jumps.
  • In an orthonormal basis, soft and hard thresholding solve the \ell_1- and \ell_0-penalised problems; the universal threshold \sigma\sqrt{2\log n} removes pure noise.
  • Missing data are MCAR, MAR or MNAR; the mechanism decides which methods are valid.
  • Splines minimise curvature; the Kalman smoother imputes with the dynamics and reports its uncertainty.
  • Multichannel data are completed by low-rank models through singular-value thresholding.

7. Extracting and selecting relevant features

Many learning problems on series are solved by turning each series into a vector of well-chosen numbers — its features — and then using any standard classifier or regressor. This chapter lists the main families of features, presents random convolutional features, and then selects the relevant features without fooling oneself. Named features also serve the official emphasis on understanding and interpreting the data.

Feature extraction

Definition 7.1 — Feature map and its families A feature map \varphi : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^p sends a series (or a window of a long series) to a fixed-size vector. It turns series of different lengths into a standard tabular data set. The main families are:

  • statistical: mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, quantiles, maximum of |x_t|;
  • temporal: autocorrelations at small lags (Definition 1.5), zero-crossing rate, number of peaks, AR coefficients (Theorem 2.3);
  • spectral: band powers from Welch’s periodogram, dominant frequency, spectral entropy, wavelet energy per scale (ch. 1);
  • pattern-based: shapelet distances (ch. 3), activation counts of learned atoms (ch. 4).

A good feature is invariant to what should not matter (offset, position, amplitude) and sensitive to what defines the classes.

Large catalogues exist. The hctsa project of Fulcher and Jones (2014) computes thousands of features. catch22 (Lubba and coauthors, 2019) keeps 22 of them, chosen for accuracy and low redundancy across many data sets. tsfresh (Christ and coauthors, 2018) filters a large catalogue by hypothesis tests.

Definition 7.2 — Random convolutional kernel features (ROCKET) Draw K random kernels. Each has a length L \in \{7, 9, 11\}, Gaussian weights centred to mean zero, and a bias b uniform in [-1, 1].

Its dilation is \delta = \lfloor 2^u \rfloor, with u uniform between 0 and \log_2\big((n-1)/(L-1)\big), so that the dilated kernel fits in the series.

For each kernel, compute the dilated convolution c_t = b + \sum_{l} w_l\, x_{t + l\delta} and keep two features: \mathrm{ppv} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{t=1}^{m} \mathbb{1}[c_t > 0], \qquad \max_{1 \le t \le m} c_t ,

where m is the number of positions of the convolution.

A linear classifier is trained on the 2K features (Dempster, Petitjean and Webb, 2020).

The dilations cover all scales, and the proportion of positive values measures how often a pattern is present, wherever it is. With K = 10\,000 kernels and a ridge classifier, ROCKET matched the most accurate classifiers of its time on the UCR archive at a small fraction of their cost. The block below is a simplified version (no padding, 500 kernels).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeClassifierCV

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
def cbf(n, length=128):                      # Cylinder-Bell-Funnel, as in ch. 3
    t, X, y = np.arange(length), [], np.repeat([0, 1, 2], n)
    for label in y:
        a = rng.integers(16, 33); b = a + rng.integers(32, 97)
        chi = ((t >= a) & (t <= b)).astype(float)
        shape = [chi, chi * (t - a) / (b - a), chi * (b - t) / (b - a)][label]
        X.append((6 + rng.standard_normal()) * shape + rng.standard_normal(length))
    return np.array(X), y

def random_kernels(k, T):                    # Definition 7.2
    ks = []
    for _ in range(k):
        L = rng.choice([7, 9, 11])
        w = rng.standard_normal(L); w -= w.mean()
        dil = int(2 ** rng.uniform(0, np.log2((T - 1) / (L - 1))))
        ks.append((w, rng.uniform(-1, 1), dil))
    return ks

def transform(X, ks):                        # 2 features per kernel: PPV and max
    out = []
    for w, b, dil in ks:
        wd = np.zeros((len(w) - 1) * dil + 1); wd[::dil] = w     # dilated kernel
        C = np.array([np.convolve(x, wd[::-1], mode="valid") for x in X]) + b
        out += [(C > 0).mean(1), C.max(1)]
    return np.column_stack(out)

Xtr, ytr = cbf(10); Xte, yte = cbf(100)
ks = random_kernels(500, Xtr.shape[1])
clf = RidgeClassifierCV(alphas=np.logspace(-3, 3, 10)).fit(transform(Xtr, ks), ytr)
print("random-kernel features: test accuracy", round(clf.score(transform(Xte, ks), yte), 3))

From 30 training series, the random features reach a test accuracy of 0.97 on 300 series, without any distance computation between series.

Feature selection

Extracting hundreds of features is easy; many are irrelevant or redundant. Selecting a few improves accuracy when data are scarce, speeds up the model, and makes it interpretable. The official survey of Li and coauthors (2018) organises the methods.

Definition 7.3 — Filter, wrapper and embedded selection Let X_j be feature j and Y the label.

  • A filter ranks features by a score computed independently of any model. Examples are the Fisher score, the ratio of between-class to within-class variance of X_j, and the mutual information I(X_j; Y).
  • A wrapper searches subsets of features, scoring each subset by the cross-validated performance of a model. Sequential forward selection adds the best feature at each step. It costs many fits and can overfit the validation data.
  • An embedded method selects while learning, as the lasso does through its \ell_1 penalty, or trees through their split importances.

Proposition 7.4 — Mutual information of a Gaussian pair If (X, Y) is a Gaussian pair with correlation \rho, then I(X; Y) = -\frac12 \log(1 - \rho^2).

Proof. Write I(X; Y) = h(X) + h(Y) - h(X, Y) with the differential entropy of a Gaussian vector h = \frac12 \log\big((2\pi e)^d \det\Sigma\big). The covariance of the pair has determinant \sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2), so all terms cancel except -\frac12\log(1 - \rho^2). ∎

Mutual information thus extends the squared correlation, and it also detects nonlinear dependence. It is estimated by nearest-neighbour methods (Kraskov, Stögbauer and Grassberger, 2004), which scikit-learn uses. Ranking by I(X_j; Y) alone keeps redundant features: the mRMR criterion (Peng, Long and Ding, 2005) adds features greedily by relevance to Y minus mean redundancy with the features already chosen.

Proposition 7.5 — Optimality conditions of the lasso The vector \hat\beta minimises \frac{1}{2n}\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_1 if and only if, for every feature j, with the residual r = y - X\hat\beta: \frac1n X_j^\top r = \lambda\, \mathrm{sign}(\hat\beta_j) \ \text{ if } \hat\beta_j \ne 0, \qquad \Big|\frac1n X_j^\top r\Big| \le \lambda \ \text{ if } \hat\beta_j = 0 .

Proof. The objective is convex, so \hat\beta is a minimiser if and only if 0 belongs to its subdifferential, -\frac1n X^\top r + \lambda\,\partial\|\hat\beta\|_1.

The subdifferential of |\beta_j| is \{\mathrm{sign}(\beta_j)\} when \beta_j \ne 0, and the interval [-1, 1] otherwise. ∎

A feature enters the model only when its correlation with the residual reaches \lambda. For \lambda \ge \|X^\top y\|_\infty / n the solution is \hat\beta = 0; decreasing \lambda lets features enter one by one along the regularisation path, which ranks them. Among strongly correlated features the lasso picks one somewhat arbitrarily; the elastic net and stability selection (Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2010) make the choice more stable.

Note 7.6 — Selection bias Selecting features on the whole data set and then cross-validating the classifier on the selected features gives an optimistic estimate. With many candidate features and few samples, pure noise can look predictive. Ambroise and McLachlan (2002) showed this effect on gene-expression data.

The selection is part of the model and must be redone inside each training fold — in scikit-learn, by putting it in a Pipeline. For a single long series, the folds must also respect time (Definition 2.10).

The code builds two classes of AR(1) series, one of which contains a weak burst near 0.22 cycle per sample. It extracts 20 named features, adds 30 pure-noise features, and selects with mutual information inside the cross-validation and with an \ell_1-penalised logistic regression.

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import lfilter, welch
from scipy.stats import skew, kurtosis
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, mutual_info_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
N, T = 300, 256
y = rng.integers(0, 2, N)
X = np.zeros((N, T))
for k in range(N):                           # AR(1) background, random coefficient
    X[k] = lfilter([1.0], [1.0, -rng.uniform(0.2, 0.8)], rng.standard_normal(T))
    if y[k]:                                 # class 1: a weak burst near 0.22 cycle/sample
        f0, start = rng.uniform(0.2, 0.25), rng.integers(0, T - 64)
        X[k, start : start + 64] += 1.2 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f0 * np.arange(64))

def features(x):                             # 20 named, interpretable features
    xc = x - x.mean()
    acf = [xc[:-h] @ xc[h:] / (xc @ xc) for h in range(1, 6)]
    f, P = welch(x, nperseg=64)
    bands = [P[(f >= lo) & (f < lo + 0.05)].sum() / P.sum() for lo in np.arange(0, 0.5, 0.05)]
    zc = np.mean(np.diff(np.sign(xc)) != 0)
    return np.r_[x.std(), skew(x), kurtosis(x), zc, np.max(np.abs(x)), acf, bands]

names = ["std", "skew", "kurt", "zero-cross", "max|x|"] + [f"acf{h}" for h in range(1, 6)] \
        + [f"band{lo:.2f}" for lo in np.arange(0, 0.5, 0.05)]
Phi = np.array([features(x) for x in X])
Phi = np.c_[Phi, rng.standard_normal((N, 30))]          # + 30 pure-noise features
names += [f"noise{j}" for j in range(30)]

mi = mutual_info_classif(Phi, y, random_state=0)
print("top-5 by mutual information:", [names[j] for j in np.argsort(mi)[::-1][:5]])
for k in (5, 50):                            # selection INSIDE the cross-validation
    pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SelectKBest(mutual_info_classif, k=k),
                         LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
    print(f"k = {k:2d} features, 5-fold accuracy {cross_val_score(pipe, Phi, y, cv=5).mean():.3f}")
l1 = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(l1_ratio=1.0, C=0.1, solver="liblinear"))
coef = l1.fit(Phi, y)[-1].coef_.ravel()
print("L1-logistic keeps:", [names[j] for j in np.flatnonzero(coef)])

Mutual information ranks first the power in the band 0.20–0.25, where the burst lives, then the autocorrelations at lags 2 to 4, which the oscillation also changes. One noise feature reaches the top five: mutual-information estimates are noisy on 300 samples. Five selected features give a cross-validated accuracy of 0.953, against 0.900 with all 50.

The \ell_1 model keeps four features: the standard deviation, the autocorrelation at lag 4 and the two bands 0.20 and 0.25. Each is a statement a domain expert can check: “class 1 has more energy between 0.20 and 0.30 cycle per sample”.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Feature maps turn series into fixed-size vectors; statistical, temporal, spectral and pattern-based features are interpretable.
  • Random dilated convolutional kernels with PPV and max features give strong, fast classifiers.
  • Filters (Fisher score, mutual information), wrappers and embedded methods (lasso) select features; mutual information generalises correlation.
  • The lasso keeps a feature only when its correlation with the residual reaches \lambda.
  • Selection belongs inside the cross-validation loop, or the estimate is biased.

8. Multivariate series and graphs

With d sensors recorded together — electrodes on a scalp, stations of a weather network, joints of a skeleton — the series is a d \times T array. Treating each channel separately wastes the structure between them; a full multivariate model has too many parameters. A graph between the channels is the compromise: it says which channels are expected to behave alike. This chapter follows the graph signal processing of Ortega and coauthors (2018), in the official references.

From vector autoregressions to graphs

Definition 8.1 — Vector autoregression and Granger causality A VAR(1) model is x_t = A x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t with x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d and a white noise \varepsilon_t. It is stationary and causal when the spectral radius of A is below 1.

Channel j Granger-causes channel i if the past of j improves the prediction of x_{i,t} given the past of all other channels (Granger, 1969). In a VAR(1), this holds exactly when A_{ij} \ne 0, so the support of A is a directed graph.

A VAR(p) has p d^2 parameters, too many when d is large: estimating it with a lasso penalty on each row of A learns a sparse directed graph. Most of this chapter uses instead an undirected graph that encodes similarity between channels.

Signals on a graph

Definition 8.2 — Graph, Laplacian and smoothness Let G be an undirected graph on d nodes with symmetric non-negative weights W_{ij}, degree matrix D = \mathrm{diag}(W\mathbf{1}) and Laplacian L = D - W.

A graph signal x \in \mathbb{R}^d gives one value per node, for instance the snapshot x_t of all sensors at time t. Its variation over the graph is the quadratic form x^\top L x = \frac12 \sum_{i,j} W_{ij} (x_i - x_j)^2 \ \ge\ 0 .

A signal is smooth on the graph when this quantity is small: strongly connected nodes carry similar values.

The identity follows by expanding: x^\top D x = \sum_i d_i x_i^2 and x^\top W x = \sum_{i,j} W_{ij} x_i x_j, and each pair contributes W_{ij}(x_i^2 + x_j^2 - 2x_ix_j)/2 twice.

Definition 8.3 — Graph Fourier transform Let L = U \Lambda U^\top with U orthogonal and eigenvalues 0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_d. The graph Fourier transform of x and its inverse are \hat x = U^\top x, \qquad x = U \hat x .

The eigenvalues play the role of frequencies: eigenvectors with small \lambda_k vary slowly across edges. On a path graph the eigenvectors are sampled cosines, and the transform reduces to a discrete cosine transform.

Proposition 8.4 — Frequencies measure variation For every x, x^\top L x = \sum_{k=1}^d \lambda_k\, \hat x_k^2 , and \lambda_k is the minimum of x^\top L x over unit vectors orthogonal to u_1, \dots, u_{k-1}, attained at u_k.

If G is connected, \lambda_1 = 0 is simple, with u_1 = \mathbf{1}/\sqrt d.

Proof. Substitute x = U\hat x: x^\top L x = \hat x^\top \Lambda \hat x.

Under the constraints, the first k - 1 coordinates of \hat x vanish and the others have squares summing to 1. So x^\top L x is a weighted average of \lambda_k, \dots, \lambda_d, hence at least \lambda_k, with equality at u_k.

If x^\top L x = 0, every edge has x_i = x_j, so x is constant on each connected component: the kernel of L is spanned by \mathbf{1} when G is connected. ∎

A signal is thus smooth exactly when its energy sits in the low graph frequencies. The next definition filters signals by frequency.

Definition 8.5 — Graph filters For a function h on the eigenvalues, the graph filter h(L) = U\, h(\Lambda)\, U^\top multiplies the graph frequency \lambda_k by h(\lambda_k).

If h is a polynomial of degree K, then (h(L)x)_i depends only on the values at nodes within K hops of node i.

The filtered signal then costs K products by the sparse Laplacian, with no eigendecomposition.

Indeed, L has nonzero entries only on the diagonal and between neighbours. By induction on k, the entry (L^k)_{ij} vanishes when i and j are more than k hops apart.

Theorem 8.6 — Denoising and interpolation on a graph Let a connected graph have Laplacian L, and let M = \mathrm{diag}(m) with m_i = 1 when node i is observed and 0 otherwise. Observe y on at least one node. For \gamma > 0, the problem \min_x \ \|M(y - x)\|^2 + \gamma\, x^\top L x has the unique solution

Key result — interpolation on a graph \hat x = (M + \gamma L)^{-1} M y .

When every node is observed, \hat x = (I + \gamma L)^{-1} y is the low-pass graph filter h(\lambda) = 1/(1 + \gamma\lambda).

Proof. The objective is a convex quadratic whose gradient is 2M(x - y) + 2\gamma L x, using M^2 = M. Setting it to zero gives (M + \gamma L)x = My.

The matrix is positive definite. Indeed, x^\top(M + \gamma L)x = \sum_{i \,:\, m_i = 1} x_i^2 + \gamma\, x^\top L x vanishes only if x is constant (Proposition 8.4) and zero on the observed nodes, that is, if x = 0.

With M = I, the solution is U (I + \gamma\Lambda)^{-1} U^\top y, the filter of Definition 8.5 with h(\lambda) = 1/(1 + \gamma\lambda). ∎

In Bayesian terms, \hat x is the posterior mean when x has the Gaussian prior with precision proportional to L (made invertible by a small multiple of the identity) and the observation noise is white. Interpolating a dead sensor from its neighbours is the graph analogue of the splines of ch. 6.

Learning the graph

Often no graph is given, and it must be learned from the multivariate series itself.

Proposition 8.7 — Gaussian graphical models Let x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma) in \mathbb{R}^d with precision matrix \Theta = \Sigma^{-1}. For i \ne j, \Theta_{ij} = 0 \iff x_i \text{ and } x_j \text{ are independent given all the other coordinates}.

Proof. Let A = \{i, j\} and B the other indices. By Proposition 0.2 and the block inversion formula, the conditional covariance of x_A given x_B is \Sigma_{AA} - \Sigma_{AB}\Sigma_{BB}^{-1}\Sigma_{BA} = (\Theta_{AA})^{-1}.

This 2 \times 2 matrix is diagonal iff \Theta_{ij} = 0, and for a Gaussian pair, uncorrelated means independent. ∎

The graphical lasso (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2008) estimates a sparse precision matrix from the empirical covariance S: \max_{\Theta \succ 0} \ \log\det\Theta - \mathrm{tr}(S\Theta) - \alpha \sum_{i \ne j} |\Theta_{ij}| .

If the snapshots are smooth signals with precision L + \varepsilon I — a Gaussian Markov random field on the graph — the support of the precision is exactly the set of edges.

Proposition 8.8 — Smoothness as a linear function of the weights Let X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T} have rows x_{(i)} (the series of node i) and columns x_t (the snapshots). With Z_{ij} = \|x_{(i)} - x_{(j)}\|^2, \sum_{t=1}^T x_t^\top L\, x_t = \mathrm{tr}(X^\top L X) = \frac12 \sum_{i,j} W_{ij} Z_{ij} .

Proof. Apply the identity of Definition 8.2 to each snapshot x_t and sum over t: \sum_t (x_{i,t} - x_{j,t})^2 = Z_{ij}. ∎

Learning a graph on which the data are smooth is therefore a problem that is linear in the weights. Kalofolias (2016) solves \min_{W \ge 0,\ W = W^\top,\ \mathrm{diag}(W) = 0} \ \sum_{i,j} W_{ij} Z_{ij} - \alpha\, \mathbf{1}^\top \log(W\mathbf{1}) + \beta \|W\|_F^2 , where the logarithmic barrier prevents isolated nodes and the Frobenius term controls sparsity. Dong and coauthors (2016) learn the Laplacian directly under a trace constraint.

Note 8.9 — Time–vertex models A multivariate series varies over the graph and over time. A joint regulariser such as \sum_t x_t^\top L\, x_t + \eta \sum_t \|x_{t+1} - x_t\|^2 penalises both variations. It is the quadratic form of the Laplacian of the Cartesian product of the graph with the path of time instants, whose time edges have weight \eta.

Its eigenvectors are products of a graph Fourier mode and a time mode. The time modes are the cosines of a path (Exercise 14); for periodic time they become the complex exponentials of the DFT. This joint time–vertex Fourier transform is developed by Grassi and coauthors (2018). Spatio-temporal neural networks (ch. 10) learn such filters.

The block below places 30 sensors at random in the unit square, connects those closer than 0.3, and draws 2000 smooth snapshots with precision L + 0.1 I. It then interpolates 30 % of missing readings with Theorem 8.6, and learns the graph back with the graphical lasso.

import numpy as np
import networkx as nx
from sklearn.covariance import GraphicalLasso

rng = np.random.default_rng(13)
G = nx.random_geometric_graph(30, 0.3, seed=13)          # 30 sensors in the unit square
Lap = nx.laplacian_matrix(G).toarray().astype(float)
lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Lap)                             # graph Fourier basis (Def. 8.3)

T = 2000                                                 # smooth signals: precision L + 0.1 I
X = U @ (rng.standard_normal((30, T)) / np.sqrt(lam + 0.1)[:, None])
Y = X + 0.3 * rng.standard_normal(X.shape)
print("share of energy in the 5 lowest graph frequencies:",
      round(float(((U.T @ X)[:5] ** 2).sum() / (X ** 2).sum()), 2))

mask = rng.random(Y.shape) > 0.3                         # 30% of readings missing
gamma = 0.1                                              # Tikhonov (Theorem 8.6)
Xhat = np.column_stack([np.linalg.solve(np.diag(m * 1.0) + gamma * Lap, m * y)
                        for m, y in zip(mask.T, Y.T)])
mean_imp = np.where(mask, Y, (Y * mask).sum(0) / mask.sum(0))   # fill with snapshot mean
err = lambda A: np.sqrt(np.mean((A - X)[~mask] ** 2))
print(f"RMSE on missing readings: snapshot mean {err(mean_imp):.3f}, graph Tikhonov {err(Xhat):.3f}")

prec = GraphicalLasso(alpha=0.02, max_iter=200).fit(X.T).precision_   # learn the graph back
iu = np.triu_indices(30, 1)
top = np.argsort(-np.abs(prec[iu]))[: G.number_of_edges()]
true = {(min(e), max(e)) for e in G.edges()}
hits = sum((int(iu[0][k]), int(iu[1][k])) in true for k in top)
print(f"graphical lasso: {hits} of its top {G.number_of_edges()} entries are true edges")

The five lowest graph frequencies carry 83 % of the energy: the snapshots are smooth. On the missing readings, the graph interpolation reaches an error of 0.416 against 0.570 for the mean of the observed sensors. The graphical lasso, given 2000 snapshots, ranks the 114 true edges exactly first: 114 \text{ of the top } 114 \text{ entries are edges}.

With fewer snapshots, or with signals that are only approximately smooth, the recovery degrades, and the choice of \alpha becomes the main difficulty.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • A graph between channels encodes which ones behave alike; the Laplacian quadratic form measures the variation of a snapshot over the graph.
  • The eigenvectors of the Laplacian define a graph Fourier transform; smooth signals live in low graph frequencies.
  • Polynomial graph filters are local and need no eigendecomposition.
  • Tikhonov regularisation with the Laplacian denoises and interpolates graph signals in closed form.
  • Graphs are learned from data through sparse precision matrices or through smoothness, which is linear in the weights.

9. Neural networks for time series

Self-study chapter, beyond the official syllabus. Neural networks learn the representation instead of choosing it. This chapter gives the three architectures used on series, one fact about each, and the baselines they must be compared with; Deep Learning covers the general theory.

Definition 9.1 — Dilated causal convolution A 1-D convolutional layer with kernel w \in \mathbb{R}^k and dilation \delta computes (w *_\delta x)_t = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} w_j\, x_{t - j\delta}, plus a bias and a nonlinearity, for each pair of input and output channels. It is causal because the output at time t uses only inputs up to t, which is required for forecasting. Stacks of such layers with dilations 1, 2, 4, \dots were introduced for audio generation by WaveNet (van den Oord and coauthors, 2016).

Proposition 9.2 — Receptive field of a dilated stack A stack of J layers with kernel size k and dilations \delta_1, \dots, \delta_J has an output at time t that depends on 1 + (k - 1)\sum_{j=1}^{J} \delta_j consecutive inputs. With \delta_j = 2^{j-1} this is 1 + (k-1)(2^J - 1): the receptive field grows exponentially with depth.

Proof. By induction on J. One layer sees 1 + (k-1)\delta_1 consecutive inputs. Adding layer J combines k outputs of the previous stack spaced by \delta_J, which extends the window by (k - 1)\delta_J. ∎

Proposition 9.3 — Vanishing and exploding gradients in recurrent networks For the linear recurrence h_t = W h_{t-1} + U x_t, \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} = W^{T-t}, \qquad \Big\| \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} \Big\| \le \|W\|^{T-t} .

The gradient thus vanishes geometrically when \|W\| < 1, and grows geometrically along an eigenvector when the spectral radius of W exceeds 1.

Proof. Differentiate the recursion T - t times; the inputs do not depend on h_t. Submultiplicativity of the operator norm gives the bound. ∎

With a nonlinearity, each factor W is multiplied by a diagonal matrix of derivatives, which does not change the picture. The LSTM (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) adds a gated memory cell whose self-connection is close to the identity, so that information and gradients can travel over long lags. DeepAR (Salinas and coauthors, 2020) trains one recurrent network on many related series and outputs a predictive distribution.

Note 9.4 — Transformers, patches and strong baselines Self-attention compares every time step with every other, at a cost quadratic in the length. Cutting the series into short patches (for instance of 16 samples) treated as tokens (PatchTST, Nie and coauthors, 2023) reduces the cost and improves long-horizon forecasts.

Zeng and coauthors (2023) showed that a single linear layer on the lag window beat several Transformer forecasters on the standard long-horizon benchmarks. The fully connected N-BEATS (Oreshkin and coauthors, 2020) and the convolutional InceptionTime classifier (Ismail Fawaz and coauthors, 2020) are other strong references.

A neural model should always be compared with the baselines of this page: seasonal naive and ARIMA for forecasting, 1-NN DTW and ROCKET for classification.

The block trains a three-layer dilated CNN (kernel 7, dilations 1, 2, 4) on 300 Cylinder–Bell–Funnel series, with global max pooling so that the class does not depend on the position of the pattern.

Its convolutions are centred, with padding on both sides, rather than causal as in Definition 9.1. For classification the whole series is available, so causality is not needed; Proposition 9.2 still gives the receptive field, now centred on t.

import numpy as np
import torch
from torch import nn

rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)                     # small model: one thread is fastest
def cbf(n, length=128):                      # Cylinder-Bell-Funnel, as in ch. 3
    t, X, y = np.arange(length), [], np.repeat([0, 1, 2], n)
    for label in y:
        a = rng.integers(16, 33); b = a + rng.integers(32, 97)
        chi = ((t >= a) & (t <= b)).astype(float)
        shape = [chi, chi * (t - a) / (b - a), chi * (b - t) / (b - a)][label]
        X.append((6 + rng.standard_normal()) * shape + rng.standard_normal(length))
    return torch.tensor(np.array(X), dtype=torch.float32)[:, None, :], torch.tensor(y)

Xtr, ytr = cbf(100)
Xte, yte = cbf(100)
net = nn.Sequential(                         # dilated convolutions, then global pooling
    nn.Conv1d(1, 16, 7, padding=3), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Conv1d(16, 16, 7, padding=6, dilation=2), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Conv1d(16, 16, 7, padding=12, dilation=4), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.AdaptiveMaxPool1d(1), nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(16, 3))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for epoch in range(20):
    for idx in torch.randperm(len(ytr)).split(32):
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(net(Xtr[idx]), ytr[idx])
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    acc = (net(Xte).argmax(1) == yte).float().mean().item()
print(f"1-D CNN, receptive field {1 + 6 * (1 + 2 + 4)} samples: test accuracy {acc:.3f}")

The receptive field is 1 + 6 \times 7 = 43 samples (Proposition 9.2), and the network classifies all 300 test series correctly. It needed 300 training series; 1-NN DTW and the random kernels of ch. 7 reached similar accuracy from 30. Exercise 15 compares the three methods as the training set shrinks.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Dilated causal convolutions reach a receptive field exponential in depth.
  • Plain recurrent networks suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients; gated cells such as the LSTM mitigate them.
  • Transformers on patches are strong forecasters, but simple linear and nearest-neighbour baselines remain hard to beat and must be reported.

10. Research directions and open problems

Self-study chapter. Each theme of the course is an active research field. This chapter points to a few directions, with works whose authors and dates are given so that they can be found; it is a starting point for the mini-project, not a survey.

Foundation models for forecasting

Following language models, several groups pretrain one large model on many series and use it without retraining on a new one (“zero-shot”). Chronos (Ansari and coauthors, 2024) scales each series, quantises its values into a vocabulary of bins, and trains a language-model architecture on the resulting tokens. TimesFM (Das, Kong, Sen and Zhou, 2024) is a decoder-only model pretrained on a large corpus of real and synthetic series.

Open questions: when do such models beat a well-tuned local model fitted to the series at hand? How to evaluate them when public benchmarks may be part of the pretraining corpus? Are their predictive intervals calibrated?

Honest evaluation

Benchmarks drive the field, and several have been questioned. For anomaly detection, Wu and Keogh (2023) found trivial and mislabelled anomalies in popular data sets, and Kim and coauthors (2022) showed that the point-adjust protocol inflates scores (Exercise 16). For classification, the UCR archive (Dau and coauthors, 2019) and the UEA multivariate archive (Bagnall and coauthors, 2018) standardise the splits, and comparisons over many data sets call for rank-based statistical tests (Demšar, 2006).

Change points beyond the mean

The official review of Truong, Oudre and Vayatis (2020) organises the field by cost, search method and constraint. Kernel costs detect changes in distribution, but the choice of the kernel and of the penalty remains largely empirical (Arlot, Celisse and Harchaoui, 2019). Online detection with false-alarm guarantees, change points in high-dimensional series, and costs learned from annotated examples are active directions.

Irregular sampling and missing values

Clinical and environmental series are sampled irregularly, with informative missingness. GRU-D (Che and coauthors, 2018) feeds the missingness mask and the time since the last observation into a recurrent network. Latent ordinary differential equations (Rubanova, Chen and Duvenaud, 2019) model the hidden state in continuous time. CSDI (Tashiro and coauthors, 2021) imputes with a conditional diffusion model and returns samples, hence uncertainty. Imputation under MNAR mechanisms, with calibrated uncertainty, is largely open.

Interpretable pattern extraction

Convolutional dictionary learning gives atoms that experts can inspect. On electrophysiological signals, rank-one multivariate atoms (Dupré la Tour and coauthors, 2018) and heavy-tailed noise models (Jas and coauthors, 2017) produce waveforms that match known physiology. Scaling these methods to days of recordings, and choosing the number and length of the atoms automatically, are open problems. Shapelet and random-kernel classifiers raise the same question: how to explain a decision with a few patterns without losing accuracy?

Graphs that change in time

Spatio-temporal neural networks combine graph filters with temporal models: DCRNN (Li and coauthors, 2018) uses diffusion convolutions on a road network inside a recurrent network, and Graph WaveNet (Wu and coauthors, 2019) learns an adaptive adjacency matrix together with dilated temporal convolutions. Time–vertex signal processing (Grassi and coauthors, 2018) gives the linear theory. Learning graphs that change over time from few samples, with guarantees, is open.

Open problems to think about

  • A principled penalty for change-point detection under dependent, non-Gaussian noise.
  • Evaluation protocols for anomaly detection that do not depend on an arbitrary threshold or event definition.
  • Imputation under MNAR mechanisms with calibrated uncertainty.
  • Zero-shot forecasting: conditions under which pretraining on other series provably helps.
  • Interpretable models that match deep networks on classification accuracy.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • Foundation models, honest evaluation, distributional change points, irregular sampling, interpretable dictionaries and dynamic graphs are open research fronts.
  • Each direction connects to a chapter of the course and to a mini-project subject below.

    import numpy as np

S1, S2 = np.r_[0, np.cumsum(y)], np.r_[0, np.cumsum(y**2)]
cost = lambda a, b: S2[b] - S2[a] - (S1[b] - S1[a]) ** 2 / (b - a)
beta = 2 * np.log(n)

def binseg(a, b, min_size=5):                # Note 5.7: best single split, then recurse
    gains = [(cost(a, b) - cost(a, t) - cost(t, b), t) for t in range(a + min_size, b - min_size)]
    if not gains:
        return []
    g, t = max(gains)
    return binseg(a, t) + [t] + binseg(t, b) if g > beta else []

def opt_partition(min_size=5):               # Proposition 5.5 (exact)
    F, last = np.full(n + 1, np.inf), np.zeros(n + 1, int)
    F[0] = -beta
    for t in range(min_size, n + 1):
        vals = [F[s] + cost(s, t) + beta for s in range(0, t - min_size + 1)]
        last[t] = int(np.argmin(vals)); F[t] = vals[last[t]]
    cps, t = [], n
    while t > 0:
        t = last[t]; cps.append(int(t))
    return sorted(cps)[1:]

print("binary segmentation:", binseg(0, n))

Compute the best F1 over thresholds point by point, then after point-adjust (a whole segment counts as detected if one of its points is flagged). Then propose an event-level metric that rewards early detection and penalises false alarms, and test it on a real detector of ch. 5.

    Hints and expected result: the best point-wise F1 of the random detector is about 0.09, and the best point-adjusted F1 is about 0.79: the inflation described by Kim and coauthors (2022). A sound metric counts events, not points, gives credit that decreases with the detection delay, and counts each false-alarm event once. Its value must stay near 0 for a random detector — this is the sanity check.

    import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 10_000
label = np.zeros(n, bool)
for s in rng.choice(np.arange(0, n - 100, 200), 5, replace=False):
    label[s : s + 100] = True                # 5 anomalous segments of 100 samples
score = rng.random(n)                        # a detector that knows nothing

def f1(pred, label):
    tp = np.sum(pred & label); fp = np.sum(pred & ~label); fn = np.sum(~pred & label)
    return 2 * tp / (2 * tp + fp + fn)

def point_adjust(pred, label):               # a hit anywhere marks the whole segment
    pred = pred.copy()
    edges = np.flatnonzero(np.diff(np.r_[0, label.astype(int), 0]))
    for a, b in zip(edges[::2], edges[1::2]):
        if pred[a:b].any():
            pred[a:b] = True
    return pred

ths = np.quantile(score, np.linspace(0.5, 0.9999, 200))
print("best point-wise F1   :", round(max(f1(score > h, label) for h in ths), 3))
print("best point-adjust F1 :", round(max(f1(point_adjust(score > h, label), label) for h in ths), 3))
