0. Review: convexity, smoothness and strong convexity

Every algorithm below chooses a step from one of three properties of a differentiable function. The function may be convex, smooth, strongly convex, or all three. This chapter writes the inequalities in the form the proofs use. The norm is Euclidean and the inner product is the dot product. A Newton step would factor a Hessian. These methods use only a scalar smoothness constant L.

Definition 0.1 — Convex, smooth, strongly convex

Let the objective be a differentiable function on Euclidean space. It is convex when every tangent lies under the graph. It is smooth when the gradient is Lipschitz. It is strongly convex when the tangent underestimates the function by a quadratic bowl. The three inequalities use a smoothness constant L and a strong-convexity constant \mu. They read as follows.

f(y) \ge f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), y - x \rangle ,

\| \nabla f(y) - \nabla f(x) \| \le L \, \| y - x \| ,

f(y) \ge f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), y - x \rangle + \frac{\mu}{2} \| y - x \|^2 .

The condition number is \kappa = L/\mu when \mu > 0. A larger \kappa means a longer valley.

Proposition 0.2 — Descent lemma

If f is L-smooth, then for every x and y

f(y) \le f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), y - x \rangle + \frac{L}{2} \| y - x \|^2 .

Proof. Write the increment as an integral of the derivative along the segment.

f(y) - f(x) = \int_0^1 \langle \nabla f(x + t(y-x)), y-x \rangle \, dt .

Subtract \langle \nabla f(x), y-x \rangle. The integrand becomes an inner product of \nabla f(x+t(y-x)) - \nabla f(x) with y-x. Lipschitz continuity bounds that inner product by t L \|y-x\|^2.

The integral of t from 0 to 1 equals 1/2. Multiply by L \|y-x\|^2 and the quadratic term appears.

∎

The lemma is an upper model. Minimizing the right-hand side in y is a gradient step of length 1/L. That is why the same step returns in ch. 1 and ch. 3. If the gradient is not Lipschitz, the model can lie under the graph and the step can climb.

Proposition 0.3 — Quadratic growth

If f is \mu-strongly convex and \nabla f(x^\star) = 0, then

f(x) \ge f(x^\star) + \frac{\mu}{2} \| x - x^\star \|^2 .

The minimizer is unique. Also \| \nabla f(x) \|^2 \ge 2\mu \, (f(x) - f(x^\star)).

Proof. Put x^\star into the strong-convexity inequality. The inner product vanishes because the gradient there is zero. That is the growth bound, and any second minimizer would have distance zero.

For the gradient bound, fix x and minimize the strong-convexity lower model in the free variable. The model is a quadratic in that variable. Its minimum value is f(x) - \| \nabla f(x) \|^2 / (2\mu). The true function lies above the model, so f(x^\star) lies above that minimum. Rearrange.

∎

Example 0.4 — One stiff direction

Take f(u, v) = (u^2 + 4 v^2)/2. The Hessian is diagonal with entries 1 and 4, so \mu = 1, L = 4 and \kappa = 4.

A gradient step of length 1/L = 1/4 multiplies u by 3/4 and multiplies v by 0. The stiff coordinate dies in one step. The flat coordinate shrinks by 3/4 at every step.

On the line, f(t) = (L/2)\, t^2 is even simpler. The step 1/L reaches the origin in one iteration. The step 2/L flips the sign and oscillates. That second step is not a descent method.

Strong convexity is what turns a 1/k guarantee into a geometric one. The constant L above is global. ch. 4 builds a tighter model at the current point.

Key — The safe step

\gamma = \frac{1}{L} .

For an L-smooth objective this step makes the quadratic upper model tight enough to descend. The step 2/L is the edge of the descent lemma and need not descend.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Convexity is a tangent underneath the graph. Smoothness is a Lipschitz gradient and a quadratic upper model.
  • Strong convexity adds a quadratic bowl. The minimizer is unique and the function grows at least like (\mu/2)\, \|x - x^\star\|^2.
  • The descent lemma is the integral of the Lipschitz bound. Its sharp step is 1/L.
  • On a quadratic, L and \mu are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of the Hessian.

1. Large-scale gradient and coordinate methods

A problem is large-scale here when forming or factorizing the Hessian is the wrong cost. A full gradient of a dense quadratic in dimension d already costs order d^2 arithmetic. A Newton step costs order d^3. This chapter uses only gradients, or only one coordinate of a gradient, and tracks the gap f(x_k) - f^\star.

The objective f is convex and L-smooth unless a statement asks for strong convexity. The minimizer is written x^\star. Assume it exists. Existence is free under Proposition 0.3, and it is an assumption when f is merely convex.

Definition 1.1 — Gradient step and coordinate step

The gradient iteration with constant step \gamma is

x_{k+1} = x_k - \gamma \, \nabla f(x_k) .

A coordinate step changes a single index i. If L_i is a Lipschitz constant of the partial derivative in that coordinate, the exact minimizer of the one-dimensional quadratic upper model is

x_i \leftarrow x_i - \frac{1}{L_i} \partial_i f(x) .

The other coordinates stay put. Cyclic order walks i = 1, \ldots, d and then repeats. Randomized order draws i with probabilities proportional to L_i, as in Nesterov (2012).

Theorem 1.2 — Gap of order 1/k

Assume f is convex and L-smooth, and set \gamma = 1/L. Then for every k \ge 1

f(x_k) - f^\star \le \frac{L \, \| x_0 - x^\star \|^2}{2k} .

Proof. Write g = \nabla f(x) and x_+ = x - g/L. Convexity gives \langle g, x - x^\star \rangle \ge f(x) - f^\star. Expand the squared distance to x^\star.

\| x_+ - x^\star \|^2 \le \| x - x^\star \|^2 - \frac{2}{L} \big( f(x) - f^\star \big) + \frac{1}{L^2} \| g \|^2 .

Proposition 0.2 with this step yields f(x) - f(x_+) \ge \| g \|^2 / (2L), so the last term is at most (2/L)\, (f(x) - f(x_+)). The two middle terms simplify to

\| x_+ - x^\star \|^2 \le \| x - x^\star \|^2 - \frac{2}{L} \big( f(x_+) - f^\star \big) .

The same descent inequality shows that f(x_k) is nonincreasing. Sum the distance inequality from step 0 through step k-1. Each function gap in the sum is at least the gap at time k. The distances telescope.

∎

Theorem 1.3 — Geometric gap

Assume in addition that f is \mu-strongly convex, and keep \gamma = 1/L. Then

f(x_k) - f^\star \le \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k \big( f(x_0) - f^\star \big) .

Proof. Proposition 0.3 gives \| \nabla f(x) \|^2 \ge 2\mu \, (f(x) - f^\star). The descent lemma with step 1/L gives a drop of at least \| \nabla f(x) \|^2 / (2L). Combine the two bounds.

f(x) - f(x_+) \ge \frac{\mu}{L} \big( f(x) - f^\star \big) .

Move f(x_+) to one side and f^\star to the other. The gap is multiplied by 1 - \mu/L at every iteration.

∎

The contraction factor is 1 - 1/\kappa. A condition number of 100 keeps 99 percent of the gap at every step, so a million-fold reduction takes on the order of 1400 iterations. Nesterov (2004) records the optimal step 2/(L+\mu). This page keeps \gamma = 1/L so that every theorem uses the same legal step.

Example 1.4 — Ten steps on a random quadratic

The matrix below is A^\top A + I with entries drawn from numpy.random.default_rng(0). The objective is \tfrac12 x^\top A x - b^\top x. The script prints L, \mu, \kappa, the gap at the origin, and the gap after ten gradient steps of length 1/L.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
A = rng.normal(size=(4, 4))
A = A.T @ A + numpy.eye(4)
b = rng.normal(size=4)
x_star = numpy.linalg.solve(A, b)
f_star = float(0.5 * x_star @ A @ x_star - b @ x_star)
evals = numpy.linalg.eigvalsh(A)
L = float(evals.max())
mu = float(evals.min())
x = numpy.zeros(4)
for _ in range(10):
    x = x - (A @ x - b) / L
gap = float(0.5 * x @ A @ x - b @ x - f_star)
print(round(L, 3), round(mu, 3), round(L / mu, 3))
print(round(-f_star, 6), round(gap, 6))

The printout is 10.210 1.101 9.271 and then 0.332883 0.011724. Theorem 1.2 only promises a gap below L \|x^\star\|^2 / 20. The observed gap is smaller, which is normal for a quadratic. The bound has to cover every convex L-smooth function, not only this one.

Coordinate descent is the large-scale variant. Nesterov (2012) proves an expected gap of order 1/k. The constant in that bound is the sum of the coordinate Lipschitz constants, not the dimension times the global smoothness constant. When a few coordinates are much stiffer than the others, the randomized method wins.

For the quadratic of Example 1.4 the diagonal entries of A are legal coordinate constants. A step longer than 2/L can diverge, as Example 0.4 shows on a line. Backtracking is legal whenever the accepted step obeys the descent inequality in the proof of Theorem 1.2.

Key — Two rates

\frac{L R^2}{2k} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^k .

The first multiplies a squared distance R^2 and needs only convexity. The second multiplies the initial gap and needs strong convexity.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • With step 1/L, convex L-smooth objectives have gap at most L \|x_0 - x^\star\|^2 / (2k).
  • Strong convexity replaces that bound by a factor (1 - \mu/L) per iteration.
  • A coordinate step uses one partial derivative. The relevant constants are the L_i, not only the global L.
  • The step 2/L is not safe. On a pure quadratic of curvature L it oscillates.

2. Proximal maps

Gradient descent needs a gradient. A penalty such as \lambda \| x \|_1 does not have one at the coordinate axes, and that penalty is exactly what makes a solution sparse. The proximal map replaces the gradient step for the nonsmooth piece. It is a minimization problem with a quadratic anchor, and for the usual penalties the minimizer is closed form.

Throughout, the nonsmooth piece is convex, proper and lower semicontinuous, and it may take the value +\infty. That value is how a hard constraint enters. The indicator of a closed convex set is zero on the set and +\infty outside.

Definition 2.1 — Proximal map and subgradient

The proximal map of f at v is

\mathrm{prox}_f(v) = \arg\min_x \left( f(x) + \frac12 \| x - v \|^2 \right) .

The quadratic is strongly convex, so the minimizer exists and is unique. A vector g is a subgradient of f at x, written g \in \partial f(x), when

f(y) \ge f(x) + \langle g, y - x \rangle \quad \text{for every } y .

If f is differentiable, the only subgradient is \nabla f(x).

The factor in front of the square is 1/2, with no extra step-size parameter. A step size \gamma is absorbed by applying the map to \gamma f rather than to f. Parikh and Boyd (2014) use that scaling. Bauschke and Combettes (2011) develop the same object as the resolvent of the subdifferential.

Proposition 2.2 — A sufficient subgradient test

If v - x \in \partial f(x), then x = \mathrm{prox}_f(v).

Proof. The subgradient inequality gives f(y) \ge f(x) + \langle v - x, y - x \rangle. Add \tfrac12 \| y - v \|^2 to both sides. The right-hand side equals f(x) + \tfrac12 \| x - v \|^2 + \tfrac12 \| y - x \|^2, which is at least the prox objective at x.

Thus x minimizes the prox objective. Uniqueness was already in Definition 2.1.

∎

For the absolute value, the subgradient is elementary. At t \neq 0, \partial (\lambda |t|) = \{ \lambda \,\mathrm{sign}(t) \}. At 0, it is the interval [-\lambda, \lambda]. Both claims are the definition of a subgradient applied to \lambda |t|, and they take one line of cases.

Example 2.3 — Soft-threshold and a box

Let f(x) = \lambda \| x \|_1 with \lambda = 1/2. The map acts coordinate by coordinate. The scalar rule is \mathrm{sign}(v)\, \max(|v| - \lambda, 0). At v = (1.2,\ -0.3,\ 0.7) the output is (0.7,\ 0,\ 0.2).

Check the test of Proposition 2.2 on the first coordinate. The input 1.2 minus the output 0.7 equals 0.5, which is \lambda times the sign of the output.

On the second coordinate the output is 0 and the absolute value of the input is at most \lambda, so the input lies in the subgradient of \lambda |t| at zero. The third coordinate matches the first.

The same test recovers projections. If f = \iota_C, the prox is the Euclidean projection onto C. For a coordinate box, the projection clips each coordinate to the interval. Clipping is the prox of the indicator. It is not the prox of the absolute value. The absolute value shrinks. The box cuts.

Parikh and Boyd (2014) prove that every proximal map is firmly nonexpansive. The scalar soft-threshold is the case one can check by hand, because its slope is either 0 or 1.

The proximal-point iteration is x_{k+1} = \mathrm{prox}_{\gamma f}(x_k). For convex f the values fall at rate 1/k, in the same family as Theorem 1.2. Alone, the iteration is rarely cheaper than minimizing f. It becomes useful when f is a sum and only one piece has a prox. That split is ch. 3.

Key — Soft-threshold

\big( \mathrm{prox}_{\lambda \| \cdot \|_1}(v) \big)_i = \mathrm{sign}(v_i)\, \max(|v_i| - \lambda,\ 0) .

The threshold equals the coefficient in front of the absolute value, because the quadratic in Definition 2.1 has coefficient 1/2.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The proximal map minimizes f plus a squared distance to the input. The minimizer is unique.
  • If v - x is a subgradient of f at x, then x is the prox of f at v.
  • The prox of \lambda \| \cdot \|_1 is soft-thresholding. The prox of an indicator is a projection.
  • Soft-thresholding does not increase distances. General firm nonexpansiveness is in Parikh and Boyd (2014).

3. Forward-backward splitting and acceleration

Many objectives split as a smooth convex piece plus a convex piece that has a prox. Least squares plus a lasso penalty is the model case. A gradient step on the sum is illegal when the second piece is not differentiable. A proximal-point step on the sum is legal and usually impossible in closed form. Forward-backward uses each piece in the way that piece allows.

Combettes and Pesquet (2011) survey the splitting methods this chapter starts from. The goal here is one algorithm with a proof and one acceleration with a cited rate.

Definition 3.1 — Forward-backward step

With step \gamma \in (0, 1/L],

x_{k+1} = \mathrm{prox}_{\gamma g}\big( x_k - \gamma \, \nabla f(x_k) \big) .

The inner map is the prox of Definition 2.1 applied to \gamma times the nonsmooth piece. For an absolute-value penalty the threshold is the product of \gamma and the penalty weight, not the weight alone. If the nonsmooth piece is zero, the iteration is gradient descent.

Theorem 3.2 — The 1/k rate survives a prox

Assume the smooth piece is convex and L-smooth, the other piece is convex, and the step is 1/L. Let F be their sum and let x^\star minimize F. Then for every k \ge 1

F(x_k) - F(x^\star) \le \frac{L \, \| x_0 - x^\star \|^2}{2k} .

Proof. Beck and Teboulle (2009, Theorem 3.1) prove this bound. The argument copies Theorem 1.2 after one substitution. The descent lemma still upper-bounds f at the new point. The subgradient inequality for g, in the sufficient form of Proposition 2.2, replaces the inner-product lower bound that convexity of f gave for free.

The algebra then produces the same telescoping sum of squared distances. Monotonicity of the values of F lets the last gap out of the sum, and the factor k appears in the denominator.

∎

FISTA, introduced by Beck and Teboulle (2009), keeps one gradient and one prox per iteration and adds a momentum extrapolation. The 1/k constant above matches plain gradient descent.

Definition 3.3 — FISTA

Set y_1 = x_0 and t_1 = 1. For each k \ge 1,

x_k = \mathrm{prox}_{\gamma g}\big( y_k - \gamma \, \nabla f(y_k) \big) ,

t_{k+1} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 t_k^2}}{2} ,

y_{k+1} = x_k + \frac{t_k - 1}{t_{k+1}} (x_k - x_{k-1}) ,

with the convention x_{-1} = x_0 so that the first momentum term vanishes. The step \gamma equals 1/L. Beck and Teboulle (2009) prove

F(x_k) - F^\star \le \frac{2 L \, \| x_0 - x^\star \|^2}{(k+1)^2} .

The sequence t_k grows like k/2. The momentum coefficient tends to 1. Early iterations barely extrapolate. Later iterations take a long step along the last displacement, and that long step is what replaces a 1/k guarantee by a 1/k^2 guarantee.

The proof is a Lyapunov argument in Beck and Teboulle (2009). The displayed bound is the working statement. When strong convexity is known, Nesterov (2004) replaces the growing momentum by a constant that depends on \sqrt{\mu/L}.

Example 3.4 — ISTA and FISTA on a small lasso

Draw a 40 \times 20 matrix with numpy.random.default_rng(0), plant four nonzero coefficients, and add noise of scale 0.05. The objective is half the squared residual plus 0.05\, \| x \|_1. The Lipschitz constant is the largest eigenvalue of A^\top A. The script prints the objective of plain forward-backward and of FISTA after 10 iterations, then after 40.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
m, d = 40, 20
A = rng.normal(size=(m, d)) / numpy.sqrt(m)
x_true = numpy.zeros(d)
x_true[:4] = rng.normal(size=4)
y = A @ x_true + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=m)
lam = 0.05
L = float(numpy.linalg.eigvalsh(A.T @ A).max())

def soft(v, t):
    return numpy.sign(v) * numpy.maximum(numpy.abs(v) - t, 0.0)

def objective(x):
    resid = A @ x - y
    return float(0.5 * numpy.sum(resid ** 2) + lam * numpy.sum(numpy.abs(x)))

def run(n_iter, accelerated):
    x = numpy.zeros(d)
    z = x.copy()
    t = 1.0
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        grad = A.T @ (A @ z - y)
        x_new = soft(z - grad / L, lam / L)
        if not accelerated:
            x = x_new
            z = x_new
            continue
        t_new = (1.0 + numpy.sqrt(1.0 + 4.0 * t * t)) / 2.0
        z = x_new + ((t - 1.0) / t_new) * (x_new - x)
        x, t = x_new, t_new
    return objective(x)

print(round(run(10, False), 6))
print(round(run(10, True), 6))
print(round(run(40, False), 6))
print(round(run(40, True), 6))

The four printed numbers are 0.104451, 0.098065, 0.097997 and 0.097997. After ten steps FISTA is ahead. By forty steps both have reached the same objective on this tiny instance, at six digits. Acceleration is a finite-time gain. On a larger condition number the 1/k^2 bound pulls away for much longer.

Key — One gradient, one prox

x_+ = \mathrm{prox}_{\gamma g}(x - \gamma \nabla f(x)), \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{L} .

FISTA wraps this map in the extrapolation of Definition 3.3. The prox threshold scales with \gamma.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Forward-backward takes a gradient step on the smooth piece and a prox step on the rest.
  • With \gamma = 1/L the gap on f+g is still at most L \|x_0 - x^\star\|^2 / (2k).
  • FISTA uses the same two oracles plus momentum and has a gap of order 1/k^2.
  • On the seeded lasso, the objectives after ten steps are 0.104451 and 0.098065.

4. Majorization-minimization

A proximal step needs a prox. Sometimes neither piece of the objective has one, but a tangent still lies above the graph. Majorization-minimization builds that tangent, or any other upper model that touches the current point, and minimizes the model instead of the objective. Hunter and Lange (2004) is the short tutorial. The official syllabus names the method, so this chapter stays with convex and differentiable examples where the decrease is a theorem rather than a hope.

Definition 4.1 — Surrogate

A surrogate of f at x_k is a function x \mapsto g(x \mid x_k) such that

g(x_k \mid x_k) = f(x_k) \qquad \text{and} \qquad g(x \mid x_k) \ge f(x) \ \text{for every}\ x .

The algorithm sets x_{k+1} to a minimizer of the surrogate. If several minimizers exist, any one of them is legal. The next surrogate is built at x_{k+1}, not at x_k.

The descent lemma is the special case where the surrogate is the quadratic upper model and its minimizer is the gradient step of length 1/L. Majorization does not invent a new idea. It allows a model that is tighter than the global quadratic, provided the model is still easy.

Theorem 4.2 — The objective decreases

If x_{k+1} minimizes the surrogate at x_k, then f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k). If f is continuous and the only point where a surrogate can touch f with zero slope is a stationary point of f, every limit point of the iteration is stationary. The second claim is the content of Hunter and Lange (2004). The first claim is proved here.

Proof. Minimizing the surrogate gives g(x_{k+1} \mid x_k) \le g(x_k \mid x_k). The touch condition says the right-hand side equals f(x_k). The majorization inequality says f(x_{k+1}) \le g(x_{k+1} \mid x_k). Chain the three relations.

∎

Decrease is not the rate of Theorem 1.2. That rate belongs to the quadratic surrogate whose curvature is L, not to an arbitrary majorizer.

One systematic construction uses composition. Let the objective be a concave increasing function of a convex nonnegative map. The tangent of the outer function lies above it. Composing that tangent with the inner map yields a surrogate. If the slope of the tangent is positive, minimizing the surrogate is a weighted copy of the inner map.

For the outer function \log(1+u) the slope at the current value u_k is 1/(1+u_k). When the inner map is a square residual, the surrogate is a weighted square. Several residuals produce one weighted least-squares problem, and that linear system is the iteration.

The EM algorithm is this pattern with a latent variable. Computational Statistics develops that case. The tangent above needs no latent variable.

Example 4.3 — Five majorization steps

Six rows in \mathbb{R}^2 and a planted vector (1,\ -1/2) are drawn with numpy.random.default_rng(0), with noise of scale 0.1 on the linear predictions. The loss sums \log(1+r_i^2) over the six residuals. Each step solves a weighted least-squares problem of size 2 \times 2.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
a = rng.normal(size=(6, 2))
planted = numpy.array([1.0, -0.5])
bvec = a @ planted + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=6)
x = numpy.zeros(2)

def loss(x):
    r = a @ x - bvec
    return float(numpy.sum(numpy.log1p(r ** 2)))

def step(x):
    r = a @ x - bvec
    w = 1.0 / (1.0 + r ** 2)
    W = numpy.diag(w)
    return numpy.linalg.solve(a.T @ W @ a, a.T @ W @ bvec)

print(round(loss(x), 6))
x = step(x)
print(round(loss(x), 6))
for _ in range(4):
    x = step(x)
print(round(loss(x), 6))
print(numpy.round(x, 4))

The script prints 1.659414, then 0.079459, then 0.077695, then the vector [1.0006,\ -0.5077]. The loss falls, as Theorem 4.2 requires. The vector sits next to the planted point, so the majorizer is not stalling far from the data.

Key — Touch and lie above

A legal surrogate equals the objective at the current point and lies above it everywhere else. Minimizing it cannot raise the objective.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • A surrogate touches f at the current point and stays above f. The next point minimizes the surrogate.
  • The descent lemma is the surrogate whose curvature is the global smoothness constant.
  • A concave increasing outer function contributes a tangent weight. Squares then become weighted least squares.
  • On the seeded example the loss falls from 1.659414 to 0.077695 in five steps.

5. Parallel block methods

A gradient step moves every coordinate with information from the same vector. Several cores can do better when the objective splits into blocks, if each core is told which coordinates it owns. The danger is the coupling. A core that uses a stale value of another block is solving a slightly wrong problem. This chapter compares the sequential update with the simultaneous one, then records when a lock-free overwrite is harmless.

Definition 5.1 — Gauss-Seidel and Jacobi updates

Split the variable into blocks. A Gauss-Seidel cycle minimizes the objective in one block at a time and uses the fresh blocks inside the same cycle. A Jacobi cycle minimizes every block against the vector from the start of the cycle, and it writes the new blocks only at the end. The Jacobi cycle is parallel. The Gauss-Seidel cycle is sequential.

An exact block update that sees the current vector cannot raise the objective. A Jacobi step can, because each block ignores the updates of the others.

Proposition 5.2 — One cycle of a coupled quadratic

Let f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 + xy. Start at (1, 1), where the value is 3.

One exact Gauss-Seidel cycle updates x and then y. It finishes at (-1/2,\ 1/4) with value 0.1875. One exact Jacobi step finishes at (-1/2,\ -1/2) with value 0.75.

Proof. The partial derivative in x is 2x + y. Setting it to zero with y held fixed gives x = -y/2.

The partial derivative in y is 2y + x. Setting it to zero with x held fixed gives y = -x/2.

Gauss-Seidel at (1,\ 1) sets x = -1/2 and then y = 1/4. The value is 1/4 + 1/16 - 1/8 = 3/16.

Jacobi uses the old coordinates for both updates, so both new coordinates equal -1/2. The value is 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 3/4.

∎

Both steps descend. The parallel step descends less, because each block still sees the partner at 1 rather than at the updated value. If the cross term were larger, a Jacobi step on this pattern could increase the objective even though each block problem is solved exactly. Parallelism is not free. It needs either a smaller step or a coupling that is weak relative to the block curvature.

Example 5.3 — When cores do not collide

Niu, Recht, Re and Wright (2011) study lock-free stochastic gradient steps. Each core reads x, computes a sparse gradient, and writes the updated coordinates without a lock. If two cores touch disjoint coordinates, the pair of writes is a Jacobi step on those coordinates and the arithmetic matches a sequential pair. If they write the same coordinate, one update is lost.

The hypothesis is sparsity. Lost updates are a small perturbation only when collisions are rare. Dense gradients need a different argument.

Chouzenoux, Pesquet and Repetti (2016) take another parallel route. They update one block at a time, but the block is chosen freely and the quadratic model uses a variable metric, not only the global smoothness constant. The prox of the nonsmooth piece stays inside the block. That paper is a proximal version of coordinate descent, aimed at imaging problems where different blocks have very different curvatures.

Key — Fresh blocks versus stale blocks

Gauss-Seidel inserts a new block before the next block problem. Jacobi freezes every block at the start of the cycle. Only the first rule is guaranteed not to raise a coupled objective.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • An exact block update that sees the latest other blocks cannot raise the objective.
  • On x^2 + y^2 + xy, one Gauss-Seidel cycle reaches value 0.1875 and one Jacobi step reaches 0.75.
  • Lock-free parallel steps are justified when gradients are sparse enough that writes rarely collide.
  • A variable metric inside a block, as in Chouzenoux, Pesquet and Repetti (2016), adapts the step to that block.

6. Consensus and alternating directions

Parallel blocks in ch. 5 still share one memory. A distributed problem gives each machine its own copy of the variable and its own piece of the objective. The copies must agree. Consensus is the constraint that forces the agreement, and the alternating direction method is the algorithm that enforces it with a quadratic penalty and a dual update. Boyd, Parikh, Chu, Peleato and Eckstein (2011) is the working reference.

Definition 6.1 — Scaled consensus ADMM

Machine i owns a convex function f_i and a copy x_i. A shared vector z is the consensus value. With penalty \rho > 0 and scaled dual vectors u_i, one iteration does three updates. Each machine solves

x_i \leftarrow \arg\min_x \left( f_i(x) + \frac{\rho}{2} \| x - z + u_i \|^2 \right) .

The machines may solve these problems at the same time. Then

z \leftarrow \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i + u_i), \qquad u_i \leftarrow u_i + x_i - z .

The primal residual on machine i is x_i - z. Agreement means every residual is zero.

The x-update is a prox of f_i. For least squares it is a linear system with \rho on the diagonal. Machines exchange copies, not data rows.

Proposition 6.2 — The z update is an average

Given the copies and the scaled duals, the z formula in Definition 6.1 is the exact minimizer of the terms in the augmented Lagrangian that depend on z.

Proof. Those terms are \sum_i (\rho/2)\, \| x_i - z + u_i \|^2. Expand each square as \| z - (x_i + u_i) \|^2. The sum of squared distances to fixed points is minimized at the average of those points. Differentiate in z if a derivative is preferred. The gradient is \rho \sum_i (z - x_i - u_i), and it vanishes at the same average.

∎

If every f_i is closed, convex and proper, and a saddle point exists, residuals go to zero. Eckstein and Bertsekas (1992) prove this by identifying ADMM with Douglas-Rachford splitting. This page proves only the z step.

A large penalty \rho forces the copies together and stiffens the local updates. A small penalty postpones agreement. Boyd and coauthors (2011) adapt \rho from the ratio of the two residuals. That rule is a heuristic, not a rate.

Example 6.3 — Two least-squares machines

Two machines each hold five noisy-free rows in dimension 2, drawn with numpy.random.default_rng(0). The planted vector is (1.5,\ -0.7). The penalty is \rho = 1, and every vector starts at zero. The script prints the consensus loss at the start, then the loss and the distance between the two copies after 5 and 15 iterations.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
A1 = rng.normal(size=(5, 2))
A2 = rng.normal(size=(5, 2))
xstar = numpy.array([1.5, -0.7])
y1 = A1 @ xstar
y2 = A2 @ xstar
rho = 1.0
x1 = numpy.zeros(2)
x2 = numpy.zeros(2)
z = numpy.zeros(2)
u1 = numpy.zeros(2)
u2 = numpy.zeros(2)

def sloss(z):
    r1 = A1 @ z - y1
    r2 = A2 @ z - y2
    return float(0.5 * numpy.sum(r1 ** 2) + 0.5 * numpy.sum(r2 ** 2))

print(f"{sloss(z):.6f}")
for k in range(1, 16):
    x1 = numpy.linalg.solve(A1.T @ A1 + rho * numpy.eye(2), A1.T @ y1 + rho * (z - u1))
    x2 = numpy.linalg.solve(A2.T @ A2 + rho * numpy.eye(2), A2.T @ y2 + rho * (z - u2))
    z = 0.5 * (x1 + u1 + x2 + u2)
    u1 = u1 + x1 - z
    u2 = u2 + x2 - z
    if k in (5, 15):
        gap = float(numpy.linalg.norm(x1 - x2))
        print(k, f"{sloss(z):.6f}", round(gap, 6))

The prints are 8.968319, then 5 0.000770 0.06007, then 15 0.000038 0.008744. Five iterations already put the consensus loss below 10^{-3}. The copies are still 0.06007 apart. By iteration 15 that distance is 0.008744 and the loss is 0.000038. The planted vector is (1.5,\ -0.7). The algorithm is approaching it, and the residual test of Boyd and coauthors would accept the iterate once the distance falls under a chosen tolerance.

ADMM does not need a central gradient. It needs a central average of the copies, or any reduction that computes the same average. ch. 7 removes that central average and lets neighboring machines mix their copies on a graph.

Key — Residual

Stop when every x_i - z is small and the dual step u_i has also settled. A small primal residual means the copies agree. A small dual step means the agreement is not still sliding.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Consensus gives each machine a copy and forces the copies to share one value z.
  • The local update is a prox of the local function. The z update is the average of x_i + u_i.
  • On the seeded two-machine least squares problem, the copy gap falls from 0.06007 at iteration 5 to 0.008744 at iteration 15.
  • Global residual convergence is the Douglas-Rachford theorem of Eckstein and Bertsekas (1992).

7. Optimization over a network

ADMM in ch. 6 averages every copy in one place. On a network a machine talks only to its neighbors, and that average becomes multiplication by a mixing matrix. A constant step can leave the copies near the optimum without putting them on top of each other.

Definition 7.1 — Mixing matrix and distributed gradient

Let W be a symmetric matrix with nonnegative entries, supported on the edges of an undirected graph, and doubly stochastic. Each row sums to 1, so a multiplication by W replaces every copy by a weighted average of its neighbors.

Each machine i has a convex local cost f_i. One step of distributed gradient descent reads

x_i \leftarrow \sum_j W_{ij} x_j - \gamma \, \nabla f_i(x_i) .

The gradient is local. The mix uses only neighbors. Nedić and Ozdaglar (2009) study the subgradient version of this recursion with diminishing steps.

On a smooth strongly convex sum the copies converge, but the limit need not be a consensual minimizer. The next proposition computes that limit on a path.

Proposition 7.2 — Constant-step bias on a path

Take three nodes on a path. Each local cost is a squared distance to a target, and the targets are 0, 1 and 3. The mixing matrix is the Metropolis matrix of the path.

With step 1/5, every fixed point has mean 4/3, the mean of the targets. The fixed point itself is not a constant vector.

Proof. A fixed point repeats under the distributed gradient update. Rearrangement gives the linear system

(I - W + \gamma I)\, x = \gamma c .

The all-ones vector is a left eigenvector of W with eigenvalue 1, because W is doubly stochastic. Multiply the fixed-point equation by that row. The disagreement terms vanish, and the two means agree.

The linear system is invertible for a positive step, so there is one fixed point. A constant vector is a fixed point only when it equals the target vector, and the three targets differ. The outer coordinates of the solution differ by 9/8.

∎

Diminishing steps remove the bias. If the steps are positive, square-summable and not summable, and if the subgradients are bounded, Nedić and Ozdaglar (2009) obtain a common minimizer. The step 1/k is the usual choice.

EXTRA, from Shi, Ling, Wu and Yin (2015), keeps a constant step and corrects it with a difference of successive gradients. Under their smoothness assumptions the copies meet at a minimizer of the sum, linearly when the objective is strongly convex.

Scaman, Bach, Bubeck, Lee and Massoulié (2017) prove a lower bound when each round exchanges one exact vector with neighbors. The round count grows like the square root of an objective condition number times a graph condition number. A path is a bad graph for that bound. A complete graph is close to a central average.

Example 7.3 — Thirty steps and the fixed point

The script runs Definition 7.1 for 30 steps from the origin with \gamma = 0.2, then solves the fixed-point system of Proposition 7.2. It prints the mean and the spread of the three copies, first for the iteration and then for the fixed point.

import numpy

W = numpy.array([
    [2.0 / 3.0, 1.0 / 3.0, 0.0],
    [1.0 / 3.0, 1.0 / 3.0, 1.0 / 3.0],
    [0.0, 1.0 / 3.0, 2.0 / 3.0],
])
gamma = 0.2
c = numpy.array([0.0, 1.0, 3.0])
x = numpy.zeros(3)
for _ in range(30):
    x = W @ x - gamma * (x - c)
print(round(float(x.mean()), 4), round(float(x.max() - x.min()), 4))
M = numpy.eye(3) - W + gamma * numpy.eye(3)
xf = numpy.linalg.solve(M, gamma * c)
print(round(float(xf.mean()), 4), round(float(xf.max() - xf.min()), 4))

The iteration prints 1.3317 1.125. The fixed point prints 1.3333 1.125. The mean is already near 4/3, and the spread has locked at 1.125. Further constant steps do not bring the copies together.

The print 1.125 is the fraction 9/8 from the proposition. Exercise 10 asks for the three coordinates.

Key — Bias of a constant step

On a network, x = W x - \gamma \nabla F(x) is not the optimality condition of the sum unless W x = x. A constant step can make the gradient term balance a nonzero disagreement x - W x.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • A doubly stochastic mixing matrix replaces the central average by neighbor exchanges.
  • With a constant step, the fixed point of distributed gradient descent on a path need not be consensual. Here the spread stays 9/8.
  • Steps of size 1/k, under the hypotheses of Nedić and Ozdaglar (2009), restore exact consensus.
  • EXTRA removes the bias at a constant step. The lower bound of Scaman and coauthors (2017) ties the rate to the graph.

8. Research directions and open problems

The in-class chapters stop at convex objectives, exact arithmetic, and a fixed undirected graph. The papers below keep those hypotheses or drop one of them. Each paragraph says what the paper proves and which hypothesis a reader would have to restore before quoting the theorem on a new problem.

Work by Shi, Ling, Wu and Yin (2015) shows that a gradient correction gives exact linear convergence for smooth strongly convex consensus on a fixed undirected graph. The argument uses a constant mixing matrix. It does not cover a directed graph that changes at every round. Nedić and Ozdaglar (2009) allow a broader class of convex costs, but only with diminishing steps, and their rate is not linear. A direction still tied to those two papers is a linear rate for a time-varying directed graph when only the sum of the local costs is strongly convex.

Work by Scaman, Bach, Bubeck, Lee and Massoulié (2017) shows a lower bound on communication rounds for smooth strongly convex objectives when neighbors exchange exact vectors. The bound is silent about quantized messages.

Alistarh, Grubic, Li, Tomioka and Vojnovic (2017) quantize a centralized stochastic gradient. Their QSGD scheme controls the variance added by the quantizer. It is not a network algorithm. Koloskova, Stich and Jaggi (2019) put compression inside decentralized gossip and prove a rate for compressed communication. The 2017 lower bound does not by itself price a quantized message.

Work by Lian, Zhang, Zhang, Hsieh, Zhang and Liu (2017) compares decentralized parallel stochastic gradient descent with a centralized algorithm on a cluster. The decentralized method can win when the communication topology is cheap relative to the gradient cost. The comparison is experimental and theoretical for smooth nonconvex objectives of a kind that appears in deep networks. It is outside the convex contract of the official syllabus. The convex theory of ch. 7 does not predict their speedup, and their speedup does not refute the convex lower bound, because the objectives differ.

Work by Chouzenoux, Pesquet and Repetti (2016) gives a block-coordinate variable-metric forward-backward method with a proved decrease for convex composite objectives. The blocks are chosen one at a time from a single memory. An asynchronous version, with delays that are unbounded but square-summable, is a natural question next to the lock-free model of Niu, Recht, Re and Wright (2011). Those two papers do not analyze each other’s iterates. Hogwild! is stochastic and sparse. The block-coordinate method is deterministic and proximal. Joining the delay model to the variable metric is a precise reading project, not a theorem of either paper.

Two further limitations are worth stating plainly. None of the rates above survives an arbitrary nonconvex constraint. And a lower bound that assumes one vector exchanged per round says nothing about a machine that can send a single bit. Both limits are reasons to keep the hypotheses visible when the algorithms of ch. 6 and ch. 7 are reused on a new architecture.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • EXTRA and the 2009 subgradient method fix different hypotheses. Neither statement covers a directed time-varying graph with a linear rate under strong convexity of the sum alone.
  • Quantized communication sits between the lower bound of Scaman and coauthors and the compression rates of Koloskova, Stich and Jaggi.
  • Decentralized stochastic gradients can beat a central algorithm on nonconvex network training. That comparison is not a convex rate.
  • Asynchronous delays and variable-metric block updates are analyzed in different papers. Combining them is a reading project.

Exercises

  1. ★ Eigenvalues of a diagonal quadratic. Let f(u, v) = (u^2 + 4 v^2)/2. Find \mu, L and \kappa. Show that the descent lemma is an equality along the second axis, starting from the origin.

    Solution. The Hessian is diagonal with entries 1 and 4, so the constants are \mu = 1, L = 4 and \kappa = 4.

    Along the second axis the function equals twice the square of that coordinate. The quadratic upper model at the origin, using this smoothness constant, is the same expression. The two sides match.

  2. ★ Ten gradient steps, in Python. Build the quadratic of Example 1.4 and run ten gradient steps of length 1/L from the origin. The fence must be the one below.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
A = rng.normal(size=(4, 4))
A = A.T @ A + numpy.eye(4)
b = rng.normal(size=4)
x_star = numpy.linalg.solve(A, b)
f_star = float(0.5 * x_star @ A @ x_star - b @ x_star)
L = float(numpy.linalg.eigvalsh(A).max())
x = numpy.zeros(4)
for _ in range(10):
    x = x - (A @ x - b) / L
gap = float(0.5 * x @ A @ x - b @ x - f_star)
print(round(gap, 6))
*Solution.* The fence prints 0.011724. Theorem 1.2 gives a larger upper bound, because it has to cover every convex smooth function with the same $L$ and the same distance to a minimizer.

  1. ★ The step that flips. Let f(t) = (L/2)\, t^2 with L > 0 and start at t_0 \neq 0. Compute one gradient step with length 1/L and one with length 2/L.

    Solution. The gradient is L t. The safe step subtracts t and lands on 0. The step of length 2/L subtracts 2 t and lands on -t_0. A second step of that length returns to t_0.

  2. ★ How many steps the convex bound asks for. Theorem 1.2 is used with L = 10 and \|x_0 - x^\star\| = 2. How large must k be before the bound guarantees a gap of at most 0.001?

    Solution. The bound is L R^2 / (2k) = 20 / k. Ask for 20 / k \le 0.001, hence k \ge 20000. The bound is sufficient. A quadratic can be faster, as in Exercise 2.

  3. ★★ Soft-threshold by hand. Compute the prox of \lambda \| \cdot \|_1 at (1.2,\ -0.3,\ 0.7) for \lambda = 1/2, in the scaling of Definition 2.1. Check Proposition 2.2 on each coordinate.

    Solution. The output is (0.7,\ 0,\ 0.2). On the first coordinate, 1.2 - 0.7 = 1/2, the right subgradient of the penalty. On the second, the output is 0 and |-0.3| \le 1/2. The third coordinate repeats the first check with input 0.7 and output 0.2.

  4. ★★ FISTA against forward-backward, in Python. Run ten steps of each method on the lasso of Example 3.4. Use the fence below, which calls numpy.random.default_rng(0).

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
m, d = 40, 20
A = rng.normal(size=(m, d)) / numpy.sqrt(m)
x_true = numpy.zeros(d)
x_true[:4] = rng.normal(size=4)
y = A @ x_true + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=m)
lam = 0.05
L = float(numpy.linalg.eigvalsh(A.T @ A).max())

def soft(v, t):
    return numpy.sign(v) * numpy.maximum(numpy.abs(v) - t, 0.0)

def objective(x):
    resid = A @ x - y
    return float(0.5 * numpy.sum(resid ** 2) + lam * numpy.sum(numpy.abs(x)))

def run(n_iter, accelerated):
    x = numpy.zeros(d)
    z = x.copy()
    t = 1.0
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        grad = A.T @ (A @ z - y)
        x_new = soft(z - grad / L, lam / L)
        if not accelerated:
            x = x_new
            z = x_new
            continue
        t_new = (1.0 + numpy.sqrt(1.0 + 4.0 * t * t)) / 2.0
        z = x_new + ((t - 1.0) / t_new) * (x_new - x)
        x, t = x_new, t_new
    return objective(x)

print(round(run(10, False), 6))
print(round(run(10, True), 6))
*Solution.* The fence prints 0.104451 and then 0.098065. Forward-backward is the first number. FISTA is the second. Both use the same step and the same threshold. Only the momentum differs.

  1. ★★ One tangent of a logarithm. Let f(t) = \log(1 + t^2). At t_0 = 1, write the quadratic surrogate coming from the tangent of \log(1+u) in the variable u = t^2. Minimize that surrogate.

    Solution. At residual square 1, the tangent of \log(1+u) has slope 1/2. The surrogate is that tangent composed with t^2.

    Its minimizer is t = 0. At the expansion point t = 1 the surrogate matches the objective. At 0 the surrogate equals \log 2 - 1/2, which is above the objective value 0.

  2. ★★ Gauss-Seidel against Jacobi. For f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 + xy, start at (1,\ 1). Compute one exact Gauss-Seidel cycle and one exact Jacobi step, and evaluate f at both new points.

    Solution. Proposition 5.2 gives the point with coordinates -1/2 and 1/4, value 0.1875, and the point with both coordinates -1/2, value 0.75.

    The start has value 3. Both steps descend. The sequential step uses the updated first coordinate inside the second block, which is why its value is smaller.

  3. ★★★ One ADMM step on two scalars. Minimize the sum of two scalar squares, centered at 1 and at 3, subject to both variables equaling a common consensus value. Take penalty \rho = 1 and start from zero. Compute the first two copies and the new consensus value.

    Solution. Stationarity in the first copy gives x = 1/2. Stationarity in the second copy gives y = 3/2.

    The consensus value is the average of the two copies, so z = 1. The copies still differ by 1. The optimum is the average of the two centers, which is 2, so one iteration has not finished.

  4. ★★★ Fixed point on the path. Using Proposition 7.2, show that every fixed point of constant-step distributed gradient descent has the same mean as c = (0,\ 1,\ 3). Then solve (I - W + \gamma I)\, x = \gamma c with \gamma = 1/5 and report the three coordinates and the spread.

    Solution. Double stochasticity kills the disagreement term when the fixed-point equation is summed, so the mean of x equals 4/3. The unique solution is (115/144,\ 23/18,\ 277/144). The spread between the last and the first coordinate is 9/8 = 1.125, which is the second number printed by Example 7.3.

Written exam (proposal)

Two hours, closed book except for one handwritten sheet. Three questions. A calculator is unnecessary. This format is a proposal for the announced 2 h exam. The official pages do not describe the questions.

  1. Conceptual question (6 points). Constant-step distributed gradient descent on a connected network can drive the average of the copies toward a minimizer while the copies themselves stay apart. Explain why, using the fixed-point relation in ch. 7. Then say, in a few sentences, what EXTRA changes.

    Expected: The fixed point balances a mixing disagreement against a local gradient step. On the quadratic path the mean matches the optimum and the spread does not vanish. EXTRA adds a gradient correction so that consensual minimizers are the fixed points. Cite Shi, Ling, Wu and Yin (2015) as the source of the correction.

  2. Computation (7 points). Compute the prox of half the absolute-value norm at (1.2,\ -0.3,\ 0.7), in the scaling of ch. 2.

    Then take f(t) = 2 t^2 at t = 1. Compute one gradient step of length 1/L and one of length 2/L. Finally compute the first ADMM update of Exercise 9 from a cold start.

    Expected: The prox is (0.7,\ 0,\ 0.2). Here L = 4, so the safe step reaches 0 and the long step reaches -1. ADMM returns the copies 1/2 and 3/2, and the consensus value 1.

  3. Short proof (7 points). Assume f is differentiable and L-smooth. Prove the descent lemma. Deduce that a gradient step of length 1/L drops the objective by at least \| \nabla f(x) \|^2 / (2L).

    Expected: Write the increment as an integral of the derivative, subtract the tangent, and bound the integrand by the Lipschitz constant. The integral of t on [0,\ 1] produces the factor 1/2. Substitute the safe step into that upper model.

The points sum to 20.
