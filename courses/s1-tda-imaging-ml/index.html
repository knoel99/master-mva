0. Review: distances, simplices, and covers

An image and a point cloud do not arrive as spaces whose holes one can count. This chapter turns both into finite complexes. Homology in ch. 1 is linear algebra on those complexes, and persistence in ch. 2 is the same algebra along a nested family.

Let P be a finite set of points in Euclidean space. The geometry we keep is the matrix of pairwise distances. Growing a radius produces a filtration, a nested family of complexes. Every barcode in these notes is the homology of some filtration.

Definition 0.1 — Complexes from a point cloud An abstract simplicial complex on a finite vertex set is a family of subsets, called simplices, such that every subset of a simplex is a simplex. The dimension of a simplex is one less than its number of vertices.

Fix a radius r at least zero. The Čech complex at that radius contains a finite subset of P when the closed balls of radius r centered at its points have a point in common. The Vietoris-Rips complex at threshold r contains the subset when every pairwise distance inside it is at most r. These notes use that pairwise threshold, not the convention that writes it as a diameter.

Proposition 0.2 — Same edges, fewer triangles The Čech complex at radius r sits inside the Rips complex at threshold 2r. In Euclidean space the two complexes have the same edges. A Rips triangle need not be a Čech triangle.

Proof. If balls of radius r meet at one point, any two centers are at most 2r apart. Every Čech simplex is therefore a Rips simplex at threshold 2r.

An edge of length at most 2r has a midpoint within r of each end, so the edges match. Three balls can meet in pairs and still miss a common point. Rips fills a triangle as soon as the three edges exist. Čech waits until the circumradius falls to r. ∎

Theorem 0.3 — Nerve of the balls Let a finite family of closed convex sets sit in Euclidean space. The nerve, whose simplices are the subfamilies with nonempty intersection, is homotopy-equivalent to the union of the sets.

Proof. Closed balls are convex, so the theorem applies to the Čech cover. The union of the balls and the Čech complex have the same homology. Edelsbrunner and Harer (2010) give the classical argument. The step used here is convexity. ∎

Example 0.4 — Corners of the unit square The four corners of the unit square have side length 1 and diagonal about 1.414.

At radius 0.5 the four sides are present and the diagonals are absent. Any three corners form a right triangle whose circumradius is about 0.707, so no triangle is filled. The complex is a 4-cycle. At radius 0.707 the center of the square lies in every ball, and the complex becomes a filled tetrahedron, which is contractible.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A simplicial complex is a downward-closed family of finite sets. Dimension is one less than the number of vertices.
  • Čech records a common point of balls. Rips records cliques. They share edges when the Rips threshold is twice the Čech radius.
  • The nerve theorem moves homology from the union of the balls onto the finite Čech complex.
  • On the unit square, radius 0.5 gives a 4-cycle and radius 0.707 fills the tetrahedron.

1. Simplicial homology

Betti numbers count components, loops, and voids. This chapter defines them as dimensions of spaces of chains, over the field with two elements. That field matches the usual imaging convention, and it removes every sign from the boundary.

Definition 1.1 — Chains, boundary, and homology Let the chain space in dimension k have as basis the simplices of that dimension, with coefficients in the field with two elements. The boundary of a simplex is the sum of its faces of dimension one lower. A cycle is a chain whose boundary is zero. A boundary chain is the boundary of some chain of dimension one higher.

Homology in dimension k is the quotient of the cycles by the boundary chains. The Betti number in that dimension is the dimension of the quotient. In dimension zero it equals the number of connected components.

Theorem 1.2 — Boundary of a boundary The boundary of a boundary is the zero chain. In symbols, the composition of two successive boundary maps is zero.

Proof. Test a single simplex of dimension at least 1. A face obtained by deleting two vertices, call them v and w, lies in exactly two faces of codimension one. One of those faces keeps v and drops w. The other keeps w and drops v.

Over the field with two elements those two faces sum to zero. Every such face cancels, so the double boundary is zero. The image of the next boundary therefore sits inside the cycles, and the quotient in Definition 1.1 is defined. ∎

The Euler characteristic is the alternating sum of the numbers of cells. It must agree with the alternating sum of Betti numbers.

Proposition 1.3 — Euler-Poincaré The alternating sum of the numbers of simplices equals the alternating sum of the Betti numbers.

Proof. Write n for the number of simplices in a fixed dimension, z for the dimension of the kernel of the boundary, and b for the dimension of the image of the boundary one step higher. The Betti number is z minus b. Rank-nullity says n equals z plus the dimension of the image of the boundary going down, which is the previous b.

Substitute and alternate the sign. The image dimensions cancel in pairs, one with a plus and the next with a minus. The two alternating sums are equal. ∎

Example 1.4 — Empty triangle and filled triangle Three vertices and three edges, and no face, have Euler characteristic 0. The complex is connected and has one independent loop, so the Betti numbers are 1 and 1. The alternating sum is 0.

Add the triangular face. The characteristic becomes 1. The loop is now a boundary, so the first Betti number drops to 0 while the zeroth stays 1. Both counts agree with Proposition 1.3.

A grayscale picture is a grid. Pixels are vertices, shared sides are edges, and unit squares are faces. The boundary of a square is the sum of its four edges, and the double boundary vanishes as in Theorem 1.2. Fewer than four cells per pixel keep the matrix linear in the size of the picture, before the reduction in ch. 2.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Over the field with two elements, the boundary of a simplex is the sum of its faces, and the boundary of a boundary is zero.
  • A Betti number is the dimension of cycles modulo boundaries. The zeroth one counts components.
  • The alternating sum of the cell counts equals the alternating sum of the Betti numbers.
  • A pixel grid is a cubical complex with fewer than four cells per pixel.

2. Persistent homology

One radius is a guess. Persistent homology runs homology at every radius and records when each class appears and when it disappears. The output is a barcode. Long bars are the features one keeps. Short bars are the candidates for noise, judged later by the stability theorem in ch. 4.

On a point cloud the input is the Rips or Čech filtration of ch. 0. On an image a cell enters at the largest value among its vertices. The algebra is the same in both cases.

Definition 2.1 — Barcode A class is born at the index where it first appears in homology, and it dies at the later index where it becomes a boundary. The barcode is the multiset of those intervals. An infinite interval is a class still alive in the final complex.

For components, the death rule is short. When two components merge, the one born later dies, and the older birth continues. That is the elder rule. It is the dimension-zero case of the general pairing.

Theorem 2.2 — Infinite bars count components Filter a graph by adding vertices and edges. The number of infinite bars in dimension zero equals the number of connected components of the final graph.

Proof. Each new vertex starts a component, which is a birth. An edge either closes a loop inside one component or merges two components. A merge reduces the component count by one and ends exactly one bar, the bar of the younger component.

Components never split along an inclusion filtration. At the end, the number of components equals the number of births minus the number of merges. Births that never merge are the infinite bars. ∎

Higher-dimensional bars come from the boundary matrix, with columns ordered by the filtration. Add earlier columns over the field with two elements until the lowest 1 is unique or the column is zero. A zero column is a birth. A lowest 1 pairs a death with the birth in that row.

Zomorodian and Carlsson (2005) prove that a finite filtration decomposes uniquely into interval summands. The pairs above are one reading of that decomposition. The reduction costs cubic time in the number of simplices in the worst case, which is why the cubical bound of ch. 1 matters for images.

Example 2.3 — Five heights on a path Place vertices a, b, c, d, e in that order, with heights 0, 6, 1, 3.2, and 2. A vertex enters at its height. An edge enters at the maximum of its endpoints, just after that higher vertex.

The bars one keeps are an infinite bar born at height 0, a bar from 1 to 6, and a bar from 2 to 3.2. Bars of length zero sit on the local maxima and are discarded. At height 3.2 the younger component dies into the older one.

Oudot (2015) treats a barcode as a complete invariant of a one-parameter module. Two parameters at once break that completeness, which ch. 8 takes up.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • A bar runs from the scale where a class is born to the scale where it becomes a boundary.
  • When two components merge, the younger bar dies. Infinite bars in dimension zero are the final components.
  • Matrix reduction pairs each death column with the birth row of its lowest 1.
  • On the five-point path the bars one keeps run from 0 onward, from 1 to 6, and from 2 to 3.2.

3. Smooth and discrete Morse theory

Persistent homology sees every cell of a filtration. Morse theory compresses a scalar field down to its critical points. On a terrain those points are pits, passes, and peaks. Milnor (1963) is the smooth reference. Forman (1998) is the discrete theory used on grids. Fomenko and Kunii (1997) draw the same pictures for visualization.

Definition 3.1 — Morse data, smooth and discrete A smooth function on a compact manifold is Morse when every critical point has an invertible Hessian. The index is the number of negative eigenvalues. Index zero is a local minimum. The top index is a local maximum. Intermediate indices are saddles.

A discrete vector field pairs some simplices so that each pair is a simplex together with one face of codimension one, and each simplex lies in at most one pair. A gradient has no closed path that alternates between a pair and a different face of the larger simplex. Unpaired simplices are critical.

Proposition 3.2 — Extrema alternate on a circle A smooth function on a circle, with nondegenerate critical points, has equally many minima and maxima.

Proof. Nondegenerate critical points are isolated, so there are finitely many. On each open arc between them the derivative does not vanish, so the function is strictly monotone there.

After a minimum the function increases, and the next critical point is a maximum. After a maximum it decreases, and the next critical point is a minimum. Walking once around the circle, the two types alternate. The loop closes only if the counts match. ∎

Theorem 3.3 — Morse inequalities Suppose a space is homotopy-equivalent to a cell complex with a given number of cells in each dimension. Then each Betti number is at most the number of cells in that dimension, and the alternating sums agree.

A Morse function produces such a complex, with one cell per critical point of a given index. That existence statement is Milnor (1963). The inequality itself is Proposition 1.3 applied to the cells, because a Betti number cannot exceed the number of cells in its dimension. Forman (1998) gives the discrete analogue. Cancelling a free face, a face that belongs to only one larger simplex, deletes a pair and leaves the Betti numbers unchanged, by the same pivot that clears a single 1 in the boundary matrix.

Repeated cancellation of close pairs leaves the critical cells and simplifies the field before the segmentation in ch. 5.

Example 3.4 — Sphere and torus Height on a round sphere has one minimum and one maximum. Theorem 3.3 says each of those Betti numbers is at most 1, and the alternating sum equals 2. The sphere meets the bounds.

An upright torus has one minimum, two saddles, and one maximum. Its Betti numbers are 1, 2, and 1, so the inequalities are equalities. A small bump adds one minimum and one saddle. The new cells contribute zero to the alternating sum, and the inequalities still hold.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • The index of a nondegenerate critical point is the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian.
  • On a circle, minima and maxima alternate, so the two counts are equal.
  • With one cell per critical point, each Betti number is at most the corresponding count, and the Euler characteristic matches.
  • Cancelling a free face and its unique coface does not change Betti numbers.

4. Persistence diagrams and stability

A barcode is a picture of intervals. A persistence diagram is the same information as points in the plane. Birth is the horizontal coordinate and death is the vertical one. Points far from the diagonal are long bars. Points near the diagonal are short bars. Stability says that a small change of the data moves these points by a small amount.

Definition 4.1 — Diagram and bottleneck distance The diagram of a filtration is the multiset of points with death strictly above birth, one point per finite bar, together with the diagonal. The bottleneck distance is the infimum, over partial matchings, of the largest displacement in the max-norm. Unmatched points go to the diagonal. The max-norm distance from a point to the diagonal equals half its persistence.

A short bar can be deleted at a cost equal to half its length. A long bar is expensive to delete. A threshold on persistence is therefore a statement about bottleneck distance, not only a picture.

For tame functions on a triangulable space, Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer (2007) prove that the bottleneck distance between the diagrams is at most the sup-norm distance between the functions.

Theorem 4.2 — Sublevel sets interleave If two functions differ by at most epsilon everywhere, the sublevel set of the first at height t sits inside the sublevel set of the second at height t plus epsilon. The symmetric inclusion holds with the functions swapped.

Proof. Take a point where the first function is at most t. The second function there is at most t plus epsilon, so the point lies in the later sublevel set of the second function. The other inclusion is the same argument with the roles reversed.

The two persistence modules are therefore interleaved by epsilon. The algebraic stability theorem of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer (2007) turns an interleaving into the bottleneck bound in the key box. These notes prove the geometric step and cite the algebraic step. ∎

The comparison is in the sup norm. A tall thin spike is large in that norm even when its integral is small.

Example 4.3 — A bar that cannot jump Use the path of Example 2.3. One finite bar is the point with birth 2 and death 3.2. Its persistence is 1.2, so its distance to the diagonal is 0.6.

If a second function differs by at most 0.1 everywhere, every diagram point moves by at most 0.1 in the max-norm. The point may go to birth 2.1 and death 3.1. It may not go to birth 2 and death 4, because that displacement is 0.8. Deleting the deep bar, from 1 to 6, would cost 2.5. A tenth of a unit of noise cannot do it.

Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer, and Mileyko (2010) prove a Wasserstein variant under a Lipschitz hypothesis. Images in these notes use the bottleneck case, because a uniform gray-level error is a sup-norm bound.

Comparing two shapes is a matching problem in this plane, which is the input of ch. 7.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • A diagram is a multiset of points above the diagonal. Distance to the diagonal is half the persistence.
  • The bottleneck distance is the cheapest worst-case matching, and the diagonal absorbs short bars.
  • A sup-norm gap of epsilon between two functions yields an interleaving of sublevel sets, hence bottleneck distance at most epsilon.
  • On the five-point path, a gap of 0.1 cannot move the point (2, 3.2) out to death 4.

5. Reeb graphs, mapper, and Morse-Smale complexes

Bars forget how features sit in the domain. Reeb graphs, mapper, and Morse-Smale complexes put the features back on the space. They are the tools the course description points at for level sets and integral lines. Pascucci, Tricoche, Hagen, and Tierny collect the visualization side in the volume listed on master-mva.com.

Definition 5.1 — Reeb graph Let a function send a space to the real line. Declare two points equivalent when they share a value and lie in the same connected component of that level set. The Reeb graph is the quotient by this relation.

For a Morse function the quotient is a graph whose vertices sit at critical values. It records the merges that dimension-zero persistence sees.

Proposition 5.2 — Height on a circle Height on a round circle, with one minimum and one maximum, has a Reeb graph homeomorphic to a circle.

Proof. Below the minimum the level set is empty. Between the two critical values it consists of two points. Above the maximum it is empty again.

Each of those points sweeps an arc from the minimum to the maximum. The quotient identifies nothing across the arcs except the endpoints. Two arcs glued at both ends form a circle. ∎

Mapper, from Singh, Mémoli, and Carlsson (2007), builds a graph in the same spirit when the space is known only through samples. Choose a filter from the sample to the line. Cover the range by overlapping intervals. Cluster the preimage of each interval. Each cluster is a vertex. Draw an edge when two clusters share a sample point.

Overlap is required. Disjoint intervals give clusters that cannot share points, so the rule draws no edge from the cover. The output also depends on the clustering method. Change one parameter and see which loops survive.

Definition 5.3 — Morse-Smale regions in the plane Assume the function is Morse-Smale, so stable and unstable manifolds meet transversely. Integral lines of the gradient join saddles to minima and saddles to maxima. In the plane those separatrices cut the domain into regions, each running from one maximum down to one minimum through two saddles.

Along the negative gradient the function decreases, from peaks toward pits. Cancelling a short bar deletes a shallow pit and its saddle, and the neighboring regions merge. Edelsbrunner, Letscher, and Zomorodian (2002) introduced persistence with this simplification in view.

Example 5.4 — Two pits Let pit A sit at height 0 and merge at a saddle of height 0.4, so its persistence is 0.4. Let pit B sit at height 0.2 and merge at height 2.5, so its persistence is 2.3.

A threshold of 1 cancels A and keeps B. The segmentation loses the shallow basin and keeps the deep one. Deleting the shallow point costs 0.2 in bottleneck distance, half of 0.4. Any sup-norm perturbation smaller than 0.2 cannot swap the roles of the two basins.

Height on an upright torus has first Betti number 2 on the surface and only 1 on the Reeb graph. One loop is invisible to this height. A second filter is the multiparameter problem of ch. 8.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • The Reeb graph quotients the domain by connected components of level sets.
  • Mapper replaces those components by clusters on a cover, and joins clusters that share points.
  • In the plane, Morse-Smale separatrices cut the domain into regions from a maximum down to a minimum.
  • A pit at height 0 that merges at height 0.4 is cancelled by any persistence threshold above 0.4.

6. Topological inference in high dimension

If samples lie near an unknown shape, which features of the shape survive in the sample? Chazal’s handbook chapter, as listed on master-mva.com, is the high-dimensional reference. Niyogi, Smale, and Weinberger (2008) give a sampling theorem. These notes prove the offset estimate and cite them for the reach.

Definition 6.1 — Offset, Hausdorff distance, and reach The offset at radius r is the set of points at distance at most r from a closed set. The Hausdorff distance is the smallest epsilon such that each set sits in the offset of the other. The reach, in the sense of Federer (1959), is the largest tau such that every closer point has a unique nearest point on the set.

A circle of radius 1 has reach 1, because the center is the first point with two nearest points. A sharp corner falls outside the smooth hypothesis, so pixel masks use ch. 5.

Proposition 6.2 — Offsets interleave If the Hausdorff distance between two closed sets is at most epsilon, then the offset of the first at radius r sits inside the offset of the second at radius r plus epsilon, which sits inside the offset of the first at radius r plus twice epsilon.

Proof. Take a point within r of the first set. Some point of that set is at most r away, and some point of the second set is at most epsilon from there. The triangle inequality puts the original point within r plus epsilon of the second set.

Swap the two sets and apply the same step once more. The second inclusion follows, with a shift of twice epsilon. ∎

Theorem 6.3 — Homology from a dense sample Let the unknown shape be a compact smooth submanifold of positive reach. Niyogi, Smale, and Weinberger (2008) prove that if a finite sample is close enough in the Hausdorff distance, relative to the reach, then a nonempty open interval of radii has a Čech complex with the homology of the shape.

Proof. Proposition 6.2 places sample offsets between offsets of the shape. When the gap is small relative to the reach, an offset of the shape retracts onto the shape, and Theorem 0.3 identifies the Čech complex with the union of the balls. The cited paper gives the interval of radii. ∎

Chazal, Guibas, Oudot, and Skraba (2013) rank clusters by the dimension-zero persistence of a density. Modes are births of superlevel sets, and a cluster is the basin of a mode that survives a chosen threshold. They call the algorithm ToMATo.

de Silva and Carlsson (2004) use landmarks and a witness complex. Sheehy (2013) approximates the Rips filtration at linear size. Either one is the practical companion of Theorem 6.3.

Example 6.4 — A circle of reach 1 The unit circle has reach 1. A sample at Hausdorff distance 0.05 has offsets interleaved with the circle by Proposition 6.2. A tube of radius 0.05 is thin beside a hole of radius 1, so a mid-range radius should see one component and one loop, once the gap passes the test in Niyogi, Smale, and Weinberger (2008).

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • The reach is the largest radius at which nearest points stay unique. A unit circle has reach 1.
  • Hausdorff distance at most epsilon interleaves offsets with a shift of epsilon.
  • A sample that is close, relative to the reach, has a Čech complex with the homology of the manifold on a range of radii.
  • ToMATo ranks clusters by the dimension-zero persistence of a density.

7. Topological signatures and implementations

A diagram has a changing number of points. A classifier expects one vector of fixed length. This chapter builds those vectors from the stability theorem in ch. 4, then names the two libraries on master-mva.com.

A Betti curve records a Betti number against the scale. Bubenik (2015) stacks tent functions into a persistence landscape, in a space where means exist. Adams and coauthors (2017) build a persistence image by integrating Gaussian bumps on a grid in birth-persistence coordinates.

Definition 7.1 — Tent and landscape The tent of a point is zero outside the interval from birth to death, and inside it equals the distance to the nearer endpoint. The landscape of order k is the k-th largest tent. Its peak equals half the persistence.

Proposition 7.2 — Tents inherit the bottleneck bound Points at most epsilon apart in the max-norm have tents that differ by at most epsilon. Diagrams at bottleneck distance epsilon therefore have landscapes at sup-norm distance epsilon.

Proof. Moving birth or death by at most epsilon moves each of the two linear pieces of the tent by at most epsilon. The minimum of two numbers is 1-Lipschitz for that displacement, and so is the positive part. The tent therefore moves by at most epsilon.

A bottleneck matching of cost epsilon matches points, and leftovers go to the diagonal. A diagonal point has tent zero. A point within epsilon of the diagonal has tent height at most epsilon. Matched tents differ by at most epsilon everywhere.

Paired functions that differ by at most epsilon have k-th order statistics that differ by at most epsilon. Apply that fact to the tents. ∎

Adams and coauthors (2017) prove that persistence images are stable for a Wasserstein distance. Fix the grid and the Gaussian width before looking at test labels.

Carrière, Cuturi, and Oudot (2017) define a kernel by slicing diagrams and comparing the one-dimensional projections. A kernel method can then take diagrams directly, with no intermediate grid.

Carrière, Chazal, Ike, Lacombe, Royer, and Umeda (2020) define PersLay, a layer that applies a learned function at each diagram point and sums the values. The pairing jumps on a thin set, so ch. 8 treats the gradient as an open point.

Example 7.3 — Three bars at time 1 The bars run from 0.2 to 1, from 0.4 to 1.5, and from 1.2 to 1.8. The fence evaluates the first landscape at time 1.

import numpy
bars = numpy.array([[0.2, 1.0], [0.4, 1.5], [1.2, 1.8]])
t = 1.0
tents = [max(0.0, min(t - b, d - t)) for b, d in bars]
print(round(max(tents), 4))

The fence prints 0.5. At time 1 only the middle bar contributes, with height 0.5. Proposition 7.2 moves that value by at most the bottleneck distance.

GUDHI, described by Maria, Boissonnat, Glisse, and Yvinec (2014), is the C++ library with a Python module named on master-mva.com for complexes and persistence. The Topology ToolKit of Tierny, Favelier, Levine, Gueunet, and Michaux (2018) computes Morse-Smale complexes and persistence simplification on images, and it connects to ParaView. The fences here stay in NumPy. A large complex should call one of those two libraries.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Landscapes and persistence images turn a diagram of variable size into a vector a classifier can use.
  • A point that moves by at most epsilon in the max-norm moves its tent by at most epsilon, and landscapes inherit the bottleneck bound.
  • PersLay learns a pointwise function and a symmetric aggregation. The pairing is not smooth on every input.
  • GUDHI targets complexes and persistence. The Topology ToolKit targets scalar fields on images. The three-bar fence prints 0.5.

8. Research directions and open problems

The eight sessions stop at a working pipeline, from a complex to a stable vector. Several questions that the pipeline raises are not settled. The research seminar of 3 hours is the official place to present one of them. The remarks below stay with papers that state a precise gap. They are reading directions, not a survey of every group in the field.

Several parameters at once

A grayscale image has one filter. A color image, or a point cloud with a density and a geometric function, has two. Carlsson and Zomorodian (2009) show that a persistence module over a product of two real lines does not decompose into intervals the way a one-parameter module does. The rank invariant, which records the rank of each map from one scale to a later scale, is computable and incomplete. Two modules can share the rank invariant and differ as modules.

Work by Lesnick and Wright built software to explore the rank invariant. The open problem for imaging is narrower than the general classification. One wants a discrete invariant that stays stable under a sup-norm change of each filter, and that still separates the modules coming from two-channel pictures. Completeness on all modules is more than a seminar can settle. A stable invariant on a restricted class, for example modules of dimension 0, is a concrete target.

Gradients through a pairing

Hofer, Kwitt, Niethammer, and Uhl (2017) use topological signatures inside a deep network. PersLay, already cited in ch. 7, learns the weight on each diagram point. On a fixed pairing the birth and death times are min and max of vertex values along simplices, and those operations have one-sided derivatives. The pairing itself changes when two columns compete for the same lowest 1. That jump set is thin, so a gradient exists almost everywhere. It is still a jump. A training run can cross it.

The open problem is to regularize the topological term so that the optimization uses a gradient one can bound, without giving up Theorem 4.2. Replacing the diagram by the landscape of Definition 7.1 restores a Lipschitz map, by Proposition 7.2, and loses some of the flexibility PersLay was built to learn. Measuring that tradeoff on one synthetic family of diagrams is a seminar-sized experiment.

Confidence sets

Fasy, Lecci, Rinaldo, Wasserman, Balakrishnan, and Singh (2014) construct confidence sets for persistence diagrams of random samples. Bubenik (2015) uses the Banach space structure of landscapes to build confidence bands by standard mean estimation. Both results assume a sampling model. A pixel grid with quantized gray levels is not that model. The noise is closer to a bounded sup-norm error, which Theorem 4.2 already controls, or to a sensor model with dependence between neighboring pixels.

The open problem is a confidence set that uses the grid and the quantization rather than an independent sample at each pixel. One can still bootstrap blocks of pixels and report the result as a heuristic. Calling it a theorem requires a proof these notes do not contain.

Scale on volumes

Sheehy (2013) gives a linear-size approximation of the Vietoris-Rips filtration, with a multiplicative control on the scales. Cubical persistence, as implemented in the Topology ToolKit, is already linear in the number of voxels and does not need that approximation. The Morse-Smale complex of a large volume is more demanding. Separatrices stored naively can touch a positive fraction of the voxels.

The open problem is a certified simplification that stores only critical cells and a controlled geometric error on the separatrices. Persistence cancellation from ch. 5 removes pairs. It does not by itself bound the Hausdorff distance between the original separatrix and the simplified one. An experiment can measure that distance on a modest volume. A bound for all volumes is open.

Which diagrams are realizable

Theorem 3.3 forbids some diagrams for a scalar field on a sphere. At least \beta_k critical points of index k are required, so a height function on a sphere cannot have an empty set of extrema. On a fixed cubical grid the constraints are stricter and combinatorial. Not every multiset of bars is the H_0 barcode of a function with values in a given set of gray levels.

Characterizing the realizable diagrams on one grid is a finite check in principle and a large check in practice. A seminar can take a 4 \times 4 grid, enumerate the distinct lower-star barcodes up to the elder rule, and compare the list with the Morse inequalities. Extending the enumeration to a clinical volume is not the same problem.

The common thread is stability. Each direction above is interesting only if the new invariant, gradient, band, or certificate still moves continuously when the input moves a little in the sup norm. That is the constraint Theorem 4.2 puts on every extension.

Exercises

  1. ★ A 3-4-5 rectangle. Four points form a rectangle of sides 3 and 4. The diagonal has length 5. List the simplices of the Čech complex at radius 2. Say whether any triangle is filled, and describe the 1-skeleton.

Solution. An edge of length L enters when the radius is at least half of L. At radius 2 both side lengths qualify, since half of 3 is 1.5 and half of 4 is 2, while the diagonal needs 2.5. Any three corners form a right triangle of circumradius 2.5, so those balls do not meet. The complex is a 4-cycle.

  1. ★ Edges of a noisy circle. Twenty-four points sit at the roots of unity on the unit circle, then move by Gaussian noise of scale 0.02. Count the undirected edges of the Rips graph at threshold 0.55.
import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
theta = numpy.linspace(0, 2 * numpy.pi, 24, endpoint=False)
pts = numpy.column_stack((numpy.cos(theta), numpy.sin(theta)))
pts = pts + 0.02 * rng.normal(size=pts.shape)
diff = pts[:, None, :] - pts[None, :, :]
dist = numpy.sqrt(numpy.sum(diff * diff, axis=-1))
numpy.fill_diagonal(dist, numpy.inf)
n_edges = int(numpy.sum(dist <= 0.55) // 2)
print(n_edges)

Solution. The fence prints 47. Neighbors on the exact circle are about 0.26 apart, so the threshold 0.55 keeps a few neighbors of each vertex. The complete graph would have 276 edges.

  1. ★ Betti numbers of a triangle. Over the field with two elements, take three vertices and three edges. Compute the zeroth and first Betti numbers. Add the triangular face and compute them again. Check both answers with the Euler characteristic.

Solution. Without the face the complex is connected and has one loop, so the Betti numbers are 1 and 1. The characteristic 3 minus 3 equals 0. With the face the loop is a boundary, so the first Betti number is 0 and the zeroth stays 1. The characteristic 3 minus 3 plus 1 equals 1.

  1. ★ Two squares on a grid. Two unit squares share an edge. The cubical complex has 6 vertices, 7 edges, and 2 squares. Compute the Euler characteristic. If the patch is a disk, what are \beta_0 and \beta_1?

Solution. The characteristic is 6 minus 7 plus 2, hence 1. A disk is connected and has no loop, so the zeroth Betti number is 1 and the first is 0. That matches Proposition 1.3.

  1. ★★ Pairs on a square cycle. Order the simplices a, b, c, d, ab, bc, cd, da. Reduce the boundary matrix over \mathbb{F}_2. List the persistence pairs and the infinite bars.

Solution. The four vertex columns are zero. Edge ab pairs with b, edge bc pairs with c, and edge cd pairs with d. Edge da starts as the sum of d and a. Adding cd, then bc, then ab clears that column, so da is a birth. The infinite bars are the component of a and the loop born at da. Both Betti numbers equal 1.

  1. ★★ Extrema on a sampled circle. Sample f(\theta) = \sin \theta + 0.3 \sin 3\theta at 64 equally spaced angles and add Gaussian noise of scale 0.05. Count strict local minima and strict local maxima in the circular array.
import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
n = 64
theta = numpy.linspace(0, 2 * numpy.pi, n, endpoint=False)
h = numpy.sin(theta) + 0.3 * numpy.sin(3 * theta)
h = h + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=n)
less = (h < numpy.roll(h, 1)) & (h < numpy.roll(h, -1))
more = (h > numpy.roll(h, 1)) & (h > numpy.roll(h, -1))
print(int(less.sum()))
print(int(more.sum()))

Solution. The fence prints 10 and then 10. Proposition 3.2 says minima and maxima alternate on a circle. A circular array with no equal neighbors has the same equality, because a strict local minimum is followed by a strict local maximum as one walks around the loop.

  1. ★★ A bottleneck matching. Let D contain (0.2, 1.0) and (0.4, 1.5). Let D' contain (0.3, 1.1) and (0.5, 1.2). Compute the bottleneck distance, using the diagonal when it helps.

Solution. Match (0.2, 1.0) with (0.3, 1.1) at cost 0.1, and (0.4, 1.5) with (0.5, 1.2) at cost 0.3. The crossed matching costs 0.4. Sending (0.4, 1.5) to the diagonal costs 0.55. The best worst cost is 0.3.

  1. ★★ Reeb graph of an upright torus. The height function has one minimum, two saddles, and one maximum. Level sets pass from one circle to two circles and back to one. Describe the Reeb graph and its first Betti number, and compare with the torus.

Solution. The graph has a pending edge from the minimum to the lower saddle, two arcs between the saddles, and a pending edge up to the maximum. Four vertices and four edges give characteristic 0, so the first Betti number is 1. The torus has first Betti number 2. This height sees only one of the two loops.

  1. ★★★ A sampling check on the unit circle. The unit circle has reach 1. Design a finite sample whose Hausdorff gap to the circle is about 0.05, and state a numerical success criterion for Theorem 6.3 that uses only quantities you can compute or look up.

Solution. Take 80 angles and add radial noise so that the Hausdorff gap stays about 0.05.

That gap must sit below the fraction of the reach in Niyogi, Smale, and Weinberger (2008). At one radius in their interval the complex has a single component, and the longest loop bar is several times the next one. Union-find checks the components. The loop bar needs a persistence library.

  1. ★★★ Landscapes at the stability limit. Using the diagrams of Exercise 7, evaluate the first landscape of each diagram at t = 1.05. Compare the gap with the bottleneck distance 0.3. Say what a threshold rule at this t can and cannot claim.

Solution. At time 1.05 the first diagram has tent values 0 and 0.45, so the first landscape equals 0.45. The second has tent values 0.05 and 0.15, so its first landscape equals 0.15.

The gap is 0.30, equal to the bottleneck distance from Exercise 7. A threshold between those two heights separates the diagrams. Proposition 7.2 says a further perturbation of size 0.3 can close the gap.

Written exam (proposal)

One hour, no documents, three questions, 20 points. This paper is a study proposal beside the official short lesson exam. It does not replace the research seminar, and it has no official weight.

  1. Basins and a gray shift (6 points). An image has two bars in dimension zero, of lengths 4 and 0.2. Say what each bar is in the picture. If every gray value moves by at most 0.05, what does stability give? Expected: The long bar is a deep basin, from a pit to the saddle that merges it. The short bar is a shallow ripple. A sup-norm change of 0.05 moves the diagram by at most 0.05. Half the length is the distance to the diagonal, hence 2 and 0.1. Both bars stay off the diagonal.

  2. Triangle reduction (7 points). Over the field with two elements, order vertices a, b, c and then edges ab, bc, and ca. Reduce the boundary matrix, then add the face abc. Expected: Edge ab pairs with b, and edge bc pairs with c. Edge ca reduces to zero. Before the face both Betti numbers equal 1. The face pairs with ca and kills the loop, so the first Betti number drops to 0.

  3. Boundary of a boundary (7 points). Let sigma be a simplex of dimension at least 2, over the field with two elements. Prove that the boundary of its boundary is zero. Expected: A face of codimension 2 comes from deleting two vertices, in either order, so it lies in exactly two faces of codimension 1. In characteristic 2 those two copies sum to zero.
