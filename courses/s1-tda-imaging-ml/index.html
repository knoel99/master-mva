Audience: a student with an M1 in mathematics, computer science or physics who knows linear algebra and some Python, and who wants to measure the shape of data — its components, loops and cavities — in images, scalar fields and point clouds, robustly to noise.

Empirical first: a 64 × 64 image of three Gaussian bumps plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.05 has 721 local maxima. Persistence gives the two secondary bumps the values 0.734 and 0.64, and every noise peak at most 0.172 (Code 4.2). One threshold in that gap recovers the three objects, and the segmentation that follows finds the three regions of the clean image (Code 6.1). In the same way, 5 % of outliers cut the persistence of the loop of a sampled circle from 0.80 to 0.44, while the distance to a measure keeps 0.63 (Code 7.1).

Topological data analysis (TDA) describes data by the features that survive continuous deformation: connected components, loops, cavities, and the way they appear and disappear across scales. The course builds these descriptors from scratch — simplicial complexes, homology, Morse theory, persistence — and uses them for scientific visualization in 2D and 3D and for machine learning in high dimension. It shares tools with Geometric Data Analysis, Geometry Processing and Geometric Deep Learning and Optimal Transport for Machine Learning, whose Wasserstein distances reappear between persistence diagrams.

What you will be able to do

  • Classify the critical points of a piecewise-linear scalar field and check the result against the Euler characteristic.
  • Compute persistence diagrams with the reduction algorithm and with union-find, and compare them with the bottleneck and Wasserstein distances.
  • State and use the stability theorem to separate topological signal from noise and to simplify a scalar field.
  • Compute merge trees, contour trees and Mapper graphs, and relate them to the Reeb graph of a function.
  • Segment an image with the cells of a Morse–Smale complex, simplified by persistence.
  • Infer the homology of a sampled shape with guarantees, and make the inference robust to outliers with the distance to a measure.
  • Cluster data with persistence-based clustering (ToMATo) and choose its parameter from a diagram.
  • Turn persistence diagrams into stable features (landscapes, images, kernels) for classification, and differentiate a topological loss.
  • Choose data structures and algorithms (simplex trees, clearing, cohomology) that make these computations fast, in Python or C++.

0. Review: topology, linear algebra over F2 and union-find

Topological data analysis counts features that survive continuous deformation, and it computes them with linear algebra and graph algorithms. This chapter recalls the three M1 tools the course uses everywhere: equivalences of spaces, linear algebra over the two-element field, and the union-find data structure.

0.1 Spaces up to deformation

Definition 0.1 — Homeomorphism, homotopy, deformation retract Let X, Y be topological spaces.

  • A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection h : X \to Y with a continuous inverse.
  • Two continuous maps f, g : X \to Y are homotopic if there is a continuous H : X \times [0, 1] \to Y with H(\cdot, 0) = f and H(\cdot, 1) = g.
  • X and Y are homotopy equivalent, written X \simeq Y, if there are maps f : X \to Y and g : Y \to X with g \circ f \text{ homotopic to } \mathrm{id}_X \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g \text{ homotopic to } \mathrm{id}_Y .
  • A \subseteq X is a deformation retract of X if there is a homotopy from \mathrm{id}_X to a map with values in A that fixes A at all times. Then A \simeq X.

An annulus deformation retracts onto its middle circle, and a disk onto its centre. A union of small balls around points sampled on a circle is never homeomorphic to the circle, but it can be homotopy equivalent to it. Homotopy equivalence is therefore the right notion for sampled data, and every invariant of this course is a homotopy invariant.

0.2 Distances between sets

Definition 0.2 — Offsets, Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff distances For a compact set K \subset \mathbb{R}^d, the distance function is d_K(x) = \min_{y \in K} \|x - y\| and the offset of radius r is K^r = d_K^{-1}([0, r]) = \bigcup_{y \in K} \bar B(y, r). The Hausdorff distance between compact sets A and B is d_H(A, B) = \inf \{ \varepsilon \geq 0 : A \subseteq B^\varepsilon \text{ and } B \subseteq A^\varepsilon \}. For two compact metric spaces, the Gromov–Hausdorff distance d_{GH} is the infimum of d_H over all isometric embeddings of both spaces into a common metric space.

Proposition 0.3 — Distance functions and the Hausdorff distance For compact sets A, B \subset \mathbb{R}^d, \| d_A - d_B \|_\infty = d_H(A, B).

Proof. Let \varepsilon = d_H(A, B) and x \in \mathbb{R}^d. Take b \in B with \|x - b\| = d_B(x), then a \in A with \|a - b\| \leq \varepsilon.

Then d_A(x) \leq \|x - a\| \leq d_B(x) + \varepsilon. Exchanging A and B gives |d_A(x) - d_B(x)| \leq \varepsilon.

For the converse, let a \in A maximize d_B on A. Then d_A(a) = 0, so the sup-distance is at least \max_A d_B.

Exchanging the roles of the two sets, and noting that the larger of the two maxima is d_H(A, B), concludes. ∎

Every stability result of ch. 4 and ch. 7 starts from this identity: two samples that are close in Hausdorff distance have close distance functions, hence close offsets.

0.3 Linear algebra over the two-element field

Definition 0.4 — Vector spaces over F2 and quotients The field \mathbb{F}_2 = \{0, 1\} has 1 + 1 = 0. A vector of \mathbb{F}_2^n is a subset of \{1, \dots, n\}, and addition is the symmetric difference.

For a subspace W \subseteq V, the quotient V / W identifies v and v' when v - v' \in W. Its dimension is \dim V / W = \dim V - \dim W . For a linear map A : \mathbb{F}_2^n \to \mathbb{F}_2^m, the rank–nullity theorem holds as over \mathbb{R}: \dim \ker A + \operatorname{rank} A = n, and Gaussian elimination computes the rank.

Example 0.5 — A rank that depends on the field Consider A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. Over \mathbb{R}, \det A = 2 and the rank is 3. Over \mathbb{F}_2, the sum of the three columns is zero, and the rank is 2.

Its columns are the three edges of a triangle, written as sets of vertices: ch. 2 reads the relation above as “the boundary of a triangle is a loop”.

0.4 Union-find

Algorithm 0.6 — Union-find (disjoint-set forest) Each element stores a parent pointer; a root is its own parent and represents its set.

  1. find(a) follows the pointers from a to its root, and makes the visited nodes point closer to the root (path compression);
  2. union(a, b) makes the root of one set point to the root of the other.

With union by rank and path compression, m operations on n elements cost O\big(m\, \alpha(n)\big), where \alpha is the inverse Ackermann function, below 5 for any practical n (Tarjan, 1975).

Proposition 0.7 — Union-find computes connected components Process the edges of a graph one by one, calling union on their endpoints. After each step, two vertices have the same root if and only if a path of processed edges joins them.

Proof. By induction on the number of processed edges. Initially every vertex is alone. Processing the edge uv merges the sets of u and v.

Afterwards, x and y are joined by processed edges if and only if they were already joined, or one is joined to u and the other to v. That is exactly the effect of the union on the sets. ∎

In this course, union-find runs on vertices sorted by a function value. The order in which components merge then carries the information: it gives 0-dimensional persistence (ch. 4), merge trees (ch. 5), watershed regions (ch. 6) and clusters (ch. 7).

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Topological invariants are invariant under homotopy equivalence, which is the right notion for thickened samples.
  • The sup-distance between distance functions equals the Hausdorff distance between the sets (Proposition 0.3).
  • Over \mathbb{F}_2, vectors are sets and addition is the symmetric difference; ranks may differ from ranks over \mathbb{R}.
  • Union-find maintains connected components in almost constant time per operation.

1. Simplicial complexes and filtrations

A computer cannot store a circle, but it can store points, edges and triangles. This chapter defines these combinatorial models, builds them from point clouds and images, and nests them along a scale.

1.1 Simplicial complexes

Definition 1.1 — Simplicial complexes A geometric k-simplex is the convex hull of k + 1 affinely independent points of \mathbb{R}^d; the convex hulls of subsets of these points are its faces.

An abstract simplicial complex on a finite vertex set V is a family K of non-empty subsets of V that contains every non-empty subset of each of its elements. An element of K with k + 1 vertices is a k-simplex.

A geometric realization |K| places the vertices in some \mathbb{R}^N so that each \sigma spans a geometric simplex and two simplices meet in a common face or not at all. A complex of dimension k always has one in \mathbb{R}^{2k+1}.

Definition 1.2 — Euler characteristic, star and link If K has n_k simplices of dimension k, its Euler characteristic is \chi(K) = \sum_{k \geq 0} (-1)^k\, n_k . For a vertex v, the star \mathrm{St}(v) is the set of simplices that contain v. The link is \mathrm{Lk}(v) = \{ \tau \in K : v \notin \tau,\ \tau \cup \{v\} \in K \}.

Example 1.3 — Three small complexes

  • The boundary of a tetrahedron has 4 vertices, 6 edges and 4 triangles: \chi = 4 - 6 + 4 = 2, the Euler characteristic of the sphere.
  • On a 3 \times 3 grid with opposite sides identified, cut each square along one diagonal. This gives a torus with 9 vertices, 27 edges and 18 triangles, and \chi = 0.
  • In a triangulated surface, the link of an interior vertex is a cycle of edges, and the link of a boundary vertex is a path.

1.2 Nerves

Definition 1.4 — Nerve of a cover Let \mathcal{U} = (U_i)_{i \in I} be a finite family of sets. Its nerve N(\mathcal{U}) is the abstract simplicial complex on I whose simplices are the non-empty J \subseteq I with \bigcap_{j \in J} U_j \neq \emptyset .

Theorem 1.5 — Nerve theorem Let \mathcal{U} be a finite family of closed convex sets of \mathbb{R}^d. Then |N(\mathcal{U})| \simeq \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i . The same holds for a finite closed cover of a triangulable space in which every non-empty intersection is contractible (a good cover).

Proof. Sketch. Map the barycentric subdivision of the nerve linearly to points chosen in the intersections; convexity keeps the image in the union. Section 10.4 builds the homotopy inverse; Hatcher (2002, Section 4.G) gives the full proof. ∎

1.3 Čech and Vietoris–Rips complexes

Definition 1.6 — Čech and Rips complexes Let X be a finite subset of \mathbb{R}^d and r \geq 0.

  • The Čech complex \check C(X, r) is the nerve of the closed balls \bar B(x, r), x \in X.
  • The Vietoris–Rips complex R(X, r) contains every non-empty \sigma \subseteq X of diameter at most 2r.

The Rips complex makes sense in any metric space. It is a flag complex: a set of vertices is a simplex as soon as all its edges are.

By the nerve theorem, since balls are convex, \check C(X, r) \simeq X^r . The Čech complex models the offset faithfully, but testing whether k + 1 balls intersect needs a smallest enclosing ball; the Rips complex only needs pairwise distances.

Theorem 1.7 — Rips and Čech complexes are interleaved For a finite X \subset \mathbb{R}^d and every r \geq 0, \check C(X, r) \subseteq R(X, r) \subseteq \check C\Big(X, \sqrt{\tfrac{2d}{d+1}}\; r\Big) \subseteq \check C(X, \sqrt{2}\, r). In any metric space, R(X, r) \subseteq \check C(X, 2r), with balls of the ambient space.

Proof. If the balls of radius r around the vertices of \sigma share a point z, then any two vertices are at distance at most 2r through z. Hence \sigma \in R(X, r).

Conversely, let \sigma have diameter \delta \leq 2r. Jung’s theorem (1901) says that a set of diameter \delta in \mathbb{R}^d lies in a ball of radius \delta \sqrt{\frac{d}{2(d+1)}} \;\leq\; r \sqrt{\frac{2d}{d+1}} . The centre of that ball lies in all the balls of this radius around the vertices of \sigma.

In a general metric space, any vertex of \sigma lies in all the balls of radius 2r around the vertices. ∎

Key result — Rips is sandwiched between two Čech complexes \check C(X, r) \subseteq R(X, r) \subseteq \check C(X, \sqrt{2}\, r)

The constant of Jung’s theorem is reached: for the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side 1, the Rips triangle appears at r = 1/2 and the Čech triangle at the circumradius 1/\sqrt 3, a ratio of 1.155 (Exercise 2).

Code 1.1 — Čech and Rips triangles of a sampled circle Thirty points are sampled near the unit circle. For several radii, the code counts the edges and the triangles of both complexes, and checks the inclusion of Theorem 1.7 in the plane.

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 30)                     # 30 points near the unit circle
P = np.c_[np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)] + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=(30, 2))
D = np.linalg.norm(P[:, None] - P[None], axis=2)

def meb_radius(a, b, c):
    """Radius of the smallest disk containing three points of the plane."""
    ab, bc, ca = np.linalg.norm(a - b), np.linalg.norm(b - c), np.linalg.norm(c - a)
    s = sorted([ab, bc, ca])
    if s[2] ** 2 >= s[0] ** 2 + s[1] ** 2:                 # right or obtuse: half the longest side
        return s[2] / 2
    u, v = b - a, c - a
    area = abs(u[0] * v[1] - u[1] * v[0]) / 2
    return ab * bc * ca / (4 * area)                       # acute: circumradius

triples = list(combinations(range(30), 3))
meb = np.array([meb_radius(*P[list(t)]) for t in triples])
diam = np.array([max(D[i, j], D[i, k], D[j, k]) for i, j, k in triples])
jung = np.sqrt(4 / 3)                                      # sqrt(2d/(d+1)) for d = 2
for r in [0.3, 0.6, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0]:
    edges = int((D[np.triu_indices(30, 1)] <= 2 * r).sum())
    cech, rips = int((meb <= r).sum()), int((diam <= 2 * r).sum())
    ok = np.all(meb[diam <= 2 * r] <= jung * r + 1e-12)   # Rips(r) inside Cech(1.155 r)
    print(f"r = {r:.1f}: edges {edges:3d}, Cech triangles {cech:4d}, "
          f"Rips triangles {rips:4d}, Rips(r) in Cech({jung:.3f} r): {ok}")

Up to r = 0.8 both complexes have the same triangles (1171 at r = 0.8). At r = 0.9 the Rips complex has 1848 triangles and the Čech complex 1800; at r = 1, 3743 against 3617. The inclusion check prints True every time.

The 48 extra triangles at r = 0.9 are acute triangles spanning the circle. An inscribed equilateral triangle has side \sqrt 3: the Rips complex fills it from r = 0.866, the Čech complex only from its circumradius r = 1. The Rips complex closes the hole earlier, which ch. 4 measures as the death of a loop.

1.4 Alpha complexes and triangulated images

Definition 1.8 — Delaunay and alpha complexes The Voronoi cell of x \in X is the set of points of \mathbb{R}^d at least as close to x as to any other point of X. The Delaunay complex is the nerve of the Voronoi cells.

The alpha complex A(X, r) is the nerve of the sets \bar B(x, r) \cap V_x, where V_x is the Voronoi cell of x.

These sets are convex with union X^r, so A(X, r) \simeq X^r by the nerve theorem. In general position, the alpha complex is a subcomplex of the Delaunay triangulation: O(n) simplices in the plane, against up to 2^n for the Čech complex.

Definition 1.9 — Triangulated grids An image or a 2D scalar field is sampled on a grid of n \times m pixels. The Freudenthal triangulation has the pixels as vertices, the horizontal, vertical and one family of diagonal edges, and two triangles per square.

With periodic boundary conditions and n, m \geq 3, it triangulates the torus with nm vertices, 3nm edges and 2nm triangles, so \chi = 0. Each vertex then has degree 6, and its link is a hexagon.

In 3D, each cube is cut into six tetrahedra; cubical complexes, which keep squares and cubes, are the other common choice.

1.5 Filtrations

Definition 1.10 — Filtrations and filtration functions A filtration of a finite complex K is a family of subcomplexes (K_r)_{r \in \mathbb{R}} with K_r \subseteq K_s whenever r \leq s.

It is given by a filtration function f : K \to \mathbb{R} that is monotone, that is f(\tau) \leq f(\sigma) whenever \tau \subseteq \sigma, through K_r = \{ \sigma \in K : f(\sigma) \leq r \}.

The Čech, Rips and alpha complexes form filtrations as r grows; for the Rips complex, f(\sigma) is half the diameter of \sigma. For scalar fields, the central example is the following.

Proposition 1.11 — Lower-star filtrations Let f be defined on the vertices of K, and set f(\sigma) = \max_{v \in \sigma} f(v). This function is monotone, and K_r is the subcomplex spanned by the vertices with f(v) \leq r. If f is extended linearly on each simplex, the sublevel set of the extension at level r deformation retracts onto |K_r|.

Proof. A face has fewer vertices to maximize over, and a simplex enters K_r when all its vertices do. For the retraction, push each point of a simplex away from the face spanned by its high vertices, along a straight line, onto the face of its low vertices; f decreases along the way (Edelsbrunner and Harer, 2010, Chapter VI). ∎

Superlevel sets use the upper-star filtration, with \min instead of \max and the order reversed.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • A simplicial complex is a finite family of vertex sets closed under subsets; its Euler characteristic is the alternating count of simplices.
  • By the nerve theorem, the Čech complex of a point cloud is homotopy equivalent to the union of balls.
  • The Rips complex only uses pairwise distances, and it is sandwiched between two Čech complexes (Theorem 1.7).
  • Alpha complexes are smaller; images become triangulated grids; scalar fields give lower-star filtrations.

2. Simplicial homology

Homology counts the holes of a complex in each dimension — components, loops, cavities — by linear algebra on its simplices. This chapter defines it over \mathbb{F}_2 and computes it by Gaussian elimination.

2.1 Chains, cycles and boundaries

Definition 2.1 — Chains and the boundary map The space of k-chains C_k(K) is the \mathbb{F}_2-vector space with one basis vector per k-simplex: a chain is a set of k-simplices, and the sum is the symmetric difference.

The boundary of a k-simplex is the sum of its (k-1)-faces, \partial_k [v_0, \dots, v_k] = \sum_{i=0}^{k} [v_0, \dots, \widehat{v_i}, \dots, v_k], where the hat removes a vertex. It extends linearly to \partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}, with \partial_0 = 0.

For a triangle, \partial [a, b, c] = ab + bc + ca, and the boundary of this loop is (a + b) + (b + c) + (c + a) = 0. This is general.

Lemma 2.2 — The boundary of a boundary is zero \partial_{k-1} \circ \partial_k = 0 .

Proof. Let \sigma be a k-simplex. Each (k-2)-face of \sigma is obtained by removing two of its vertices, in two possible orders. It therefore appears twice in \partial \partial \sigma, and twice is zero in \mathbb{F}_2. Linearity concludes. ∎

Definition 2.3 — Homology and Betti numbers The cycles are Z_k = \ker \partial_k and the boundaries are B_k = \operatorname{im} \partial_{k+1}. By Lemma 2.2, B_k \subseteq Z_k. The k-th homology group and the Betti number are H_k(K) = Z_k / B_k, \qquad \beta_k = \dim H_k(K).

A k-cycle that is not a boundary encloses a k-dimensional hole; two cycles are homologous when their difference bounds.

Proposition 2.4 — Betti numbers by ranks With n_k the number of k-simplices,

Key result — Betti numbers from two ranks \beta_k = n_k - \operatorname{rank} \partial_k - \operatorname{rank} \partial_{k+1}

Proof. By Definition 0.4, \beta_k = \dim Z_k - \dim B_k. By rank–nullity, \dim Z_k = n_k - \operatorname{rank} \partial_k, and \dim B_k = \operatorname{rank} \partial_{k+1} by definition. ∎

2.2 First computations

Example 2.5 — Circle, disk and sphere

  • Hollow triangle (3 vertices, 3 edges). The matrix of \partial_1 is the matrix of Example 0.5, of rank 2 over \mathbb{F}_2. Hence \beta_0 = 3 - 0 - 2 = 1 and \beta_1 = 3 - 2 - 0 = 1: one component, one loop.
  • Filled triangle. Add the 2-simplex: \operatorname{rank} \partial_2 = 1, so \beta_1 = 0 and \beta_2 = 1 - 1 = 0. The loop now bounds.
  • Boundary of a tetrahedron (4, 6, 4 simplices). The ranks are \operatorname{rank} \partial_1 = 3 and \operatorname{rank} \partial_2 = 3, so the Betti numbers are (1, 0, 1): one cavity, as for a sphere.

Proposition 2.6 — \beta_0 counts connected components \beta_0 is the number of connected components of the graph formed by the vertices and edges of K.

Proof. In H_0 = C_0 / B_0, two vertices u, v are equal when u + v is a boundary. If a path u = w_0, \dots, w_\ell = v exists, u + v is the boundary of the sum of its edges, by telescoping.

Conversely, a sum of edges has an even number of endpoints in each component. So u + v is a boundary only if u and v lie in the same component. One vertex per component therefore gives a basis of H_0. ∎

Theorem 2.7 — Euler–Poincaré formula \chi(K) = \sum_k (-1)^k\, n_k = \sum_k (-1)^k\, \beta_k .

Proof. Sum Proposition 2.4 with signs. Each \operatorname{rank} \partial_k appears twice, once in \beta_k and once in \beta_{k-1}, with opposite signs; the sum telescopes. ∎

The torus of Example 1.3 has \chi = 0, and Code 2.1 finds \beta = (1, 2, 1): two independent loops and one cavity.

2.3 Maps and invariance

Definition 2.8 — Simplicial maps and contiguity A simplicial map \varphi : K \to L sends vertices to vertices and simplices to simplices; it induces linear maps C_k(K) \to C_k(L) that commute with \partial, hence maps \varphi_* : H_k(K) \to H_k(L).

Two simplicial maps \varphi, \psi : K \to L are contiguous if, for every simplex \sigma of K, the vertex set \varphi(\sigma) \cup \psi(\sigma) is a simplex of L.

A simplex is sent to zero in C_k(L) when its image has fewer vertices. Commutation with \partial makes \varphi_* well defined: cycles go to cycles and boundaries to boundaries.

Theorem 2.9 — Invariance

  1. If |K| \simeq |L|, then H_k(K) \cong H_k(L) for every k: homology is a homotopy invariant of the realization.
  2. Contiguous simplicial maps induce the same map on homology.

The first statement is proved through singular homology (Hatcher, 2002, Corollary 2.11 and Theorem 2.27; Munkres, 1984, Chapter 2). The second follows from a chain homotopy built on the simplices \varphi(\sigma) \cup \psi(\sigma) (Munkres, 1984, Chapter 1).

With the nerve theorem, the homology of the Čech complex is the homology of the union of balls. Contiguity is the tool of the stability proof for Rips filtrations in ch. 7.

2.4 Other coefficients

Over \mathbb{Q} or \mathbb{Z}, the vertices of each simplex are ordered, and faces receive signs: \partial [v_0, \dots, v_k] = \sum_{i=0}^{k} (-1)^i\, [v_0, \dots, \widehat{v_i}, \dots, v_k]. Lemma 2.2 still holds, because the two copies of each (k-2)-face now carry opposite signs (Exercise 9).

The Betti numbers can depend on the field. On the projective plane, a loop that goes around twice bounds, but the loop itself does not: over \mathbb{Z}, H_1 is \mathbb{Z}/2. Over \mathbb{F}_2 this torsion is visible as \beta_1 = 1, and over \mathbb{Q} it disappears.

Over \mathbb{F}_2 no orientation is needed and each column of a boundary matrix is a set of indices. TDA software therefore works over \mathbb{F}_2 or a small prime field.

Code 2.1 — Betti numbers of a torus and a projective plane The code builds all faces of a list of triangles, assembles the boundary matrices, and computes the Betti numbers with Proposition 2.4: over \mathbb{F}_2 by Gaussian elimination, over \mathbb{Q} with signed matrices.

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

def closure(top):
    """All faces of a list of top simplices, sorted by dimension."""
    faces = {tuple(sorted(f)) for s in top for k in range(1, len(s) + 1)
             for f in combinations(s, k)}
    return [sorted(f for f in faces if len(f) == k + 1) for k in range(len(top[0]))]

def boundary(S, k, signed):
    """Matrix of the boundary map C_k -> C_{k-1} (rows: (k-1)-faces)."""
    index = {f: i for i, f in enumerate(S[k - 1])}
    B = np.zeros((len(S[k - 1]), len(S[k])), dtype=np.int64)
    for j, s in enumerate(S[k]):
        for i in range(len(s)):
            B[index[s[:i] + s[i + 1:]], j] = (-1) ** i if signed else 1
    return B

def rank_f2(B):
    """Rank over Z/2 by Gaussian elimination."""
    A, r = B.copy() % 2, 0
    for c in range(A.shape[1]):
        piv = np.nonzero(A[r:, c])[0]
        if len(piv) == 0:
            continue
        A[[r, r + piv[0]]] = A[[r + piv[0], r]]
        rows = np.nonzero(A[:, c])[0]
        A[rows[rows != r]] ^= A[r]
        r += 1
        if r == A.shape[0]:
            break
    return r

def betti(top, field):
    S = closure(top)
    rk = [0] + [rank_f2(boundary(S, k, False)) if field == "F2"
                else np.linalg.matrix_rank(boundary(S, k, True).astype(float))
                for k in range(1, len(S))] + [0]
    return [int(len(S[k]) - rk[k] - rk[k + 1]) for k in range(len(S))]

n = 3                                    # 3 x 3 periodic grid: a 9-vertex torus
v = lambda i, j: (i % n) * n + (j % n)
torus = [t for i in range(n) for j in range(n) for t in
         [(v(i, j), v(i + 1, j), v(i + 1, j + 1)), (v(i, j), v(i, j + 1), v(i + 1, j + 1))]]
rp2 = [(0, 1, 3), (0, 1, 5), (0, 2, 4), (0, 2, 5), (0, 3, 4),
       (1, 2, 3), (1, 2, 4), (1, 4, 5), (2, 3, 5), (3, 4, 5)]   # 6-vertex projective plane
for name, top in [("torus", torus), ("projective plane", rp2)]:
    S = closure(top)
    chi = sum((-1) ** k * len(S[k]) for k in range(3))
    print(f"{name:16s} f-vector {[len(s) for s in S]}, chi = {chi}, "
          f"Betti over F2 {betti(top, 'F2')}, over Q {betti(top, 'Q')}")

The torus has f-vector (9, 27, 18), \chi = 0 and Betti numbers (1, 2, 1) over both fields.

The projective plane has f-vector (6, 15, 10) and \chi = 1. Its Betti numbers are (1, 1, 1) over \mathbb{F}_2 and (1, 0, 0) over \mathbb{Q}: in both cases the alternating sum is the Euler characteristic, as Theorem 2.7 requires.

Gaussian elimination on n simplices costs O(n^3) operations in the worst case. Ch. 4 reorganises this elimination so that one pass over a filtration gives the homology of every subcomplex at once.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Chains are sets of simplices; the boundary map satisfies \partial \circ \partial = 0.
  • H_k = Z_k / B_k, and \beta_k follows from the ranks of two boundary matrices (Proposition 2.4).
  • \beta_0 counts components, \beta_1 independent loops, \beta_2 cavities; their alternating sum is \chi.
  • Homology is a homotopy invariant, and contiguous maps induce the same map on it.
  • Over \mathbb{F}_2 no orientation is needed; coefficients matter only in the presence of torsion.

3. Smooth and discrete Morse theory

A scalar field changes the topology of its sublevel sets only at critical points. Morse theory makes this precise; its piecewise-linear and discrete versions turn it into algorithms.

3.1 Smooth Morse theory

Definition 3.1 — Morse functions and index Let M be a closed smooth manifold of dimension d and f : M \to \mathbb{R} smooth. A point p is critical if df(p) = 0, and non-degenerate if moreover the Hessian there is invertible.

The index \lambda(p) is the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian. f is a Morse function if all its critical points are non-degenerate; it is generic if, moreover, its critical values are distinct.

Minima have index 0 and maxima index d; on a surface, saddles have index 1. Generic Morse functions are dense among smooth functions.

Theorem 3.2 — Morse lemma and cell attachment Write M_a = f^{-1}(-\infty, a].

  1. Near a non-degenerate critical point p of index \lambda, there are coordinates in which f = f(p) - x_1^2 - \dots - x_\lambda^2 + x_{\lambda+1}^2 + \dots + x_d^2 .
  2. If f^{-1}([a, b]) contains no critical point, M_a is a deformation retract of M_b.
  3. If p is the only critical point with value in [c - \varepsilon, c + \varepsilon], then M_{c+\varepsilon} is homotopy equivalent to M_{c-\varepsilon} with one cell of dimension \lambda(p) attached.

Proof. Sketch (Milnor, 1963, Lemma 2.2 and Theorems 3.1 and 3.2). For (2), the flow of -\nabla f / \|\nabla f\|^2 decreases f at unit speed and pushes M_b onto M_a. For (3), in the coordinates of (1), the sublevel set gains a thickened \lambda-disk along the negative directions, glued along its boundary sphere. ∎

Example 3.3 — The height function on a torus Stand a torus vertically and let f be the height. It has four critical points: a minimum, two saddles, a maximum.

The sublevel sets are successively a disk (after the minimum), a cylinder (after the first saddle attaches a 1-cell), a torus minus a disk (after the second saddle) and the torus (after the maximum attaches a 2-cell).

Theorem 3.4 — Morse inequalities Let m_\lambda be the number of critical points of index \lambda of a Morse function on M. Then m_\lambda \geq \beta_\lambda(M) for every \lambda, and \sum_\lambda (-1)^\lambda\, m_\lambda = \chi(M).

Proof. Euler formula. By Theorem 3.2, M is homotopy equivalent to a CW complex with m_\lambda cells of dimension \lambda. The Euler characteristic of a CW complex is the alternating count of its cells, and Theorem 2.7 extends to cell complexes.

Inequalities. Attaching a \lambda-cell changes one Betti number by one: it raises \beta_\lambda or lowers \beta_{\lambda-1}. To reach \beta_\lambda(M), at least \beta_\lambda(M) cells of dimension \lambda are needed. ∎

For the torus, 1 - 2 + 1 = 0 = \chi, and m = (1, 2, 1) equals the Betti numbers: the height function is perfect.

3.2 Piecewise-linear Morse theory

Data come as values on the vertices of a triangulation, extended linearly. Ties are broken by the vertex index (simulation of simplicity, Edelsbrunner and Mücke, 1990), so all values are distinct.

Definition 3.5 — Lower link and PL critical points The lower link of a vertex v is the subcomplex of its link spanned by the vertices of lower value: \mathrm{Lk}^-(v) = \{ \tau \in \mathrm{Lk}(v) : f(u) < f(v) \text{ for all } u \in \tau \}. The upper link is defined with f(u) > f(v). On a triangulated surface, where the link is a cycle, v is:

  • a minimum if \mathrm{Lk}^-(v) is empty, a maximum if \mathrm{Lk}^-(v) is the whole link;
  • regular if \mathrm{Lk}^-(v) is one arc;
  • a saddle of multiplicity k - 1 if \mathrm{Lk}^-(v) has k \geq 2 arcs (a monkey saddle for k = 3).

This classification follows Banchoff (1967): entering v adds a cone over \mathrm{Lk}^-(v), which merges k fronts when the lower link has k arcs.

Theorem 3.6 — Banchoff’s index formula For a function with distinct values on the vertices of any finite simplicial complex K, \sum_{v} \Big( 1 - \chi\big(\mathrm{Lk}^-(v)\big) \Big) = \chi(K).

Proof. Each simplex \sigma has a unique highest vertex v. Removing v leaves either nothing, when \sigma = \{v\}, or a simplex \tau all of whose vertices are lower than v.

Such a \tau lies in \mathrm{Lk}^-(v). Conversely, every simplex of the lower link, joined with v, is a simplex of K with highest vertex v.

Group the terms of \chi(K) by highest vertex. The contribution of v is 1 for the vertex itself, plus (-1)^{\dim \tau + 1} for each \tau in the lower link: \chi(K) = \sum_v \Big( 1 - \sum_{\tau \in \mathrm{Lk}^-(v)} (-1)^{\dim \tau} \Big) = \sum_v \big( 1 - \chi(\mathrm{Lk}^-(v)) \big). ∎

On a surface, the term 1 - \chi(\mathrm{Lk}^-(v)) of a vertex is:

  • 1 for a minimum (empty lower link) and for a maximum (a cycle, of Euler characteristic 0);
  • 0 for a regular vertex (an arc, of Euler characteristic 1);
  • 1 - k for a saddle with k lower arcs.

Hence:

Corollary 3.7 — Critical points on a surface Counting each saddle with its multiplicity, \#\text{minima} - \#\text{saddles} + \#\text{maxima} = \chi(K).

Code 3.1 — Critical points of a noisy field on a torus The field \cos 2\pi x + \cos 2\pi y is sampled on a 64 \times 64 periodic grid with the Freudenthal triangulation of Definition 1.9, with and without Gaussian noise. The code walks around the hexagonal link of each vertex, counts the lower arcs, and checks Corollary 3.7 with \chi = 0.

import numpy as np

def critical_points(f):
    """PL critical vertices of f on the Freudenthal triangulation of a periodic grid.
    Ties are broken by the vertex index (simulation of simplicity)."""
    n, m = f.shape
    key = f.ravel().argsort(kind="stable").argsort().reshape(n, m)  # rank of each vertex
    ring = [(1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1), (-1, 0), (-1, -1), (0, -1)]  # cyclic link of a vertex
    low = np.stack([np.roll(key, (-di, -dj), axis=(0, 1)) < key for di, dj in ring])
    arcs = (low & ~np.roll(low, 1, axis=0)).sum(axis=0)     # number of lower arcs in the hexagon
    n_low = low.sum(axis=0)
    minima, maxima = n_low == 0, n_low == 6
    saddle_mult = np.where(~minima & ~maxima, arcs - 1, 0)  # a k-fold saddle has k lower arcs
    return minima.sum(), saddle_mult.sum(), maxima.sum()

n = 64
x, y = np.meshgrid(np.arange(n) / n, np.arange(n) / n, indexing="ij")
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for noise in [0.0, 0.05, 0.2]:
    f = np.cos(2 * np.pi * x) + np.cos(2 * np.pi * y) + noise * rng.normal(size=(n, n))
    m0, m1, m2 = critical_points(f)
    print(f"noise {noise:.2f}: minima {m0:4d}, saddles {m1:4d}, maxima {m2:4d}, "
          f"m0 - m1 + m2 = {m0 - m1 + m2}")

Without noise, the code finds 1 minimum, 2 saddles and 1 maximum. With noise 0.05 it finds 164, 320 and 156; with noise 0.2, 508, 1005 and 497. The alternating sum is 0 every time.

Noise multiplies the critical points by hundreds, while their alternating sum cannot move. Deciding which of them matter is the job of persistence (ch. 4).

3.3 Discrete Morse theory

Forman’s theory replaces the gradient by a matching between simplices, which suits exact combinatorial algorithms.

Definition 3.8 — Discrete Morse functions A function F on the simplices of K is a discrete Morse function if, for every p-simplex \sigma:

  • at most one coface \tau of dimension p + 1 has F(\tau) \leq F(\sigma);
  • at most one face \nu of dimension p - 1 has F(\nu) \geq F(\sigma).

A simplex is critical when both numbers are zero.

Definition 3.9 — Discrete gradient vector fields The gradient of F is the set V of pairs (\sigma < \tau), of dimensions p and p + 1, with F(\tau) \leq F(\sigma). Each simplex belongs to at most one pair, and the unpaired simplices are the critical ones.

A V-path alternates \sigma_0 < \tau_0 > \sigma_1 < \tau_1 > \dots, with (\sigma_i, \tau_i) \in V and \sigma_{i+1} \neq \sigma_i a face of \tau_i. A matching is the gradient of some discrete Morse function if and only if it has no closed V-path (Forman, 1998).

Theorem 3.10 — Forman’s theorem If F is a discrete Morse function on K, then |K| is homotopy equivalent to a CW complex with exactly one cell of dimension p for each critical p-simplex.

In particular, if m_p counts the critical p-simplices, then m_p \geq \beta_p(K) and \sum_p (-1)^p m_p = \chi(K). The proof replaces each gradient pair by an elementary collapse, which does not change the homotopy type (Section 10.3).

Example 3.11 — Gradients on a triangle and on its boundary

  • Filled triangle abc. The pairs (b, ab), (c, ac) and (bc, abc) leave only a critical: the triangle is contractible.
  • Hollow triangle. Pair b with ab and c with bc. The vertex a and the edge ca remain critical: one 0-cell and one 1-cell, a circle.
  • On the hollow triangle, the pairs (a, ab), (b, bc) and (c, ca) form a closed V-path: they are not a gradient.

Proposition 3.12 — Steepest-descent pairing Let f have distinct values on the vertices. Pair each vertex that is not a local minimum with the edge to its lowest neighbour. This is a discrete gradient, whose critical vertices are the local minima.

Proof. An edge vu with f(u) < f(v) can only be paired with v, so each edge is used at most once. Along a V-path v_0, v_0 v_1, v_1, \dots, each step goes to a lower vertex, so the values strictly decrease and no path closes. ∎

Robins, Wood and Sheppard (2011) extend this pairing to all dimensions, with critical simplices matching the topology changes of the lower-star filtration; their V-paths replace integral lines in ch. 6.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Sublevel sets of a Morse function change only at critical points, by attaching a cell of dimension equal to the index.
  • The Morse inequalities bound the number of critical points below by the Betti numbers; their alternating sum is \chi.
  • On triangulated data, the lower link classifies vertices; Banchoff’s formula is an exact count by highest vertex.
  • Discrete gradients are acyclic matchings of simplices; critical simplices give a cell complex of the same homotopy type.

4. Persistent homology and persistence diagrams

Data come with a scale or a threshold that nobody knows how to choose. Persistence follows every hole across all scales at once, records when it appears and disappears, and summarizes the result stably.

4.1 Persistence modules and barcodes

Let \emptyset = K_0 \subset K_1 \subset \dots \subset K_m = K be a filtration in which K_i adds one simplex \sigma_i to K_{i-1} (ties broken arbitrarily, faces before cofaces).

Definition 4.1 — Persistence modules and persistent Betti numbers The persistence module of degree k is the sequence H_k(K_0) \to H_k(K_1) \to \dots \to H_k(K_m), with the maps \iota_i^j : H_k(K_i) \to H_k(K_j) induced by inclusion for i \leq j. The persistent Betti numbers are \beta_k^{i,j} = \operatorname{rank} \iota_i^j . A class \gamma \in H_k(K_i) is born at i if it is not in the image of \iota_{i-1}^{i}.

It dies at j > i if its image in H_k(K_{j-1}) is not in the image of H_k(K_{i-1}), while its image in H_k(K_j) is: at j it merges with an older class.

\beta_k^{i,j} counts the k-dimensional holes of K_i that are still holes in K_j.

Theorem 4.2 — Structure theorem A persistence module of finite-dimensional spaces decomposes, uniquely up to order, into a direct sum of interval modules: each interval [b, d) contributes a copy of \mathbb{F}_2 at the indices b \leq i < d, connected by identity maps, and 0 elsewhere.

The multiset of intervals is the barcode. It satisfies, for i \leq j, \beta_k^{i,j} = \#\{ [b, d) \text{ in the barcode} : b \leq i \text{ and } j < d \}. For finite filtrations this is due to Zomorodian and Carlsson (2005), through graded modules over \mathbb{F}_2[t]; Crawley-Boevey (2015) extends it to modules indexed by \mathbb{R} with finite-dimensional spaces.

An interval [b, d) of indices becomes the interval [f(\sigma_b), f(\sigma_d)) of scales; intervals of length zero are discarded.

4.2 The elder rule

Proposition 4.3 — 0-dimensional persistence by union-find Sweep the filtration with union-find (Algorithm 0.6). A vertex starts a new component. When an edge joins two components, the younger one — whose oldest vertex entered later — dies, and its bar [b, d) goes from the entry of that oldest vertex to the entry of the edge. The oldest component never dies.

The resulting barcode is the barcode of H_0.

Proof. By Proposition 2.6, \beta_0^{i,j} is the number of components of K_j that contain a vertex of K_i. Each component C of K_j carries exactly one bar alive at j, the bar of its oldest vertex.

C meets K_i if and only if its oldest vertex entered at or before i, that is, if and only if this bar starts at or before i. So the elder-rule bars satisfy the formula of Theorem 4.2 for all i \leq j. The persistent Betti numbers determine the barcode by inclusion–exclusion, which concludes. ∎

For the Rips filtration, the finite H_0 bars die at half the edge lengths of a minimum spanning tree: the sweep is Kruskal’s algorithm (Exercise 14).

4.3 The reduction algorithm

Order the simplices as in the filtration, and let \partial be the m \times m boundary matrix over \mathbb{F}_2: column j holds the faces of \sigma_j. For a non-zero column, \mathrm{low}(j) is the index of its lowest 1.

Algorithm 4.4 — Standard reduction

  1. For j = 1, \dots, m: while some j' < j has \mathrm{low}(j') = \mathrm{low}(j), add column j' to column j.
  2. The result is R = \partial V with V upper triangular and invertible; the non-zero columns of R have distinct lows.

The cost is O(m^3) in the worst case (Edelsbrunner, Letscher and Zomorodian, 2002; Zomorodian and Carlsson, 2005).

Theorem 4.5 — Persistence pairs After the reduction:

  • each non-zero column j with \mathrm{low}(j) = i gives a bar [f(\sigma_i), f(\sigma_j)) in degree \dim \sigma_i: \sigma_i creates a class that \sigma_j kills;
  • each i with R_i = 0 that is the low of no column gives an infinite bar [f(\sigma_i), \infty).

The pairs do not depend on the order of the column additions (pairing lemma, Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner and Morozov, 2006). Section 10.1 proves it with ranks of lower-left submatrices.

A simplex with R_i = 0 is positive: it creates a class. A simplex with R_j \neq 0 is negative: it kills the class created by \sigma_{\mathrm{low}(j)}, the youngest simplex of the cycle R_j.

Code 4.1 — Persistence of the Rips filtration of a noisy circle The code sorts the simplices of dimension at most 2 of the Rips filtration of the points of Code 1.1, then runs Algorithm 4.4 with columns stored as sets.

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

def rips_filtration(P, rmax):
    """Simplices of dimension <= 2 of the Rips filtration, value r = diameter / 2."""
    D = np.linalg.norm(P[:, None] - P[None], axis=2) / 2
    n = len(P)
    S = [((i,), 0.0) for i in range(n)]
    S += [((i, j), D[i, j]) for i, j in combinations(range(n), 2) if D[i, j] <= rmax]
    S += [((i, j, k), max(D[i, j], D[i, k], D[j, k])) for i, j, k in combinations(range(n), 3)
          if max(D[i, j], D[i, k], D[j, k]) <= rmax]
    return sorted(S, key=lambda s: (s[1], len(s[0])))       # faces before cofaces

def persistence(S):
    """Standard column reduction over Z/2; returns (dim, birth, death) triples."""
    index = {s: i for i, (s, _) in enumerate(S)}
    low_of, cols, pairs, paired = {}, [], [], set()
    for j, (s, val) in enumerate(S):
        col = {index[s[:i] + s[i + 1:]] for i in range(len(s))} if len(s) > 1 else set()
        while col and max(col) in low_of:                  # add the column with the same pivot
            col ^= cols[low_of[max(col)]]
        cols.append(col)
        if col:
            i = max(col)
            low_of[i] = j
            paired |= {i, j}
            if val > S[i][1]:                              # skip zero-length intervals
                pairs.append((len(S[i][0]) - 1, S[i][1], val))
    pairs += [(len(s) - 1, val, np.inf) for j, (s, val) in enumerate(S)
              if j not in paired and len(s) < 3]           # essential classes (dim < top)
    return pairs

4.4 Persistence diagrams and their distances

Definition 4.6 — Persistence diagram The persistence diagram \mathrm{Dgm}_k(f) is the multiset of points (b, d), one per bar of degree k, with d = +\infty for infinite bars.

It also contains every point of the diagonal \Delta = \{(t, t)\}, with infinite multiplicity. The persistence of (b, d) is d - b.

Points far from the diagonal are long-lived features; the diagonal lets diagrams of different sizes be matched.

Definition 4.7 — Bottleneck and Wasserstein distances A matching between two diagrams D and D' is a bijection \gamma : D \to D', where points may be matched to the diagonal. The bottleneck distance and the q-Wasserstein distance are d_B(D, D') = \inf_\gamma \sup_{p \in D} \| p - \gamma(p) \|_\infty, \qquad W_q(D, D') = \inf_\gamma \Big( \sum_{p \in D} \| p - \gamma(p) \|_\infty^q \Big)^{1/q}. Matching (b, d) to its closest diagonal point costs (d - b)/2.

Example 4.8 — Distances by hand Take D = \{(0, 4), (1, 2)\} and D' = \{(0, 3.5)\}.

  • Match (0, 4) with (0, 3.5), at cost 0.5, and send (1, 2) to the diagonal, at cost 0.5.
  • Matching (1, 2) with (0, 3.5) instead would cost 1.5.

Hence d_B(D, D') = 0.5, \qquad W_1(D, D') = 1 .

Proposition 4.9 — Computing the bottleneck distance For finite diagrams with n and n' points, add a diagonal copy of each point of the other diagram, and let any two diagonal copies be matched at cost 0. Then d_B is the smallest threshold t among the pairwise costs for which the bipartite graph of the pairs of cost at most t has a perfect matching.

A binary search over the O((n + n')^2) costs and the Hopcroft–Karp algorithm give d_B in polynomial time; W_q is an assignment problem on the same graph.

4.5 Stability

Theorem 4.10 — Stability of persistence diagrams Let f, g be two monotone filtration functions on the same finite complex K, for instance the lower-star filtrations of two images on the same grid. For every degree k:

Key result — stability d_B\big( \mathrm{Dgm}_k(f), \mathrm{Dgm}_k(g) \big) \;\leq\; \| f - g \|_\infty

Proof. Sketch (the vineyard argument of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner and Morozov, 2006). Let f_t = (1 - t) f + t g for t \in [0, 1]; each f_t is monotone.

The pairs of Theorem 4.5 depend only on the order of the simplices. Between two times at which this order changes, the paired simplices (\sigma, \tau) are fixed, and the point (f_t(\sigma), f_t(\tau)) moves at speed at most \| g - f \|_\infty in the sup norm.

At a time when two simplices swap, they have equal values, and the diagram is continuous across the swap: only points with coinciding coordinates exchange their pairs. Following each point along [0, 1] defines a matching that moves no point by more than \| f - g \|_\infty. ∎

Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner and Harer (2007) proved it for tame functions on triangulable spaces; Section 10.2 gives the algebraic version.

4.6 Images, noise and simplification

For an image, bright blobs are maxima, tracked by superlevel sets. In degree 0, each local maximum dies at the saddle level where it joins a higher peak: its persistence is its prominence.

Code 4.2 — Superlevel-set persistence of a noisy image Three Gaussian bumps of heights 1, 0.8 and 0.6 (width 6 pixels) on a 64 \times 64 grid, plus noise of standard deviation 0.05. Proposition 4.3 runs on the 4-neighbour graph, in decreasing order.

import numpy as np

def superlevel_h0(f):
    """0-dim persistence of the superlevel sets of an image (4-neighbour grid),
    by union-find and the elder rule. Returns (birth, death) pairs, birth >= death."""
    n, m = f.shape
    flat = f.ravel()
    parent = -np.ones(n * m, dtype=int)                # -1 = pixel not yet in the filtration
    def find(a):
        while parent[a] != a:
            parent[a] = parent[parent[a]]
            a = parent[a]
        return a
    pairs = []
    for v in np.argsort(-flat, kind="stable"):         # decreasing values
        parent[v] = v
        i, j = divmod(v, m)
        for a, b in [(i - 1, j), (i + 1, j), (i, j - 1), (i, j + 1)]:
            if 0 <= a < n and 0 <= b < m and parent[a * m + b] >= 0:
                ru, rv = find(a * m + b), find(v)
                if ru != rv:                           # elder rule: the lower peak dies
                    young, old = (ru, rv) if flat[ru] < flat[rv] else (rv, ru)
                    if flat[young] > flat[v]:          # skip zero-length pairs
                        pairs.append((flat[young], flat[v]))
                    parent[young] = old
    pairs.append((flat.max(), -np.inf))                # the essential class
    return np.array(pairs)

n = 64
x, y = np.meshgrid(np.arange(n), np.arange(n), indexing="ij")

The gap between 0.64 and 0.172 separates objects from noise. Theorem 4.10 explains it: noise of sup-norm \delta only creates points of persistence at most 2\delta.

This bound is a worst case. Here \delta = 0.195, so it allows noise points up to 0.39, while the highest one reaches 0.172 (Exercise 6).

Code 4.3 — Stability in practice A noisy signal is perturbed by uniform noise of amplitude \delta; the code compares both H_0 diagrams with Proposition 4.9.

import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import csr_matrix
from scipy.sparse.csgraph import maximum_bipartite_matching

def sublevel_h0(f):
    """Finite 0-dim intervals (birth, death) of the sublevel sets of a 1D signal."""
    parent, pairs = -np.ones(len(f), dtype=int), []
    def find(a):
        while parent[a] != a:
            a = parent[a]
        return a
    for v in np.argsort(f, kind="stable"):
        parent[v] = v
        for u in (v - 1, v + 1):
            if 0 <= u < len(f) and parent[u] >= 0:
                ru, rv = find(u), find(v)
                if ru != rv:
                    young, old = (ru, rv) if f[ru] > f[rv] else (rv, ru)
                    if f[young] < f[v]:
                        pairs.append((f[young], f[v]))
                    parent[young] = old
    return np.array(pairs).reshape(-1, 2)

def bottleneck(A, B):
    """Bottleneck distance between finite diagrams, by thresholded bipartite matching.
    Rows: points of A, then diagonal copies of B; columns: points of B, then copies of A."""
    a, b = len(A), len(B)
    C = np.full((a + b, b + a), np.inf)
    C[:a, :b] = np.abs(A[:, None, :] - B[None, :, :]).max(axis=2)
    C[np.arange(a), b + np.arange(a)] = (A[:, 1] - A[:, 0]) / 2   # A_i to the diagonal
    C[a + np.arange(b), np.arange(b)] = (B[:, 1] - B[:, 0]) / 2   # diagonal to B_j
    C[a:, b:] = 0.0                                                # diagonal to diagonal
    values = np.unique(C[np.isfinite(C)])
    lo, hi = 0, len(values) - 1
    while lo < hi:                                     # smallest threshold with a perfect matching
        mid = (lo + hi) // 2
        match = maximum_bipartite_matching(csr_matrix(C <= values[mid]), perm_type="column")
        lo, hi = (mid + 1, hi) if (match < 0).any() else (lo, mid)
    return values[lo]

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
t = np.linspace(0, 1, 400)
f = np.sin(6 * np.pi * t) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=t.size)
for delta in [0.01, 0.05, 0.2]:
    g = f + rng.uniform(-delta, delta, size=t.size)
    Df, Dg = sublevel_h0(f), sublevel_h0(g)
    big = [(D[:, 1] - D[:, 0] > 1.5).sum() for D in (Df, Dg)]
    print(f"|f - g|_inf = {np.abs(f - g).max():.4f}: bottleneck = {bottleneck(Df, Dg):.4f}; "
          f"{len(Df)} and {len(Dg)} points, persistence > 1.5: {big[0]} and {big[1]}")

For perturbations of sup-norm 0.0100, 0.0500 and 0.2000, the bottleneck distances are 0.0100, 0.0490 and 0.1825. The diagrams have 130 to 138 points, but always exactly two finite points of persistence above 1.5.

Removing a point of persistence p needs a perturbation of sup-norm at least p/2, its distance to the diagonal. On surfaces this bound is reached.

Theorem 4.11 — Persistence simplification Let f be a function on a triangulated surface and \delta > 0. Some g with \| f - g \|_\infty \leq \delta has exactly as many critical points as f has critical points of persistence greater than 2\delta, counting the essential ones.

By Theorem 4.10, no function within \delta of f has fewer: this lower bound is tight.

See Edelsbrunner, Morozov and Pascucci (2006) and Bauer, Lange and Wardetzky (2012); Tierny and Pascucci (2012) give the general algorithm used in TTK.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • A persistence module decomposes into intervals: the barcode, or the diagram.
  • In degree 0, union-find with the elder rule computes it; the reduction algorithm does in every degree.
  • Diagrams are compared by matching, with the bottleneck and Wasserstein distances.
  • Stability: d_B \leq \| f - g \|_\infty; noise of amplitude \delta only adds points of persistence at most 2\delta, which simplification removes.

5. Reeb graphs, contour trees and Mapper

The level sets of a scalar field split and merge as the level varies. The Reeb graph records this history; Mapper computes a discrete version of it for point clouds.

5.1 Reeb graphs

Definition 5.1 — Reeb graph Let f : X \to \mathbb{R} be continuous. Write x \sim y when f(x) = f(y) and x, y lie in the same connected component of the level set f^{-1}(f(x)). The Reeb graph (Reeb, 1946) is the quotient R(f) = X / \sim , and f induces a function \bar f on it. Each point of R(f) is one connected component of one level set.

Theorem 5.2 — Reeb graphs of Morse functions Let f be a Morse function on a compact connected manifold X.

  1. R(f) is a finite graph. Its nodes are the images of the critical points at which the number of components of the level set changes; minima and maxima are nodes of degree 1.
  2. The quotient map induces a surjection on first homology, so \beta_1(R(f)) \leq \beta_1(X).
  3. If X is a closed orientable surface of genus g, then R(f) has exactly g independent loops.

See Cole-McLaughlin, Edelsbrunner, Harer, Natarajan and Pascucci (2004) for the third statement, and Dey and Wang (2022, Chapter 7) for the first two.

For the height function of the torus (Example 3.3), the Reeb graph is a minimum, a saddle where the circle splits into two, two parallel arcs, a saddle where they merge, and a maximum: one loop, as g = 1.

5.2 Merge trees and contour trees

On a simply connected domain, such as a rectangular image, \beta_1 = 0, so the Reeb graph is a tree by Theorem 5.2: the contour tree.

Definition 5.3 — Merge trees The join tree of f tracks the components of the superlevel sets f^{-1}([t, \infty)) as t decreases: its leaves are the maxima, and its interior nodes are the saddles at which two components join. The split tree does the same for sublevel sets and minima.

Both are computed by the union-find sweep of Proposition 4.3, recording a node at every merge. A persistence pair is then a leaf together with the node where its branch meets an older branch: the diagram in degree 0 is the branch decomposition of the merge tree (Pascucci, Cole-McLaughlin and Scorzelli, 2004).

Example 5.4 — A split tree by hand Let a path v_1, \dots, v_7 carry the values 2, 6, 1, 4, 3, 5, 0. Sweep upwards:

  1. v_7 (0), v_3 (1), v_1 (2) and v_5 (3) are minima: four leaves;
  2. at v_4 (4), the components of v_3 and v_5 meet; the younger one, born at 3, dies: pair (3, 4);
  3. at v_6 (5), the components of v_3 and v_7 meet; the one born at 1 dies: pair (1, 5);
  4. at v_2 (6), the component of v_1 meets the rest: pair (2, 6).

The split tree has four leaves and three merge nodes, and \mathrm{Dgm}_0 = \{ (3, 4),\ (1, 5),\ (2, 6),\ (0, \infty) \}.

Algorithm 5.5 — Contour tree from the join and split trees Carr, Snoeyink and Axen (2003) compute the contour tree of a PL function on a simply connected domain in any dimension. They build both merge trees, then repeatedly move to the contour tree a leaf of one tree that satisfies a local degree condition in the other, and delete it from both. The cost is O(n \log n + N \alpha(N)) for n vertices and N simplices.

In scientific visualization, an arc of the contour tree is a family of contours without topological change: selecting it segments a feature. Cutting the branch decomposition at a persistence threshold simplifies the tree.

5.3 Mapper

Definition 5.6 — Mapper Let X be a point cloud, \varphi : X \to \mathbb{R} a filter (a coordinate, a density, an eccentricity), and \mathcal{I} a cover of \varphi(X) by overlapping intervals. For each interval I, cluster the preimage \varphi^{-1}(I).

The Mapper graph (Singh, Mémoli and Carlsson, 2007) is the nerve of the family of all these clusters: one node per cluster, one edge between two clusters that share a point.

When the clusters are connected components and the intervals shrink, Mapper converges to the Reeb graph (Munch and Wang, 2016). Carrière and Oudot (2018) describe which features a given cover keeps, and Carrière, Michel and Oudot (2018) choose the cover from data.

Code 5.1 — Mapper of a circle and of a figure eight Six hundred noisy points are sampled on a circle and on the figure eight t \mapsto (\sin t, \sin t \cos t). The filter is the first coordinate, covered by 8 intervals with 30 % overlap; each preimage is clustered by single linkage at scale 0.25.

import numpy as np
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import fcluster, linkage

def mapper(X, filt, n_int=8, overlap=0.3, eps=0.25):
    """Mapper graph: cover the range of the filter by overlapping intervals,
    cluster each preimage by single linkage at scale eps, link clusters that share points."""
    lo, hi = filt.min(), filt.max()
    length = (hi - lo) / (n_int - (n_int - 1) * overlap)
    nodes = []
    for k in range(n_int):
        a = lo + k * length * (1 - overlap)
        idx = np.nonzero((filt >= a) & (filt <= a + length))[0]
        if len(idx) == 1:
            nodes.append(set(idx))
        elif len(idx) > 1:
            labels = fcluster(linkage(X[idx], "single"), eps, criterion="distance")
            nodes += [set(idx[labels == c]) for c in np.unique(labels)]
    edges = {(i, j) for i in range(len(nodes)) for j in range(i + 1, len(nodes))
             if nodes[i] & nodes[j]}
    return nodes, edges

def components(n, edges):
    parent = list(range(n))
    def find(a):
        while parent[a] != a:
            a = parent[a]
        return a
    for i, j in edges:
        parent[find(i)] = find(j)
    return len({find(i) for i in range(n)})

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 600)
data = {"circle": np.c_[np.cos(t), np.sin(t)],
        "figure eight": np.c_[np.sin(t), np.sin(t) * np.cos(t)]}
for name, X in data.items():
    X = X + 0.03 * rng.normal(size=X.shape)
    nodes, edges = mapper(X, filt=X[:, 0])
    c = components(len(nodes), edges)
    print(f"{name:12s}: {len(nodes)} nodes, {len(edges)} edges, {c} component(s), "
          f"independent loops = {len(edges) - len(nodes) + c}")

The circle gives 14 nodes, 14 edges and one loop; the figure eight gives 12 nodes, 13 edges and two loops. The number of loops of a graph is E - V + C (Exercise 10).

Too few intervals merge branches and too little overlap disconnects the graph (Exercise 13). Nicolau, Levine and Carlsson (2011) used Mapper on breast-cancer expression profiles to find a subgroup with excellent survival.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • The Reeb graph contracts each component of each level set to a point; for Morse functions it is a finite graph with at most \beta_1(X) loops.
  • On simply connected domains it is a tree, the contour tree, built from the join and split trees.
  • Merge trees come from the union-find sweep; their branch decomposition is the 0-dimensional diagram.
  • Mapper is the nerve of clusters of preimages of a filter; it approximates the Reeb graph and depends on the cover.

6. Morse–Smale complexes

Following the gradient from every point partitions the domain into cells, each flowing from one minimum to one maximum. These cells segment images along ridges and valleys, and persistence decides which to merge.

6.1 Integral lines and cells

Definition 6.1 — Integral lines, ascending and descending manifolds Let f be a Morse function on a closed Riemannian manifold M. An integral line is a maximal curve \gamma with \gamma'(t) = \nabla f(\gamma(t)). Through each regular point passes exactly one integral line; it starts and ends at critical points.

For a critical point p, the descending manifold D(p) consists of p and the points whose integral line ends at p.

The ascending manifold A(p) consists of p and the points whose integral line starts at p.

By the stable manifold theorem, if p has index \lambda, then D(p) is an open cell of dimension \lambda and A(p) an open cell of dimension d - \lambda.

The descending manifolds partition M, and so do the ascending ones.

Definition 6.2 — Morse–Smale functions and complexes f is Morse–Smale if every ascending manifold meets every descending manifold transversally. The Morse–Smale complex has as cells the connected components of the sets D(p) \cap A(q), \quad p, q \text{ critical}.

Proposition 6.3 — The Morse–Smale complex of a surface On a closed surface, let f be Morse–Smale with m_0 minima, m_1 saddles and m_2 maxima.

  • Each saddle has four separatrices: two integral lines descending to minima and two ascending to maxima.
  • Each 2-cell is a quadrangle with corners minimum, saddle, maximum, saddle (Edelsbrunner, Harer and Zomorodian, 2003).
  • There are 4 m_1 arcs and 2 m_1 quadrangles.

Proof. The two first statements follow from the local model of a saddle and from the transversality of the flow (EHZ 2003).

For the counts, each arc is a separatrix and has exactly one saddle end, which gives 4 m_1 arcs. Each quadrangle has two saddle corners, and each saddle is a corner of the four sectors between its separatrices. Hence 2 \times \#\text{quadrangles} = 4 m_1.

Euler’s formula on this cell decomposition then reads (m_0 + m_1 + m_2) - 4 m_1 + 2 m_1 = m_0 - m_1 + m_2 = \chi(M), which is Theorem 3.4 again. ∎

For \cos 2\pi x + \cos 2\pi y on the torus, m = (1, 2, 1): the complex has 4 nodes, 8 arcs and 4 quadrangles.

6.2 Watersheds

The ascending manifolds A(m) of the minima are the catchment basins of f: the points whose steepest descent ends at m. Their boundaries form the watershed used in image segmentation (Vincent and Soille, 1991). Symmetrically, the descending manifolds D(M) of the maxima of an image are the regions that climb to each bright peak.

On a grid, steepest ascent replaces the gradient: each pixel points to its highest neighbour, and the regions are the trees of these pointers. Each local maximum then has a region, and noise makes hundreds of them.

6.3 Discrete Morse–Smale complexes

Integrating integral lines numerically is fragile at saddles. The discrete gradients of Section 3.3 replace them: V-paths from critical edges to critical vertices are the descending separatrices, and dual paths to critical triangles the ascending ones. Gyulassy, Bremer, Hamann and Pascucci (2008) and Shivashankar and Natarajan (2012) compute such complexes in 3D and in parallel; TTK implements them.

6.4 Persistence-driven simplification

A saddle and an adjacent extremum connected by a single separatrix can be cancelled: the two critical points disappear, and the cells on both sides merge. Cancelling pairs in increasing order of persistence gives a hierarchy of Morse–Smale complexes (Edelsbrunner, Harer and Zomorodian, 2003).

In terms of regions, cancelling a maximum with the saddle where it meets an older peak merges its region into the older one. This is exactly the elder rule of Proposition 4.3, applied with a threshold \tau.

Code 6.1 — Segmentation by simplified descending manifolds On the noisy image of Code 4.2, steepest ascent assigns each pixel to a maximum. Whenever two regions meet at a pixel v and the lower peak exceeds f(v) by less than \tau, the lower region merges into the higher one. The result is compared with the regions of the clean image.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

def segment(f, tau):
    """Descending manifolds of the maxima of f on the 4-neighbour grid, by steepest
    ascent, with the regions of maxima of persistence < tau merged (elder rule)."""
    n, m = f.shape
    flat = f.ravel()
    parent = -np.ones(n * m, dtype=int)
    def find(a):
        while parent[a] != a:
            parent[a] = parent[parent[a]]
            a = parent[a]
        return a
    for v in np.argsort(-flat, kind="stable"):          # decreasing values
        i, j = divmod(v, m)
        nb = [a * m + b for a, b in [(i - 1, j), (i + 1, j), (i, j - 1), (i, j + 1)]
              if 0 <= a < n and 0 <= b < m and parent[a * m + b] >= 0]
        if not nb:
            parent[v] = v                               # a new maximum
            continue
        parent[v] = find(max(nb, key=lambda u: flat[u]))  # steepest ascent
        for u in nb:
            ru, rv = find(u), find(v)
            if ru != rv and flat[v] > min(flat[ru], flat[rv]) - tau:   # low persistence
                young, old = (ru, rv) if flat[ru] < flat[rv] else (rv, ru)
                parent[young] = old
    return np.array([find(v) for v in range(n * m)]).reshape(n, m)

n = 64
x, y = np.meshgrid(np.arange(n), np.arange(n), indexing="ij")
bump = lambda cx, cy, h: h * np.exp(-((x - cx) ** 2 + (y - cy) ** 2) / (2 * 6.0 ** 2))
clean = bump(16, 16, 1.0) + bump(44, 20, 0.8) + bump(30, 48, 0.6)
img = clean + 0.05 * np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(n, n))
truth = segment(clean, 0.0)
for tau in [0.0, 0.1, 0.25]:
    lab = segment(img, tau)
    ari = adjusted_rand_score(truth.ravel(), lab.ravel())
    print(f"tau = {tau:.2f}: {len(np.unique(lab)):3d} regions, "
          f"adjusted Rand index with the clean segmentation {ari:.3f}")
print("clean image:", len(np.unique(truth)), "regions")

The clean image has 3 regions. On the noisy image, \tau = 0 gives 721 regions (adjusted Rand index 0.008), \tau = 0.1 gives 66 (0.337), and \tau = 0.25, chosen in the gap of Code 4.2, gives 3 regions (0.844).

The remaining disagreement lies in the flat background, where noise moves the boundaries.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Integral lines of the gradient partition the domain into ascending and descending manifolds; their intersections form the Morse–Smale complex.
  • On a surface, every cell is a quadrangle, and there are 4 m_1 arcs and 2 m_1 cells.
  • Catchment basins are the ascending manifolds of minima; regions of bright features are the descending manifolds of maxima.
  • Discrete gradients compute these complexes exactly; cancelling pairs by persistence simplifies them, which removes the over-segmentation due to noise.

7. Topological inference in high dimensions

A point cloud in \mathbb{R}^{100} cannot be looked at, but its homology can be estimated. This chapter gives conditions under which the homology of a sampled shape is recovered, bounds the effect of noise and outliers, and turns persistence into a clustering algorithm.

7.1 Offsets, reach and sampling

Definition 7.1 — Medial axis and reach The medial axis of a compact set K \subset \mathbb{R}^d is the set of points with at least two nearest points in K. The reach \tau(K) is the distance from K to its medial axis (Federer, 1959).

A circle of radius R has reach R: its medial axis is the centre. The boundary of a square has reach 0, since its medial axis, the diagonals, reaches the corners. Closed smooth submanifolds have positive reach.

Theorem 7.2 — Sampling theorem (Niyogi, Smale and Weinberger, 2008) Let M \subset \mathbb{R}^d be a compact submanifold of reach \tau, and X \subset M a finite set with M \subseteq X^{\varepsilon / 2}. If \varepsilon < \sqrt{3/5}\; \tau , then X^\varepsilon deformation retracts onto M. Consequently, \check C(X, \varepsilon) has the homology of M.

The retraction is the straight segment from each point to its projection onto M; the nerve theorem transfers the result to the Čech complex. Chazal, Cohen-Steiner and Lieutier (2009) extend it to compact sets of positive \mu-reach, which include sets of zero reach such as polygons.

7.2 Inference with Rips complexes

The Rips complex is not homotopy equivalent to the union of balls, but two Rips complexes at different scales are enough.

Lemma 7.3 — Sandwich lemma Let A \to B \to C \to D \to E be linear maps. If the composites A \to C and C \to E are isomorphisms, then \operatorname{rank}(B \to D) = \dim C .

Proof.

  • A \to C is onto and factors through B, so B \to C is onto;
  • C \to E is one-to-one and factors through D, so C \to D is one-to-one;
  • hence the image of B \to D is the image of C under C \to D, of dimension \dim C. ∎

Proposition 7.4 — Homology inference from two Rips complexes Let X \subset \mathbb{R}^d be finite. Suppose that the inclusions X^r \subseteq X^{\sqrt 2 r} \subseteq X^{2r} induce isomorphisms in homology. Then the rank of H_k(R(X, r)) \to H_k(R(X, \sqrt 2 r)) equals \beta_k(X^{\sqrt 2 r}).

In barcode terms: it is the number of Rips intervals of degree k that contain [r, \sqrt 2 r].

Proof. Theorem 1.7 gives the chain of inclusions \check C(X, r) \subseteq R(X, r) \subseteq \check C(X, \sqrt 2 r) \subseteq R(X, \sqrt 2 r) \subseteq \check C(X, 2r). By the nerve theorem, the Čech complexes have the homology of the offsets, and the maps between them are induced by the inclusions of offsets. Lemma 7.3 concludes. ∎

The hypothesis holds for a submanifold sampled as in Theorem 7.2 when r \geq \varepsilon and 2r < \sqrt{3/5}\, \tau: the three offsets then retract onto M, compatibly with the inclusions. For samples of more general compact sets, Chazal and Oudot (2008) prove a variant with a longer chain of scales.

7.3 Stability of Rips persistence

Theorem 7.5 — Stability of Rips diagrams For finite metric spaces X and Y, with the Rips filtration indexed by r = \text{diameter} / 2, and every degree k, d_B\big( \mathrm{Dgm}_k R(X), \mathrm{Dgm}_k R(Y) \big) \;\leq\; d_{GH}(X, Y) . For two subsets of the same metric space, the right-hand side may be replaced by d_H(X, Y).

This is due to Chazal, Cohen-Steiner, Guibas, Mémoli and Oudot (2009) and Chazal, de Silva and Oudot (2014); Section 10.2 gives the proof. Two \varepsilon-dense samples of the same shape thus have diagrams within \varepsilon of each other. But a single outlier can change d_H, hence the diagram, by an arbitrary amount.

7.4 Distance to a measure

Definition 7.6 — Distance to a measure (DTM) Let \mu be a probability measure on \mathbb{R}^d and m \in (0, 1] a mass parameter. With \delta_{\mu, u}(x) = \inf \{ r : \mu(\bar B(x, r)) > u \}, the distance to the measure is d_{\mu, m}(x)^2 = \frac{1}{m} \int_0^m \delta_{\mu, u}(x)^2 \, du . For the empirical measure of n points and m = k / n, it is the root mean square of the distances from x to its k nearest sample points.

Theorem 7.7 — Properties of the DTM (Chazal, Cohen-Steiner and Mérigot, 2011) The function d_{\mu, m} is 1-Lipschitz, and x \mapsto \|x\|^2 - d_{\mu, m}(x)^2 is convex, like for a distance function. Moreover, \| d_{\mu, m} - d_{\nu, m} \|_\infty \;\leq\; \frac{1}{\sqrt m}\, W_2(\mu, \nu).

Moving one of n sample points by a distance L changes W_2 by at most L / \sqrt n, hence the empirical DTM by at most L / \sqrt k, everywhere (Exercise 7). The sampling theorems of Section 7.1 extend to its sublevel sets.

Code 7.1 — Outliers, distance function and DTM Five hundred points are sampled near the unit circle, then 25 uniform outliers (5 %) are added in [-1.5, 1.5]^2. The distance function and the DTM with k = 50 are evaluated on a 61 \times 61 grid, and the H_1 diagram of their sublevel sets is computed with the lower-star filtration of Proposition 1.11 and Algorithm 4.4.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

def lower_star_h1(F):
    """Finite H1 intervals of the sublevel sets of F on the Freudenthal triangulation
    of a grid (lower-star filtration: a simplex enters with its highest vertex)."""
    n, m = F.shape
    v = lambda i, j: i * m + j
    S = [((v(i, j),), F[i, j]) for i in range(n) for j in range(m)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(m):
            for a, b in [(i + 1, j), (i, j + 1), (i + 1, j + 1)]:
                if a < n and b < m:
                    S.append(((v(i, j), v(a, b)), max(F[i, j], F[a, b])))
            if i + 1 < n and j + 1 < m:
                for c in [(i + 1, j), (i, j + 1)]:
                    tri = (v(i, j), v(*c), v(i + 1, j + 1))
                    S.append((tuple(sorted(tri)), max(F[i, j], F[c], F[i + 1, j + 1])))
    S.sort(key=lambda s: (s[1], len(s[0])))
    index = {s: k for k, (s, _) in enumerate(S)}
    low_of, cols, out = {}, [], []
    for k, (s, val) in enumerate(S):
        col = {index[s[:i] + s[i + 1:]] for i in range(len(s))} if len(s) > 1 else set()
        while col and max(col) in low_of:
            col ^= cols[low_of[max(col)]]
        cols.append(col)
        if col:
            low_of[max(col)] = k
            if len(s) == 3 and val > S[max(col)][1]:
                out.append((S[max(col)][1], val))
    return sorted(out, key=lambda p: p[0] - p[1])

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 500)
X = np.c_[np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)] + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=(500, 2))
Y = np.r_[X, rng.uniform(-1.5, 1.5, size=(25, 2))]          # add 5 % of uniform outliers
g = np.linspace(-1.5, 1.5, 61)
Z = np.stack(np.meshgrid(g, g, indexing="ij"), axis=-1).reshape(-1, 2)
dist, _ = cKDTree(Y).query(Z, k=50)                        # 50 nearest points: mass m = 50 / 525
functions = {"distance, clean": cKDTree(X).query(Z)[0],
             "distance, outliers": dist[:, 0],
             "DTM, outliers": np.sqrt((dist ** 2).mean(axis=1))}
for name, f in functions.items():
    bars = lower_star_h1(f.reshape(61, 61))
    pers = [d - b for b, d in bars]
    print(f"{name:18s}: longest H1 interval [{bars[0][0]:.3f}, {bars[0][1]:.3f}), "
          f"persistence {pers[0]:.3f}; intervals longer than 0.05: {sum(p > 0.05 for p in pers)}")

Without outliers, the distance function has one long interval, [0.055, 0.857), of persistence 0.802. With the outliers, its longest interval shrinks to [0.055, 0.490), persistence 0.435, and a second interval longer than 0.05 appears. The DTM keeps a single long interval, [0.256, 0.885), of persistence 0.629.

Outliers inside the circle fill its hole early in the offsets of the distance function. For the DTM they carry too little mass: the hole dies at 0.885, close to the clean 0.857.

7.5 Persistence-based clustering (ToMATo)

An estimated density has many spurious local maxima, like the image of Code 4.2. ToMATo (Chazal, Guibas, Oudot and Skraba, 2013) keeps the prominent ones.

Algorithm 7.8 — ToMATo Input: a neighbourhood graph on the points, a density estimate \hat f at each point, a threshold \tau.

  1. Process the points by decreasing \hat f; attach each point to the cluster of its densest processed neighbour, or start a new cluster if it has none (graph-based hill climbing).
  2. When a point v touches two clusters, the one with the lower peak r merges into the other if \hat f(r) - \hat f(v) < \tau (elder rule).

The output has one cluster per connected component of the graph, plus one per other peak of prominence at least \tau. The prominences of all peaks form the 0-dimensional diagram of the superlevel sets of \hat f, and \tau is chosen in its largest gap.

Under regularity assumptions, the diagram of \hat f is close to that of the true density, and the prominent peaks and their clusters are recovered (Chazal, Guibas, Oudot and Skraba, 2013).

Code 7.2 — ToMATo on two moons The code runs ToMATo once with \tau = \infty to record the merge history and the prominence of every peak, then resolves the clusters for the \tau in the largest gap of the prominences. The data come from make_moons (scikit-learn); density and graph use k nearest neighbours.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

def tomato(X, k):
    """ToMATo with tau = infinity: hill climbing on the k-NN graph of a k-NN density estimate.
    Returns the peak of each point, the merge history and the prominence of each merged peak."""
    dist, nbrs = cKDTree(X).query(X, k=k + 1)
    dens = 1.0 / dist[:, -1] ** X.shape[1]                     # k-NN density, up to a constant
    rank = np.empty(len(X), dtype=int)
    rank[np.argsort(-dens, kind="stable")] = np.arange(len(X))
    parent, peak, merged_into, prominence = -np.ones(len(X), dtype=int), {}, {}, {}
    def find(a):
        while parent[a] != a:
            parent[a] = parent[parent[a]]
            a = parent[a]
        return a
    for v in np.argsort(rank):                                 # decreasing density
        up = [u for u in nbrs[v, 1:] if rank[u] < rank[v]]
        parent[v] = v if not up else find(min(up, key=lambda u: rank[u]))
        peak[v] = parent[v]                                    # the hill v climbs to
        for u in up:
            ru, rv = find(u), find(v)
            if ru != rv:                                       # elder rule
                young, old = (ru, rv) if rank[ru] > rank[rv] else (rv, ru)
                merged_into[young], prominence[young] = old, dens[young] - dens[v]
                parent[young] = old
    return peak, merged_into, prominence

def labels_at(peak, merged_into, prominence, tau):
    """Merge every peak of prominence < tau into its elder; one label per point."""
    def resolve(p):
        while p in merged_into and prominence[p] < tau:
            p = merged_into[p]
        return p
    return np.array([resolve(peak[v]) for v in range(len(peak))])

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=600, noise=0.06, random_state=0)
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(X)
print(f"k-means with 2 clusters: ARI = {adjusted_rand_score(y, km):.3f}")
for k in [15, 30]:
    peak, merged_into, prominence = tomato(X, k)
    top = np.sort(list(prominence.values()))[::-1]
    gap = np.argmax(top[:-1] - top[1:])                        # largest gap of the diagram
    labels = labels_at(peak, merged_into, prominence, tau=(top[gap] + top[gap + 1]) / 2)
    print(f"ToMATo, k = {k}: prominences / largest {np.round(top[:3] / top[0], 3)}, "
          f"{len(np.unique(labels))} clusters, ARI = {adjusted_rand_score(y, labels):.3f}")

k-means reaches an adjusted Rand index of 0.255: the moons are not convex.

With k = 15, the graph already has two connected components, one per moon. The finite prominences, in the ratios 1, 0.706, 0.418, show no clear gap, and the cut in the largest one keeps a spurious peak that splits a moon: 3 clusters (ARI 0.750).

With k = 30, the graph is connected and the ratios are 1, 0.574, 0.437; ToMATo returns 2 clusters, with ARI 0.987.

The density estimate must be smooth enough. When no gap stands out, as at k = 15, the diagram warns that \tau is not determined by the data.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • A dense enough sample of a set of positive reach has offsets, hence Čech complexes, with the homology of the set (Theorem 7.2).
  • Two Rips complexes at scales r and \sqrt 2 r give the homology through the rank of the map between them.
  • Rips diagrams are stable: d_B \leq d_{GH}, and d_B \leq d_H for subsets of one space.
  • The distance to a measure is stable in Wasserstein distance, which makes inference robust to outliers.
  • ToMATo clusters by the prominent peaks of a density, read in its persistence diagram.

8. Topological signatures and features for learning

A persistence diagram is a multiset, not a vector: it cannot be averaged or fed to a linear model as it stands. This chapter maps diagrams to vectors and kernels without losing stability, and differentiates through them.

8.1 The space of diagrams

Diagrams with d_B or W_q form a metric space. Fréchet means exist (Mileyko, Mukherjee and Harer, 2011) but need not be unique, and the space does not embed bi-Lipschitz into a Hilbert space (Carrière and Bauer, 2019). Learning therefore goes through stable vectorizations or kernels.

8.2 Betti curves and landscapes

Definition 8.1 — Betti curves and persistence landscapes The Betti curve of a diagram D is t \mapsto \#\{(b, d) \in D : b \leq t < d\}. For a point p = (b, d), the tent function is \Lambda_p(t) = \max\big(0, \min(t - b,\ d - t)\big). The k-th landscape \lambda_k(t) is the k-th largest value among the \Lambda_p(t), p \in D, or 0 if there are fewer than k points (Bubenik, 2015).

The Betti curve counts every short bar and is not stable. Landscapes are.

Theorem 8.2 — Stability of landscapes For finite diagrams D, D' and every k \geq 1,

Key result — landscapes are 1-Lipschitz \| \lambda_k(D) - \lambda_k(D') \|_\infty \;\leq\; d_B(D, D')

Proof. Let \gamma be a matching that attains d_B = \varepsilon; a point matched to the diagonal is matched to its projection, whose tent is zero.

The functions (b, d) \mapsto t - b and (b, d) \mapsto d - t are 1-Lipschitz for the sup norm, and so are minima and maxima of such functions. Hence, for every p and t, |\Lambda_p(t) - \Lambda_{\gamma(p)}(t)| \leq \varepsilon .

At fixed t, the two families of values \Lambda_p(t) and \Lambda_{\gamma(p)}(t) thus differ by at most \varepsilon entry by entry. The k-th largest entry of a vector is 1-Lipschitz for the sup norm, which concludes. ∎

Landscapes can be averaged; mean landscapes satisfy a law of large numbers and a central limit theorem (Bubenik, 2015).

8.3 Persistence images

Definition 8.3 — Persistence images Map each point (b, d) to (b, d - b), birth and persistence. Place on it a Gaussian of width \sigma, weighted by a function w that vanishes at zero persistence, for instance w(b, \ell) = \min(1, \ell / \ell_{\max}). The persistence image is the integral of the sum of these weighted Gaussians over each pixel of a fixed grid (Adams and coauthors, 2017).

The weight makes the image continuous in the diagram, and persistence images are stable for the 1-Wasserstein distance (Adams and coauthors, 2017).

8.4 Kernels on diagrams

Definition 8.4 — Two kernels on diagrams

  • The persistence scale-space kernel (Reininghaus, Huber, Bauer and Kwitt, 2015) is the L^2 inner product of heat diffusions started from the points of each diagram, with a negative copy reflected across the diagonal: k_\sigma(D, E) = \frac{1}{8\pi\sigma} \sum_{p \in D,\, q \in E} \Big( e^{-\|p - q\|^2 / 8\sigma} - e^{-\|p - \bar q\|^2 / 8\sigma} \Big), where \bar q is the reflection of q.
  • The sliced Wasserstein distance SW(D, E) (Carrière, Cuturi and Oudot, 2017) averages over directions \theta the 1D transport cost between the projections of D \cup \pi_\Delta(E) and E \cup \pi_\Delta(D) on the line of direction \theta, where \pi_\Delta projects onto the diagonal.

The first kernel is an inner product, hence positive definite. For the second, transport on a line is an L^1 distance between cumulative distribution functions, which is conditionally negative definite; by Schoenberg’s theorem k(D, E) = \exp\big( -SW(D, E) / 2\sigma^2 \big) is positive definite, ready for a support vector machine.

Code 8.1 — Telling a circle from an open arc Each sample has 30 noisy points, either on a full circle or on an arc missing a quarter, with a random radius between 0.8 and 1.2. The features are the first two H_1 landscapes of the Rips filtration (computed as in Code 4.1) on 60 values of r. They are compared with a histogram of the pairwise distances, in a logistic regression with 5-fold cross-validation.

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

def h1_diagram(P, rmax=1.2):
    """Finite H1 intervals of the Rips filtration (r = diameter / 2), as in Code 4.1."""
    D = np.linalg.norm(P[:, None] - P[None], axis=2) / 2
    n = len(P)
    S = [((i,), 0.0) for i in range(n)]
    S += [((i, j), D[i, j]) for i, j in combinations(range(n), 2) if D[i, j] <= rmax]
    S += [((i, j, k), max(D[i, j], D[i, k], D[j, k])) for i, j, k in combinations(range(n), 3)
          if max(D[i, j], D[i, k], D[j, k]) <= rmax]
    S.sort(key=lambda s: (s[1], len(s[0])))
    index = {s: i for i, (s, _) in enumerate(S)}
    low_of, cols, out = {}, [], []
    for j, (s, val) in enumerate(S):
        col = {index[s[:i] + s[i + 1:]] for i in range(len(s))} if len(s) > 1 else set()
        while col and max(col) in low_of:
            col ^= cols[low_of[max(col)]]
        cols.append(col)
        if col:
            low_of[max(col)] = j
            if len(s) == 3 and val > S[max(col)][1]:
                out.append((S[max(col)][1], val))
    return np.array(out).reshape(-1, 2)

def landscape(dgm, grid, K=2):
    """First K persistence landscapes on a grid: k-th largest tent max(0, min(t - b, d - t))."""
    tents = np.maximum(0, np.minimum(grid[None] - dgm[:, :1], dgm[:, 1:] - grid[None]))
    tents = np.vstack([np.sort(tents, axis=0)[::-1], np.zeros((K, len(grid)))])
    return tents[:K].ravel()

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def sample(label):
    """30 noisy points on a circle (label 0) or on an arc missing a quarter (label 1)."""
    span = 2 * np.pi if label == 0 else 1.5 * np.pi
    t = rng.uniform(0, span, 30)
    R = rng.uniform(0.8, 1.2)
    return R * np.c_[np.cos(t), np.sin(t)] + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=(30, 2))

labels = np.repeat([0, 1], 40)
clouds = [sample(c) for c in labels]
grid = np.linspace(0, 1.2, 60)
topo = np.array([landscape(h1_diagram(P), grid) for P in clouds])
hist = np.array([np.histogram(np.linalg.norm(P[:, None] - P[None], axis=2)[np.triu_indices(30, 1)],
                              bins=30, range=(0, 2.6), density=True)[0] for P in clouds])
for name, F in [("H1 landscapes", topo), ("distance histogram", hist)]:
    acc = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(C=10, max_iter=5000), F, labels, cv=5)
    print(f"{name:18s}: 5-fold accuracy {acc.mean():.3f} +/- {acc.std():.3f}")

The landscapes reach an accuracy of 0.938 ± 0.068, against 0.725 ± 0.031 for the distance histogram.

The histogram sees the missing quarter only through a few long distances, blurred by the random radius; the landscape sees whether a long H_1 bar exists.

8.5 Learned layers and differentiable persistence

PersLay (Carrière, Chazal, Ike, Lacombe, Royer and Umeda, 2020) is a neural layer of the form D \mapsto \operatorname{op}_{p \in D}\; w(p)\, \varphi(p), where \operatorname{op} is a sum, a maximum or a k-th largest value, and w and \varphi are learned; landscapes and persistence images are special cases.

Persistence can also sit inside a model, when a filtration depends on parameters \theta such as pixel values or point positions.

Proposition 8.5 — Diagrams are differentiable almost everywhere Let \theta \mapsto f_\theta be continuously differentiable. Suppose that, near \theta_0, the order of the simplices by value, ties broken by dimension and then by a fixed rule, does not depend on \theta.

This holds for a lower-star filtration whose vertex values are distinct at \theta_0, and for a Rips filtration whose pairwise distances are distinct.

Then the pairing is constant near \theta_0, each point of the diagram is a differentiable function of \theta, and for a loss L of the diagram, \nabla_\theta L = \sum_{(\sigma, \tau)} \Big( \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\, \nabla_\theta f_\theta(\sigma) + \frac{\partial L}{\partial d}\, \nabla_\theta f_\theta(\tau) \Big).

Proof. The pairs of Theorem 4.5 depend only on the order of the simplices. In the two examples, simplices with different highest vertices (or longest edges) are ordered by strict inequalities, which persist near \theta_0; the others are ordered by the fixed rule. Each coordinate of each point is then one of the C^1 functions f_\theta(\sigma), and the chain rule gives the formula. ∎

Stochastic subgradient descent converges for such losses (Carrière, Chazal, Glisse, Ike, Kannan and Umeda, 2021). They enforce connectivity in segmentation (Hu, Li, Samaras and Chen, 2019) and regularize models (Brüel-Gabrielsson, Nelson, Dwaraknath and Skraba, 2020).

Code 8.2 — Topological simplification by gradient descent A noisy sine f_0 on 300 samples has many local minima. The loss sums the squared persistences of all finite H_0 pairs except the two largest, plus a fidelity term. The pairing is recomputed by union-find at every step, and PyTorch differentiates through the paired values (Proposition 8.5).

import numpy as np
import torch

torch.set_num_threads(1)

def h0_pairs(f):
    """Index pairs (birth vertex, death vertex) of the finite 0-dim sublevel intervals."""
    parent, pairs = -np.ones(len(f), dtype=int), []
    def find(a):
        while parent[a] != a:
            a = parent[a]
        return a
    for v in np.argsort(f, kind="stable"):
        parent[v] = v
        for u in (v - 1, v + 1):
            if 0 <= u < len(f) and parent[u] >= 0:
                ru, rv = find(u), find(v)
                if ru != rv:
                    young, old = (ru, rv) if f[ru] > f[rv] else (rv, ru)
                    if f[young] < f[v]:
                        pairs.append((young, v))
                    parent[young] = old
    return np.array(pairs, dtype=int).reshape(-1, 2)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.linspace(0, 1, 300)
f0 = torch.tensor(np.sin(6 * np.pi * t) + 0.15 * rng.normal(size=t.size))
x = f0.clone().requires_grad_(True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([x], lr=0.01)
keep = 2                                          # keep the two most persistent finite pairs
print("local minima before:", len(h0_pairs(f0.numpy())) + 1)
for step in range(300):
    pairs = h0_pairs(x.detach().numpy())          # the pairing is piecewise constant in x
    pers = x[pairs[:, 1]] - x[pairs[:, 0]]        # death - birth, differentiable in x
    small = torch.sort(pers, descending=True).values[keep:]
    loss = (small ** 2).sum() + 0.1 * ((x - f0) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
pairs = h0_pairs(x.detach().numpy())
pers = np.sort(x.detach().numpy()[pairs[:, 1]] - x.detach().numpy()[pairs[:, 0]])[::-1]
print(f"after 300 steps: largest persistences {', '.join(f'{p:.3f}' for p in pers[:4])}, "
      f"pairs above 0.05: {(pers > 0.05).sum()}, |x - f0|_inf = {(x - f0).abs().max():.3f}")

The noisy signal has 94 local minima. After 300 steps, the largest finite persistences are 2.657, 2.384, 0.016 and 0.002: two finite pairs remain above 0.05. The signal moved by at most 0.615.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Diagrams form a metric space without a linear structure; learning uses stable vectorizations or kernels.
  • Landscapes are 1-Lipschitz for the bottleneck distance and can be averaged; persistence images are stable for W_1.
  • The persistence scale-space and sliced Wasserstein kernels are positive definite.
  • Diagrams are differentiable wherever the order of the simplices is locally constant, so topological losses can be trained.

9. Implementations in Python and C++

The algorithms of this course are simple, but naive implementations do not scale: a Rips complex has a cubic number of triangles, and the reduction is cubic in the number of simplices. This chapter, for self-study alongside the labs, presents the data structures and the algebraic shortcuts used by the Gudhi and TTK libraries, and runs two of them.

9.1 Data structures

Definition 9.1 — Simplex tree Order the vertices. A simplex tree stores each simplex as the path of its sorted vertices in a trie: the children of a node are the larger vertices that extend it into a simplex. Each node holds the filtration value of its simplex (Boissonnat and Maria, 2014).

A simplex of dimension k is found in O(k) steps along its path, and its faces are reached by removing one vertex. A Rips complex is built by computing the neighbourhood graph and then expanding it: the cofaces of a node are the common neighbours of its vertices.

Proposition 9.2 — The size of Rips complexes For n points and a radius larger than half the diameter, the Rips complex up to dimension 2 has n + \binom{n}{2} + \binom{n}{3} \sim \frac{n^3}{6} simplices. For n = 30 that is 4525 simplices; for n = 10^4, about 1.7 \times 10^{11}.

Proof. Every pair and every triple of points has diameter at most twice the radius. ∎

Three remedies keep the size manageable: stop the filtration at a radius r_{\max}; use the alpha complex, of size O(n) in the plane and O(n^2) in the worst case in 3D; or simplify the complex while preserving its persistence, for instance by edge collapses (Boissonnat and Pritam, 2020).

9.2 Faster reductions

Lemma 9.3 — Clearing If i = \mathrm{low}(j) for a reduced column j, then column i reduces to zero, so it can be zeroed without any work.

Proof. R_j = \partial V_j is a cycle whose last simplex is \sigma_i. So \sigma_i closes a cycle when it enters: it is positive, and adding it does not raise the rank of the boundary matrix. Its reduced column is therefore zero. ∎

Processing the dimensions from the top down lets every pivot found in dimension k + 1 clear a column of dimension k: this is the twist algorithm (Chen and Kerber, 2011).

Theorem 9.4 — Persistent cohomology has the same barcode Over a field, the barcode of the persistent cohomology of a filtration equals the barcode of its persistent homology (de Silva, Morozov and Vejdemo-Johansson, 2011). The pairs can therefore be computed by reducing the coboundary matrix, the anti-transpose of \partial.

For a Rips filtration, cohomology runs the clearing in the favourable direction: dimension 0 first, then dimension 1, so that the many triangles that create no class are never touched. Ripser (Bauer, 2021) combines cohomology, clearing, an implicit coboundary matrix computed on the fly, and apparent pairs detected without reduction.

Code 9.1 — Homology, twist and cohomology on the same filtration The code builds the full 2-skeleton of the Rips filtration of the 30 points of Code 1.1 and computes the persistence pairs three times: the standard reduction (Algorithm 4.4), the twist algorithm, and the reduction of the coboundary matrix with clearing. It checks that the pairs coincide and counts the column additions.

import time
import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 30)
P = np.c_[np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)] + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=(30, 2))
D = np.linalg.norm(P[:, None] - P[None], axis=2) / 2
S = [((i,), 0.0) for i in range(30)]
S += [((i, j), D[i, j]) for i, j in combinations(range(30), 2)]
S += [((i, j, k), max(D[i, j], D[i, k], D[j, k])) for i, j, k in combinations(range(30), 3)]
S.sort(key=lambda s: (s[1], len(s[0])))                 # the full 2-skeleton, 4525 simplices
index = {s: i for i, (s, _) in enumerate(S)}
bd = [{index[s[:i] + s[i + 1:]] for i in range(len(s))} if len(s) > 1 else set() for s, _ in S]
cobd = [set() for _ in S]
for j, faces in enumerate(bd):
    for i in faces:
        cobd[i].add(j)
by_dim = [[j for j, (s, _) in enumerate(S) if len(s) == d + 1] for d in range(3)]

def homology(order, clearing):
    """Reduce boundary columns (pivot = largest row) in the given order."""
    low_of, cols, adds, t0 = {}, {}, 0, time.perf_counter()
    for j in order:
        if clearing and j in low_of:
            continue                                     # positive simplex: column is zero
        col = set(bd[j])
        while col and max(col) in low_of:
            col ^= cols[low_of[max(col)]]
            adds += 1
        cols[j] = col
        if col:
            low_of[max(col)] = j
    return {(i, j) for i, j in low_of.items()}, adds, time.perf_counter() - t0

def cohomology():
    """Reduce coboundary columns (pivot = smallest row), dimension 0 then 1, with clearing."""
    piv_of, cols, adds, t0 = {}, {}, 0, time.perf_counter()
    for d in (0, 1):
        for j in reversed(by_dim[d]):
            if j in piv_of:
                continue                                 # cleared: j already died
            col = set(cobd[j])
            while col and min(col) in piv_of:
                col ^= cols[piv_of[min(col)]]
                adds += 1
            cols[j] = col
            if col:
                piv_of[min(col)] = j
    return {(j, k) for k, j in piv_of.items()}, adds, time.perf_counter() - t0

ref, _, _ = homology(range(len(S)), False)
runs = [("homology, standard", homology(range(len(S)), False)),
        ("homology, twist", homology(by_dim[2] + by_dim[1], True)),
        ("cohomology, clearing", cohomology())]
for name, (pairs, adds, sec) in runs:
    print(f"{name:21s}: same pairs {pairs == ref}, {adds:5d} column additions, {sec:.3f} s")

All three computations return the same 435 pairs. The standard reduction performs 39,132 column additions, the twist algorithm 37,291, and the cohomology with clearing only 41, in a few milliseconds.

The twist saves little here because the expensive columns are the 4060 triangles, which nothing clears in homology. In cohomology, the triangles are never reduced at all: only the 406 edges that create loops are processed, and most of their columns are reduced already.

9.4 A union-find in C++

The sweep of Proposition 4.3 is the inner loop of merge trees, watersheds and ToMATo. In C++ it runs on millions of vertices per second.

Code 9.2 — Sublevel-set persistence of a signal in C++ Compile with g++ -std=c++17 -O2 persistence.cpp. The signal is deterministic, so the output is reproducible.

// 0-dimensional sublevel-set persistence of a sampled signal: union-find + elder rule.
#include <algorithm>
#include <cmath>
#include <cstdio>
#include <numeric>
#include <vector>

struct UnionFind {
  std::vector<int> parent;
  explicit UnionFind(int n) : parent(n, -1) {}
  int find(int a) {                       // path halving
    while (parent[a] != a) a = parent[a] = parent[parent[a]];
    return a;
  }
};

int main() {
  const int n = 400;
  const double pi = std::acos(-1.0);
  std::vector<double> f(n);
  for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) {           // a deterministic "noisy" signal
    double t = i / (n - 1.0);
    f[i] = std::sin(6 * pi * t) + 0.1 * std::sin(173 * t) + 0.05 * std::sin(517 * t);
  }
  std::vector<int> order(n);
  std::iota(order.begin(), order.end(), 0);
  std::stable_sort(order.begin(), order.end(), [&](int a, int b) { return f[a] < f[b]; });

  UnionFind uf(n);
  std::vector<std::pair<double, double>> pairs;   // (birth, death)
  for (int v : order) {
    uf.parent[v] = v;
    for (int u : {v - 1, v + 1}) {
      if (u < 0 || u >= n || uf.parent[u] < 0) continue;
      int ru = uf.find(u), rv = uf.find(v);
      if (ru == rv) continue;
      int young = f[ru] > f[rv] ? ru : rv, old = young == ru ? rv : ru;
      if (f[young] < f[v]) pairs.emplace_back(f[young], f[v]);
      uf.parent[young] = old;               // elder rule
    }
  }
  std::sort(pairs.begin(), pairs.end(), [](auto& a, auto& b) {
    return a.second - a.first > b.second - b.first;
  });
  std::printf("%zu finite pairs; the three most persistent:\n", pairs.size());
  for (int k = 0; k < 3; ++k)
    std::printf("  [%.3f, %.3f)  persistence %.3f\n", pairs[k].first, pairs[k].second,
                pairs[k].second - pairs[k].first);
}

The program finds 61 finite pairs. The three most persistent are [-1.095, 1.097), [-1.093, 1.078) and [0.000, 1.102), of persistence 2.193, 2.171 and 1.102: the two deep minima of the sine, and the minimum at the left end of the signal.

10.1 The pairing lemma

Let \partial be the m \times m boundary matrix of a filtration (Section 4.3).

For indices i, j, let r_\partial(i, j) be the rank of its lower-left corner: the submatrix made of the rows i to m and the columns 1 to j.

Lemma 10.1 — Corner ranks are invariant Adding a column j' to a column j > j' does not change any r(i, j'').

Proof. If j'' < j, the corner does not contain column j and is unchanged. If j'' \geq j, it contains both columns j' and j, and adding one column of a matrix to another preserves its rank. ∎

Lemma 10.2 — Corner ranks of a reduced matrix If the non-zero columns of R have distinct lows, then r_R(i, j) = \#\{ j' \leq j : R_{j'} \neq 0,\ \mathrm{low}(j') \geq i \}.

Proof. In the corner, the columns with \mathrm{low}(j') < i vanish. The others keep their lowest 1, at distinct rows; sorted by these rows, they form a triangular family, hence are independent. ∎

Theorem 10.3 — Pairing lemma For any reduction R of \partial by Algorithm 4.4, \mathrm{low}(j) = i if and only if r_\partial(i, j) - r_\partial(i + 1, j) - r_\partial(i, j - 1) + r_\partial(i + 1, j - 1) = 1 . In particular, the persistence pairs do not depend on the reduction.

Proof. By Lemma 10.1, r_R = r_\partial. By Lemma 10.2, r_R(i, j) - r_R(i + 1, j) counts the columns j' \leq j with \mathrm{low}(j') = i.

Taking the difference between j and j - 1 leaves 1 exactly when \mathrm{low}(j) = i. ∎

To read the barcode from the pairs (Theorem 4.5), recall that V is upper triangular.

  • If R_i = 0, the column V_i is a cycle whose last simplex is \sigma_i: a class is born at i.
  • If moreover i = \mathrm{low}(j), the boundary R_j also ends with \sigma_i, so V_i - R_j is a cycle of K_{i-1}.
  • In K_j, the new class therefore equals an older one: it dies at j.

Counting these classes gives the persistent Betti numbers of Theorem 4.2 (Edelsbrunner and Harer, 2010, Chapter VII).

10.2 Interleavings and the isometry theorem

Definition 10.4 — Persistence modules over the reals and interleavings A persistence module V is a family of vector spaces (V_t)_{t \in \mathbb{R}} with linear maps v_s^t : V_s \to V_t for s \leq t, such that v_t^t = \mathrm{id} and v_s^t \circ v_r^s = v_r^t.

Two modules V and W are \varepsilon-interleaved if there are maps \varphi_t : V_t \to W_{t + \varepsilon} and \psi_t : W_t \to V_{t + \varepsilon}, commuting with the maps of the modules, such that \psi_{t + \varepsilon} \circ \varphi_t = v_t^{t + 2\varepsilon}, \qquad \varphi_{t + \varepsilon} \circ \psi_t = w_t^{t + 2\varepsilon}. The interleaving distance d_I(V, W) is the infimum of such \varepsilon.

Theorem 10.5 — Isometry theorem For modules whose maps v_s^t, s < t, have finite rank, d_B\big( \mathrm{Dgm}\, V, \mathrm{Dgm}\, W \big) = d_I(V, W). The inequality \leq, algebraic stability, is due to Chazal, Cohen-Steiner, Glisse, Guibas and Oudot (2009); the equality to Lesnick (2015).

Bauer and Lesnick (2015) give a direct proof: an \varepsilon-interleaving gives a morphism from V to W shifted by \varepsilon, whose kernel and cokernel die within 2\varepsilon, and the matching of barcodes it induces moves each endpoint by at most \varepsilon.

Corollary 10.6 — Stability for functions If \| f - g \|_\infty \leq \varepsilon for two monotone functions on a complex, their sublevel-set modules are \varepsilon-interleaved, so their diagrams are within \varepsilon in bottleneck distance. This is Theorem 4.10.

Proof. f(\sigma) \leq t implies g(\sigma) \leq t + \varepsilon, so K^f_t \subseteq K^g_{t + \varepsilon}, and symmetrically. The inclusions induce the maps \varphi_t and \psi_t, and every square commutes because all maps are induced by inclusions. ∎

Proposition 10.7 — Proof of Theorem 7.5 Let X, Y be finite subsets of a metric space with d_H(X, Y) = \varepsilon. Then the persistence modules of their Rips filtrations, indexed by r = \text{diameter}/2, are \varepsilon-interleaved.

Proof. Choose \varphi : X \to Y with d(x, \varphi(x)) \leq \varepsilon, and \psi : Y \to X likewise.

  1. If \sigma \in R(X, r), the diameter of \varphi(\sigma) is at most 2r + 2\varepsilon. So \varphi maps R(X, r) simplicially into R(Y, r + \varepsilon), compatibly with the inclusions.
  2. For x, x' \in \sigma, the triangle inequality gives d(x, \psi\varphi(x')) \leq 2r + 2\varepsilon, \qquad d(\psi\varphi(x), \psi\varphi(x')) \leq 2r + 4\varepsilon . So \sigma \cup \psi\varphi(\sigma) is a simplex of R(X, r + 2\varepsilon).
  3. Hence \psi \circ \varphi is contiguous to the inclusion, and by Theorem 2.9 they induce the same map on homology; the same holds for \varphi \circ \psi.

For the Gromov–Hausdorff version, replace the nearest points by a correspondence of distortion 2 d_{GH} (Chazal, de Silva and Oudot, 2014). ∎

10.3 Collapses and discrete Morse theory

Definition 10.8 — Free faces and collapses A face \sigma of a simplex \tau, of dimension one less, is free if \tau is the only simplex of K that strictly contains it. Removing the pair (\sigma, \tau) is an elementary collapse.

Proposition 10.9 — Collapses preserve the homotopy type If K' is obtained from K by an elementary collapse, then |K'| is a deformation retract of |K|.

Proof. Project \tau from a point just outside it, beyond the barycentre of \sigma, onto the union of its other facets. The projection fixes these facets, so it extends by the identity to the rest of K, and the straight-line homotopy to it stays in \tau. ∎

To prove Theorem 3.10, order the simplices so that faces come before cofaces and each gradient pair (\sigma, \tau) is consecutive; such an order exists exactly when there is no closed V-path. Build K in this order.

  • A pair (\sigma, \tau) enters while the other cofaces of \sigma are still absent, so \sigma is a free face of \tau: the step is an inverse elementary collapse and keeps the homotopy type.
  • A critical simplex of dimension p enters along its whole boundary: the step attaches a p-cell.

Hence K is homotopy equivalent to a CW complex with one p-cell per critical p-simplex (Forman, 1998).

10.4 The nerve theorem for convex sets

Let \mathcal{U} = (U_i) be a finite family of open convex sets with union U, and N its nerve.

  • From the nerve to the union. For each simplex J of N, choose a point p_J in the intersection of the sets U_j, j \in J.

    A simplex of the barycentric subdivision is a chain J_0 \subset \dots \subset J_k. Map its vertices to the points p_{J_i} and extend linearly. These points all lie in the convex set U_j for any j \in J_0, so the image lies in the union: this defines g.

  • From the union to the nerve. Take a partition of unity (\phi_i) on U subordinate to the open cover (U_i), and set h(x) = \sum_i \phi_i(x)\, e_i, where e_i is the vertex i.

    The indices with \phi_i(x) > 0 label sets that contain x, so they form a simplex and h(x) \in |N|.

  • Homotopies. The point h(x) lies in a simplex J_0 \subset \dots \subset J_k of the subdivision with \phi_j(x) > 0 for j \in J_0.

    So x and g(h(x)) both lie in the convex set U_j, and the straight segment between them stays in U: this gives g \circ h \simeq \mathrm{id}_U.

    A similar argument inside the simplices of N gives h \circ g \simeq \mathrm{id}.

Details are in Hatcher (2002, Section 4.G). Closed convex sets, such as the closed balls of the Čech complex, need one more step: Borsuk (1948) proves the nerve theorem for finite closed covers with contractible intersections, and Björner (1995) surveys both versions.

10.5 Statistics of persistence diagrams

Let X_n be n i.i.d. points from a measure \mu supported on a compact set M. Assume that, for some constants a, b > 0 and all x \in M, \mu(\bar B(x, r)) \geq \min(1, a r^b).

Proposition 10.10 — Convergence of Rips diagrams Under this assumption, for a constant C depending on a and b, \mathbb{E}\, d_B\big( \mathrm{Dgm}\, R(X_n), \mathrm{Dgm}\, R(M) \big) \;\leq\; C \Big( \frac{\log n}{n} \Big)^{1/b}, and this rate is minimax optimal up to the logarithmic factor (Chazal, Glisse, Labruère and Michel, 2015).

Proof. Idea. By Theorem 7.5 it suffices to bound d_H(X_n, M). Cover M by N \leq C' \varepsilon^{-b} balls of radius \varepsilon / 2 centred on M. If each contains a sample point, then d_H(X_n, M) \leq \varepsilon. A given ball is empty with probability at most \big( 1 - a (\varepsilon / 2)^b \big)^n \leq \exp\big( -n a (\varepsilon / 2)^b \big), and a union bound over the N balls, followed by an integration of the tail, gives the rate. ∎

Fasy, Lecci, Rinaldo, Wasserman, Balakrishnan and Singh (2014) turn the same bound into confidence sets: a bootstrap estimate \hat c of the quantile of d_H(X_n, M) defines a band of width 2 \hat c along the diagonal, and the points outside the band are significant features. Chazal, Fasy, Lecci, Rinaldo and Wasserman (2015) build bootstrap confidence bands for mean landscapes (ch. 8).

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • Corner ranks of the boundary matrix determine the persistence pairs, whatever the reduction.
  • Interleavings turn inclusions or nearest-point maps into bottleneck bounds through the isometry theorem; both stability theorems of the page follow.
  • Gradient pairs are inverse elementary collapses, and critical simplices are cell attachments.
  • The nerve theorem for convex sets follows from explicit maps and straight-line homotopies.
  • Rips diagrams of samples converge at the rate of the Hausdorff distance, and bootstrap bands separate significant features.

11. Research directions and open problems

Topological data analysis is an active field, and the research seminar of the course is an entry point to it. This chapter, for self-study, points to directions that a project or an internship can start from, with references that the References list in full.

Multiparameter persistence

Data often come with two parameters at once — a scale and a density, or a scale and a time. Carlsson and Zomorodian (2009) showed that modules indexed by a grid of dimension two or more admit no complete discrete invariant like the barcode. Lesnick (2015) extended the interleaving distance to them, and Lesnick and Wright (2015) developed the RIVET software to explore two-parameter modules interactively. Carrière and Blumberg (2020) proposed multiparameter persistence images for learning.

Open problems: invariants that are stable, computable at scale and informative enough for statistics and learning; algorithms for minimal presentations in more than two parameters.

Statistics and learning with topology

The statistical theory of diagrams — convergence rates, bootstrap confidence sets, central limit theorems for landscapes (Section 10.5, ch. 8) — is surveyed by Chazal and Michel (2021). Learned filtrations go one step further: Hofer, Graf, Rieck, Niethammer and Kwitt (2020) learn the filtration of a graph end to end, and Horn, De Brouwer, Moor, Moreau, Rieck and Borgwardt (2022) add persistence to graph neural networks. Topological losses and regularizers (ch. 8) are now used in segmentation and representation learning (Moor, Horn, Rieck and Borgwardt, 2020).

Open problems: generalization guarantees for models with topological layers; the optimization landscape of topological losses, which are non-smooth and non-convex; filtrations that remain informative in very high dimension.

Topology for scientific visualization at scale

Simulations produce ensembles of large fields, and comparing their topological summaries requires geometry on the space of summaries. Vidal, Budin and Tierny (2020) compute Wasserstein barycenters of persistence diagrams progressively; Pont, Vidal, Delon and Tierny (2022) define Wasserstein distances, geodesics and barycenters of merge trees. Soler, Plainchault, Conche and Tierny (2018) compress fields lossily while controlling the change of their diagram.

Open problems: in-situ topological analysis during simulations, distributed computation of merge trees and Morse–Smale complexes on supercomputers, and summaries of uncertain or time-varying fields.

Shapes and transforms

The persistent homology transform of Turner, Mukherjee and Boyer (2014) records the diagrams of the height functions of a shape in all directions; for triangulated surfaces in \mathbb{R}^3, this collection of diagrams determines the shape. Related Euler characteristic transforms are used in biology and medical imaging.

Open problems: the number of directions needed in practice, and statistical models on these transforms.

Applications in the sciences

Persistent homology describes the medium-range order of amorphous solids (Hiraoka and coauthors, 2016), the filaments of the cosmic web (Sousbie, 2011), and the topology of anatomical structures in segmentation (Hu, Li, Samaras and Chen, 2019). In each case, a stable summary of multi-scale structure answers a question that pointwise statistics miss.

Open problems: the interpretation of diagram points in terms of the underlying physics or biology, and the design of filtrations adapted to each domain.

Key takeaways — chapter 11

  • Multiparameter persistence has no barcode; stable and computable invariants are an open problem.
  • Statistics, learned filtrations and topological losses connect persistence with modern learning.
  • Large-scale visualization needs distances, barycenters and compression for diagrams and merge trees.
  • Transforms of shapes and scientific applications ask for interpretable, domain-specific filtrations.

  7. ★ Distance to a measure by hand. On the line, take X = \{-1, 0, 1, 10\} and k = 2. (a) Compute d_X and the empirical DTM at the points 0, 5 and 10. (b) Move the outlier from 10 to 20, and compare the changes of d_X and of the DTM at 20 with the bound L / \sqrt k of Section 7.4.

  8. ★ Landscapes by hand. For D = \{(0, 4), (1, 3)\} and D' = \{(0, 4)\}, compute \lambda_1 and \lambda_2 of both diagrams and check Theorem 8.2.

    Solution: the tent of (1, 3) lies below the tent of (0, 4), so \lambda_1 = \Lambda_{(0, 4)} for both diagrams, with peak 2 at t = 2.

    Then \lambda_2(D) = \Lambda_{(1, 3)}, with peak 1, and \lambda_2(D') = 0. So \| \lambda_2(D) - \lambda_2(D') \|_\infty = 1 = d_B(D, D'): the bound of Theorem 8.2 is attained.

  9. ★★ Signs. Prove that \partial \circ \partial = 0 for the signed boundary of Section 2.4.

    Solution: let i < j, and follow the face obtained by removing v_i and v_j.

    • Removing v_j first, with sign (-1)^j, and then v_i, still in position i, gives the sign (-1)^{i + j}.
    • Removing v_i first, with sign (-1)^i, moves v_j to position j - 1; removing it then gives (-1)^{i + j - 1} in total.

    The two terms have opposite signs and cancel.

  10. ★★ Loops of a graph. Prove that a graph with V vertices, E edges and C connected components has \beta_1 = E - V + C over any field, and use it, after ch. 5, to check the loop counts of Code 5.1.

    Solution: (a) 1 - 4 + 1 = -2 = 2 - 2g. (b) Six nodes: the extrema have degree 1 and each saddle degree 3, since a level circle splits or two merge. The degrees sum to 2 + 12 = 14, so there are 7 arcs and 7 - 6 + 1 = 2 loops, as Theorem 5.2 predicts.

    1. By Proposition 6.3, 4 \times 4 = 16 arcs and 8 quadrangles, and 6 - 16 + 8 = -2.

  12. ★★ Rips stability on the line. Let X = \{0, 1, 3\} and Y = \{0, 1.2, 3\}. Compute the Rips diagrams in degrees 0 and 1 (with r = diameter / 2), their bottleneck distance, and d_H(X, Y).

  14. ★★ H_0 of Rips filtrations and spanning trees. Prove that the finite intervals of \mathrm{Dgm}_0 R(X) are [0, \ell_e / 2), where \ell_e runs over the edge lengths of a minimum spanning tree of X.

    Solution: all vertices enter at r = 0, and the edges enter in increasing order of length. The sweep of Proposition 4.3 is then Kruskal’s algorithm: an edge kills a bar exactly when it joins two components, that is, when Kruskal keeps it in the tree. It dies at half its length. The merge tree of this sweep is the single-linkage dendrogram.

Build the Rips complex up to r = 1 in dimension 2, from the triangles of common neighbours, and compute \mathrm{Dgm}_1 with the cohomology and clearing of Code 9.1. Explain the result.

    Hints and expected result: the complex has 407,279 simplices, and the computation takes a few seconds in plain Python. Two intervals stand out, [0.270, 0.887) and [0.272, 0.874), of persistence 0.617 and 0.602; the next one has persistence 0.370.

    They are the two generators of H_1 of the torus, \beta_1 = 2: a meridian around the tube and a longitude around the hole. Both die near \sqrt 3 / 2 = 0.866: the tube and the inner equator are circles of radius 1, and the Rips complex fills an inscribed equilateral triangle of such a circle at that scale (Code 1.1).

  16. ★★★ Open-ended: a topological loss for thin structures. Generate synthetic images of a thin curve (a random smooth curve drawn with a width of 2 pixels) with noise and blur. Predict a probability map with a small convolutional network, trained (a) with a pixelwise cross-entropy, (b) with an added term that penalizes the persistence of every component but the largest one in \mathrm{Dgm}_0 of the superlevel sets of the prediction (Proposition 8.5).     Hints and expected result: with cross-entropy alone, thin parts break, and the predicted mask has several components on many images. Written exam (proposal)

Format: 1 hour, no documents, no electronic devices, four exercises.

  1. Question (4 points). Define the persistence diagram of a filtration and the bottleneck distance. State the stability theorem, and explain why it justifies thresholding persistence to separate features from noise in an image.

    Expected: Definitions 4.6 and 4.7, Theorem 4.10. Noise of sup-norm \delta moves each point of the clean diagram by at most \delta, and every extra point lies within \delta of the diagonal, so it has persistence at most 2\delta.

    A clean feature of persistence above 4\delta keeps a persistence above 2\delta, so a threshold in a gap above 2\delta keeps exactly these features.

  2. Question (6 points). (a) Compute the Betti numbers of the boundary of a tetrahedron from the ranks of its boundary matrices. (b) Compute \mathrm{Dgm}_0 of the sublevel sets of the values (4, 1, 3, 0, 5, 2, 6) on a path. (c) Compute its bottleneck distance to \{(1, 3.5), (0, \infty)\}.

    Expected: (a) n = (4, 6, 4), \operatorname{rank} \partial_1 = 3, \operatorname{rank} \partial_2 = 3, so \beta = (1, 0, 1). (b) \{(1, 3), (2, 5), (0, \infty)\}, by the elder rule at the values 3 and 5.

    1. Matching (1, 3) with (1, 3.5) and sending (2, 5) to the diagonal costs \max(0.5, 1.5); the other matchings also cost at least 1.5. So d_B = 1.5.

  3. Question (5 points). Prove Banchoff’s formula (Theorem 3.6), and deduce that on a triangulated surface the numbers of minima, saddles counted with multiplicity and maxima satisfy m_0 - m_1 + m_2 = \chi.

    Expected: the grouping of simplices by highest vertex, with the bijection between the simplices of highest vertex v and \{v\} \cup \mathrm{Lk}^-(v); then the Euler characteristics of the lower links of Corollary 3.7.

  4. Question (5 points). Prove that \check C(X, r) \subseteq R(X, r) \subseteq \check C(X, 2r) in any metric space. State and prove the sandwich lemma, and deduce how to read the homology of an offset from two Rips complexes.

    Expected: the proof of Theorem 1.7 (a common point gives pairwise distances at most 2r; any vertex is a common point at radius 2r), Lemma 7.3 and Proposition 7.4, with the condition that the inclusions of offsets induce isomorphisms.
