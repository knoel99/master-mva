The course builds probabilistic modelling from one idea: write the joint distribution of everything, observed or hidden, as a product of simple pieces, then compute or approximate what you need from it. Graphs encode the factorisation. Inference is exact on trees (sum-product), and approximate elsewhere (MCMC, variational methods, expectation propagation). Deep generative models replace the simple pieces by neural networks and keep the same inference questions.

0. Review: probability tools for probabilistic models

Every later chapter manipulates joint densities, conditions them on observations and compares two distributions. This review fixes the four tools used throughout: conditional independence, the Gaussian, the Kullback–Leibler divergence and exponential families. The probability refresher gives the background.

0.1 Conditional independence

Definition 0.1 — Conditional independence Random variables X and Y are independent given Z, written X \perp Y \mid Z, if for all z with p(z) > 0: p(x, y \mid z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(y \mid z).

Equivalently, p(x \mid y, z) = p(x \mid z) whenever p(y, z) > 0.

Proposition 0.2 — Factorisation criterion X \perp Y \mid Z if and only if there are non-negative functions f and g with p(x, y, z) = f(x, z)\, g(y, z).

Proof. The forward direction takes f = p(x \mid z) and g = p(y, z).

Conversely, integrate the factorisation over x, over y, then over both. With \bar f(z) = \int f\,dx and \bar g(z) = \int g\,dy: p(y, z) = \bar f(z)\, g(y, z), \qquad p(x, z) = f(x, z)\, \bar g(z), \qquad p(z) = \bar f(z)\, \bar g(z).

Hence p(x, y, z)\, p(z) = p(x, z)\, p(y, z), which is the definition after division by p(z)^2. ∎

Graphical models (ch. 4) are nothing but a bookkeeping of such factorisations.

0.2 The multivariate Gaussian

Proposition 0.3 — Gaussian marginals and conditionals Let (x_a, x_b) be Gaussian with mean (\mu_a, \mu_b) and covariance blocks \Sigma_{aa}, \Sigma_{ab}, \Sigma_{bb}, with \Sigma_{bb} invertible. Then x_a \sim \mathcal N(\mu_a, \Sigma_{aa}), and:

Key result — Gaussian conditioning x_a \mid x_b \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\big(\mu_a + \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(x_b - \mu_b),\;\; \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba}\big).

Proof. Set r = x_a - \mu_a - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(x_b - \mu_b).

  1. The vector (r, x_b) is Gaussian, as a linear image of a Gaussian vector.
  2. Its cross-covariance vanishes: \mathrm{Cov}(r, x_b) = \Sigma_{ab} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{bb} = 0 .
  3. For jointly Gaussian vectors, zero covariance means independence, so r is independent of x_b.
  4. Its covariance is the Schur complement \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba}, and its mean is 0.

Writing x_a as the conditional mean plus r gives the result. ∎

A useful corollary, the linear-Gaussian model: if z \sim \mathcal N(m, P) and x \mid z \sim \mathcal N(Az + b, R), then x \sim \mathcal N(Am + b,\; R + APA^\top).

The posterior of z given x is also Gaussian, by Proposition 0.3 applied to the pair (z, x). Probabilistic PCA, Bayesian regression and Gaussian processes (ch. 2–3) are three instances.

0.3 The Kullback–Leibler divergence

Definition 0.4 — KL divergence For densities q and p with q \ll p: \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = \int q(z) \log \frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\, dz .

Gibbs’ inequality: \mathrm{KL}(q \| p) \ge 0, with equality if and only if q = p almost everywhere.

Proof. The logarithm is concave, so Jensen’s inequality under q gives -\mathrm{KL}(q \| p) = \mathbb E_q\Big[\log \frac{p}{q}\Big] \le \log \mathbb E_q\Big[\frac{p}{q}\Big] \le \log 1 = 0.

Equality in Jensen forces p/q to be constant q-almost surely, and the constant is 1. ∎

The KL divergence is not symmetric. Minimising it in q (variational inference, ch. 7) and in p (maximum likelihood, ch. 1; expectation propagation, ch. 7.4) give different answers.

0.4 Exponential families

Definition 0.5 — Exponential family A family with sufficient statistic T, natural parameter \eta and log-partition A: p(x \mid \eta) = h(x) \exp\big(\eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta)\big), \qquad A(\eta) = \log \int h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top T(x)}\, dx .

Bernoulli, categorical, Poisson, Gaussian, Gamma and Dirichlet laws are exponential families.

Proposition 0.6 — Moments from the log-partition \nabla A(\eta) = \mathbb E_\eta[T(X)], \qquad \nabla^2 A(\eta) = \mathrm{Cov}_\eta[T(X)] \succeq 0 .

So A is convex, and the log-likelihood of an i.i.d. sample is concave in \eta. Its stationarity condition is moment matching: \mathbb E_{\hat\eta}[T(X)] = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n T(x_i).

Proof. Differentiate under the integral: \nabla A = \int T\, h\, e^{\eta^\top T}\,dx \,/\, e^{A}, which is \mathbb E_\eta[T].

Differentiating once more gives \mathbb E[TT^\top] - \mathbb E[T]\,\mathbb E[T]^\top. The log-likelihood is \eta^\top \sum_i T(x_i) - nA(\eta) plus a constant. ∎

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Conditional independence is a factorisation of the joint density (Proposition 0.2).
  • Gaussians are closed under marginalisation, conditioning and linear maps (Proposition 0.3).
  • \mathrm{KL}(q \| p) \ge 0, with equality only at q = p.
  • In an exponential family, maximum likelihood matches expected and empirical sufficient statistics.

1. Maximum likelihood, linear models and K-means

Given data and a parametric family, which parameter should we pick? Maximum likelihood answers in one line and covers regression, classification and, after a limit, clustering. This chapter states the principle, then applies it to linear regression, logistic regression and K-means.

1.1 Maximum likelihood

Definition 1.1 — Likelihood and maximum-likelihood estimator For i.i.d. observations x_1, \dots, x_n from a model \{p_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}, the log-likelihood and the MLE are \ell_n(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(x_i), \qquad \hat\theta_n \in \arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta} \ell_n(\theta).

Maximising \ell_n / n is minimising an empirical version of \mathrm{KL}(p^\star \| p_\theta), where p^\star is the data distribution. Indeed, by the law of large numbers: \frac1n \ell_n(\theta) \;\to\; \mathbb E_{p^\star}[\log p_\theta(X)] = -\mathrm{KL}(p^\star \| p_\theta) - H(p^\star).

Theorem 1.2 — Consistency and asymptotic normality Assume the model is well specified, p^\star = p_{\theta^\star}, identifiable, smooth, with invertible Fisher information I(\theta^\star), plus standard regularity conditions. Then \hat\theta_n \to \theta^\star in probability, and: \sqrt n\, (\hat\theta_n - \theta^\star) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal N\big(0,\, I(\theta^\star)^{-1}\big), \qquad I(\theta) = -\mathbb E_\theta\big[\nabla^2 \log p_\theta(X)\big].

Proof. Sketch. Consistency: \ell_n / n converges uniformly to -\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta^\star} \| p_\theta) up to a constant. That limit has a unique maximiser \theta^\star by Gibbs’ inequality and identifiability.

Normality: a Taylor expansion of \nabla \ell_n(\hat\theta_n) = 0 around \theta^\star gives \sqrt n (\hat\theta_n - \theta^\star) \approx \Big(-\tfrac1n \nabla^2 \ell_n(\theta^\star)\Big)^{-1} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\nabla \ell_n(\theta^\star).

The score \nabla \ell_n(\theta^\star)/\sqrt n is asymptotically \mathcal N(0, I) by the central limit theorem. The bracket tends to I by the law of large numbers, and Slutsky’s lemma concludes. Full conditions: van der Vaart (1998), chapter 5. ∎

1.2 Linear regression

The model is y_i = w^\top x_i + \varepsilon_i with \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) i.i.d. Stack the inputs in X \in \mathbb R^{n \times d} and the outputs in y \in \mathbb R^n.

Proposition 1.3 — Gaussian likelihood and least squares \ell_n(w, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|y - Xw\|^2 .

If X^\top X is invertible, the MLE is \hat w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n \|y - X\hat w\|^2 .

Proof. For any fixed \sigma^2, maximising in w means minimising \|y - Xw\|^2, a convex quadratic. Its gradient 2X^\top(Xw - y) vanishes at the normal equations X^\top X w = X^\top y.

Then differentiate in \sigma^2: the condition -n/(2\sigma^2) + \|y - X\hat w\|^2/(2\sigma^4) = 0 gives \hat\sigma^2. ∎

Geometrically, X\hat w is the orthogonal projection of y onto the column space of X. Laplace noise would give least absolute deviations instead: the loss is the negative log-likelihood of the noise model.

1.3 Logistic regression

Definition 1.4 — Logistic regression For a label y \in \{0, 1\} and features x \in \mathbb R^d: p(y = 1 \mid x; w) = \sigma(w^\top x), \qquad \sigma(t) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-t}} .

Proposition 1.5 — Concavity and Newton’s method With p_i = \sigma(w^\top x_i), the log-likelihood and its derivatives are \ell_n(w) = \sum_{i} \big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - p_i)\big], \qquad \nabla \ell_n = X^\top (y - p), \qquad \nabla^2 \ell_n = -X^\top S X, where S = \mathrm{diag}(p_i(1 - p_i)). So \ell_n is concave.

Newton’s step w \leftarrow w + (X^\top S X)^{-1} X^\top (y - p) is a weighted least-squares problem: iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS).

Proof. Use \sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma). The derivative of y \log \sigma(t) + (1-y)\log(1 - \sigma(t)) in t is y - \sigma(t).

The chain rule with t = w^\top x_i gives the gradient. Differentiating -\sigma(w^\top x_i)\, x_i once more gives the Hessian, which is negative semi-definite since S \succeq 0.

Finally, the Newton step equals (X^\top S X)^{-1} X^\top S\, u with working response u = Xw + S^{-1}(y - p). ∎

If the two classes are linearly separable, \ell_n increases along the separating direction without bound, and no MLE exists (Exercise 7). A Gaussian prior on w, that is a ridge penalty, restores existence (ch. 3).

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, d = 500, 3
X = np.c_[np.ones(n), rng.normal(size=(n, d - 1))]   # intercept + 2 features
w_true = np.array([-0.5, 2.0, -1.0])
y = rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ w_true))

w = np.zeros(d)
for it in range(7):                       # Newton = IRLS
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ w))
    grad = X.T @ (y - p)                  # gradient of the log-likelihood
    H = X.T @ (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None])
    w = w + np.linalg.solve(H, grad)
    print(it, np.round(w, 4), f"|grad| = {np.linalg.norm(grad):.2e}")

sk = LogisticRegression(C=np.inf, fit_intercept=False, tol=1e-10, max_iter=1000).fit(X, y)
print("scikit-learn:", np.round(sk.coef_.ravel(), 4))

The gradient norm falls from 150 to 10^{-14} in six steps, the quadratic convergence of Newton’s method. The estimate (-0.449, 1.638, -1.103) agrees with scikit-learn’s unpenalised fit.

1.4 K-means

Definition 1.6 — K-means objective and Lloyd’s algorithm For centres \mu_1, \dots, \mu_K and assignments z_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}: J(z, \mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \mu_{z_i}\|^2 .

Lloyd’s algorithm alternates two steps:

  1. assign each point to its closest centre;
  2. move each centre to the mean of its points.

Proposition 1.7 — Lloyd’s algorithm decreases J and stops Each step does not increase J, and the algorithm stops after finitely many iterations at a fixed point.

Proof. Step 1 minimises J over z for fixed \mu, term by term.

Step 2 minimises over \mu for fixed z: the mean minimises \sum_{i \in C_k} \|x_i - m\|^2 over m, because the gradient 2\sum_{i \in C_k}(m - x_i) vanishes there.

So J is non-increasing. There are at most K^n assignments, and a strict decrease cannot revisit one, so the algorithm stops. ∎

The fixed point is only a local minimum: minimising J exactly is NP-hard in general. In practice one uses several restarts and k-means++ seeding (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007). Ch. 2.3 shows K-means is the zero-variance limit of EM for a Gaussian mixture.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • The MLE minimises an empirical KL divergence to the data distribution; it is consistent and asymptotically efficient in regular models.
  • Gaussian noise gives least squares; Bernoulli labels give the concave logistic likelihood, solved by Newton = IRLS.
  • Separable data have no logistic MLE without a prior.
  • K-means is coordinate descent on a non-convex objective; it converges to a local minimum.

2. Latent variables: EM, Gaussian mixtures and probabilistic PCA

Many models are simple only once an unobserved variable is added: the cluster of a point, the low-dimensional cause of a high-dimensional vector. The likelihood of the observed data then contains a sum or an integral over the hidden variable, and has no closed-form maximiser. The EM algorithm turns this into a sequence of complete-data problems.

2.1 Gaussian mixtures

Definition 2.1 — Gaussian mixture model A latent label z \in \{1, \dots, K\} with p(z = k) = \pi_k, and x \mid z = k \sim \mathcal N(\mu_k, \Sigma_k). The marginal density and the log-likelihood are p(x; \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\, \mathcal N(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad \ell_n(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\, \mathcal N(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k).

The logarithm of a sum has no closed-form maximiser. Two pathologies deserve a warning.

  • Unbounded likelihood: put \mu_k = x_1 and let \Sigma_k \to 0; then \ell_n \to +\infty. The MLE is a local maximum away from these singularities, or one adds a small floor or a prior on \Sigma_k.
  • Label switching: permuting the components leaves p(x; \theta) unchanged, so the parameter is identifiable only up to a permutation.

2.2 The EM algorithm

For any distribution q on the latent variables, define the evidence lower bound (ELBO): \mathcal L(q, \theta) = \mathbb E_{q(z)}\big[\log p(x, z; \theta)\big] - \mathbb E_{q(z)}\big[\log q(z)\big].

Proposition 2.2 — ELBO decomposition

Key result — the ELBO decomposition \log p(x; \theta) = \mathcal L(q, \theta) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z) \,\|\, p(z \mid x; \theta)\big).

So \mathcal L(q, \theta) \le \log p(x; \theta), with equality if and only if q is the posterior.

Proof. Write the joint as the marginal times the posterior, \log p(x, z; \theta) = \log p(x; \theta) + \log p(z \mid x; \theta), and take the expectation under q: \mathcal L(q, \theta) = \log p(x; \theta) + \mathbb E_q\big[\log p(z \mid x; \theta) - \log q(z)\big] = \log p(x; \theta) - \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p(\cdot \mid x; \theta)).

Gibbs’ inequality (Definition 0.4) gives the bound and the equality case. ∎

EM (Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977) alternates two steps from \theta_0:

  1. E-step: q_{t+1}(z) = p(z \mid x; \theta_t), which closes the gap;
  2. M-step: maximise the expected complete log-likelihood, \theta_{t+1} \in \arg\max_\theta Q(\theta, \theta_t), \qquad Q(\theta, \theta_t) = \mathbb E_{p(z \mid x; \theta_t)}\big[\log p(x, z; \theta)\big].

Theorem 2.3 — EM never decreases the likelihood For every t: \log p(x; \theta_{t+1}) \;\ge\; \log p(x; \theta_t).

Proof. The entropy term of \mathcal L does not depend on \theta, so the M-step also maximises \mathcal L(q_{t+1}, \cdot). Then \log p(x; \theta_{t+1}) \;\ge\; \mathcal L(q_{t+1}, \theta_{t+1}) \;\ge\; \mathcal L(q_{t+1}, \theta_t) \;=\; \log p(x; \theta_t).

The first inequality is Proposition 2.2. The second is the M-step. The equality holds because q_{t+1} is the posterior at \theta_t. ∎

Monotonicity does not give convergence to a global maximum. Under smoothness conditions, limit points are stationary points of the likelihood (Wu, 1983). In practice EM is run from several initialisations, and the best final likelihood is kept.

2.3 EM for Gaussian mixtures

Proposition 2.4 — EM updates for a Gaussian mixture The E-step computes the responsibilities \tau_{ik} = p(z_i = k \mid x_i; \theta_t) = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal N(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{l} \pi_l\, \mathcal N(x_i; \mu_l, \Sigma_l)} .

With N_k = \sum_i \tau_{ik}, the M-step is \pi_k = \frac{N_k}{n}, \qquad \mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \tau_{ik}\, x_i, \qquad \Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \tau_{ik}\,(x_i - \mu_k)(x_i - \mu_k)^\top .

Proof. The E-step is Bayes’ rule. The function to maximise is Q = \sum_{i,k} \tau_{ik}\big[\log \pi_k + \log \mathcal N(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)\big].

It separates. For \pi, a Lagrange multiplier for \sum_k \pi_k = 1 gives \pi_k \propto N_k.

For (\mu_k, \Sigma_k), it is a weighted Gaussian log-likelihood, maximised by the weighted mean and covariance, as in the unweighted case. ∎

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal as mvn

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
X = np.r_[rng.normal([0, 0], 1.0, (200, 2)), rng.normal([4, 3], 0.7, (150, 2))]
n, K = len(X), 2
pi, mu = np.full(K, 1 / K), X[rng.choice(n, K, replace=False)]
Sigma = np.array([np.eye(2)] * K)

trace = []
for it in range(30):
    dens = np.column_stack([pi[k] * mvn.pdf(X, mu[k], Sigma[k]) for k in range(K)])
    trace.append(np.log(dens.sum(1)).sum())          # log-likelihood at current theta
    tau = dens / dens.sum(1, keepdims=True)          # E-step: responsibilities
    Nk = tau.sum(0)                                  # M-step
    pi, mu = Nk / n, (tau.T @ X) / Nk[:, None]
    for k in range(K):
        R = X - mu[k]
        Sigma[k] = (tau[:, k, None] * R).T @ R / Nk[k]

print("log-likelihood:", np.round(trace[::6], 2))
print("never decreases:", np.all(np.diff(trace) >= -1e-9))
print("weights", np.round(pi, 3), " means", np.round(mu, 2).tolist())

The log-likelihood rises from −3456 to −1094.66 and never decreases, as Theorem 2.3 predicts. The estimated weights are (0.57, 0.43) for true proportions (0.57, 0.43), and the means are close to (0, 0) and (4, 3).

Proposition 2.5 — K-means as a limit of EM Fix \Sigma_k = \varepsilon I and \pi_k = 1/K, and run EM on the means only. As \varepsilon \to 0, the responsibilities become hard assignments to the closest mean, and EM becomes Lloyd’s algorithm.

Proof. The responsibilities are a softmax: \tau_{ik} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - \mu_k\|^2 / 2\varepsilon)}{\sum_l \exp(-\|x_i - \mu_l\|^2 / 2\varepsilon)} .

As \varepsilon \to 0, this tends to 1 for the closest centre and 0 elsewhere, assuming no ties. The mean update becomes the cluster mean. ∎

2.4 Probabilistic PCA

Definition 2.6 — Probabilistic PCA (Tipping and Bishop, 1999) With q < d: z \sim \mathcal N(0, I_q), \qquad x \mid z \sim \mathcal N(Wz + \mu,\; \sigma^2 I_d).

By the linear-Gaussian model of ch. 0.2, the marginal is x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, C) with C = WW^\top + \sigma^2 I_d.

With M = W^\top W + \sigma^2 I_q, the posterior of the latent variable is z \mid x \sim \mathcal N\big(M^{-1} W^\top (x - \mu),\; \sigma^2 M^{-1}\big).

Theorem 2.7 — Maximum-likelihood PPCA Let S be the empirical covariance, with eigenvalues \lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d and eigenvectors U = (u_1, \dots, u_d). The MLE is \hat\mu = \bar x, and, for any rotation R \in O(q): \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d - q}\sum_{j = q+1}^d \lambda_j, \qquad \hat W = U_q\,(\Lambda_q - \hat\sigma^2 I)^{1/2} R .

Proof. Sketch. Up to constants, the log-likelihood is \ell_n = -\frac n2 \big[\log|C| + \mathrm{tr}(C^{-1}S)\big].

  1. The gradient in W vanishes when S C^{-1} W = W.
  2. The identity C^{-1}W = W M^{-1} turns this into SW = W M.
  3. Take the singular value decomposition W = L D V^\top and substitute. Each column l_j with d_j > 0 satisfies S\, l_j = (\sigma^2 + d_j^2)\, l_j . So l_j is an eigenvector of S, and d_j^2 = \lambda_j - \sigma^2.
  4. Substituting back, the optimal \sigma^2 is the mean of the discarded eigenvalues.
  5. Comparing the values of \ell_n, the maximum keeps the q largest eigenvalues.

Details: Tipping and Bishop (1999), appendix A. ∎

As \sigma^2 \to 0, the posterior mean projects x onto the principal subspace: classical PCA is the noiseless limit. EM for PPCA costs O(ndq) per iteration instead of a full eigendecomposition, and handles missing coordinates naturally.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n, d, q = 2000, 5, 2
W_true = rng.normal(size=(d, q))
X = rng.normal(size=(n, q)) @ W_true.T + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(n, d)) + 1.0

S = np.cov(X, rowvar=False, bias=True)          # ML covariance (divides by n)
lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(S)
lam, U = lam[::-1], U[:, ::-1]                   # decreasing eigenvalues
sigma2 = lam[q:].mean()                          # Theorem 2.7: discarded variance
W = U[:, :q] * np.sqrt(lam[:q] - sigma2)         # rotation R = I

C = W @ W.T + sigma2 * np.eye(d)
ll = -0.5 * n * (d * np.log(2 * np.pi) + np.linalg.slogdet(C)[1]
                 + np.trace(np.linalg.solve(C, S)))
pca = PCA(n_components=q).fit(X)
print(f"sigma2 = {sigma2:.4f} (true 0.09); scikit-learn: {pca.noise_variance_:.4f}")
print(f"average log-likelihood {ll / n:.4f}; scikit-learn PCA.score: {pca.score(X):.4f}")

The estimate \hat\sigma^2 = 0.0899 recovers the true noise variance 0.09. The average log-likelihood, -5.1007, equals scikit-learn’s PCA.score, which implements the PPCA likelihood.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The log-likelihood is the ELBO plus a KL gap: EM closes the gap (E-step), then raises the bound (M-step).
  • EM never decreases the likelihood, but it can stop at a local maximum or a saddle.
  • For Gaussian mixtures, EM is soft K-means with covariances; K-means is its zero-variance limit.
  • PPCA has a closed-form MLE given by the top eigenvectors; PCA is its noiseless limit.

3. Bayesian linear models, Gaussian processes and model selection

Maximum likelihood returns one parameter and no uncertainty, and it always prefers the most complex model. A prior on the parameters gives a posterior, a predictive distribution with error bars and a marginal likelihood to compare models. Gaussian priors keep every computation in closed form.

3.1 Bayesian linear regression

The model takes a prior w \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I_d) and a likelihood y \mid w \sim \mathcal N(Xw, \beta^{-1} I_n), where \alpha and \beta are precisions.

Proposition 3.1 — Posterior and predictive distributions The posterior is w \mid y \sim \mathcal N(m_N, S_N) with S_N = (\alpha I + \beta X^\top X)^{-1}, \qquad m_N = \beta\, S_N X^\top y .

At a new input x_\ast, the predictive distribution is y_\ast \mid y \sim \mathcal N\big(m_N^\top x_\ast,\;\; \beta^{-1} + x_\ast^\top S_N\, x_\ast\big).

Proof. The log-posterior is, up to a constant, -\frac{\alpha}{2}\|w\|^2 - \frac{\beta}{2}\|y - Xw\|^2 = -\frac12 w^\top(\alpha I + \beta X^\top X) w + \beta\, w^\top X^\top y + \text{const}.

This quadratic form identifies the Gaussian with precision S_N^{-1} and mean S_N\, \beta X^\top y.

For the prediction, y_\ast = w^\top x_\ast + \varepsilon_\ast, where \varepsilon_\ast is independent of w given the data. Means and variances add. ∎

The posterior mean is the ridge estimator with penalty \lambda = \alpha / \beta, which is also the MAP estimate. The predictive variance has two terms: the noise \beta^{-1}, and the parameter uncertainty x_\ast^\top S_N x_\ast, which shrinks as data accumulate.

Example 3.2 — A two-point posterior Take one feature, data x = (1, 2), y = (1, 3) and \alpha = \beta = 1.

  • X^\top X = 5 and X^\top y = 7, so S_N = 1/6 and m_N = 7/6 \approx 1.17.
  • Least squares would give 7/5 = 1.4: the prior shrinks the slope towards 0.
  • At x_\ast = 3, the predictive mean is 3.5 and the predictive variance is 1 + 9/6 = 2.5.

y_\ast \mid y \sim \mathcal N(3.5,\; 2.5).

3.2 Gaussian processes

Replace x by features \phi(x). Under the prior of ch. 3.1, the function f(x) = w^\top \phi(x) has Gaussian finite-dimensional marginals with covariance \alpha^{-1}\phi(x)^\top \phi(x'). A Gaussian process specifies this covariance directly, through a kernel.

Definition 3.3 — Gaussian process A random function f is a Gaussian process \mathcal{GP}(m, k) if every finite vector (f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n)) is Gaussian with mean (m(x_i))_i and covariance (k(x_i, x_j))_{ij}.

The kernel must be positive semi-definite. A standard choice is the squared-exponential kernel: k(x, x') = s^2 \exp\Big(-\frac{\|x - x'\|^2}{2\ell^2}\Big).

Proposition 3.4 — Gaussian-process regression Observe y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i with f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k) and \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2). Write K = (k(x_i, x_j))_{ij} and k_\ast = (k(x_i, x_\ast))_i. Then f(x_\ast) \mid y is Gaussian with \mu_\ast = k_\ast^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} y, \qquad \sigma^2_\ast = k(x_\ast, x_\ast) - k_\ast^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} k_\ast .

The log marginal likelihood (the evidence) is \log p(y) = -\frac12 y^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} y - \frac12 \log |K + \sigma^2 I| - \frac n2 \log 2\pi .

Proof. The pair (y, f(x_\ast)) is Gaussian, with \mathrm{Cov}(y) = K + \sigma^2 I and cross-covariance k_\ast. Apply Proposition 0.3. The evidence is the density of y \sim \mathcal N(0, K + \sigma^2 I). ∎

The evidence balances a data-fit term against a complexity term, the log-determinant. Hyperparameters such as \ell are chosen by maximising it (type-II maximum likelihood). Exact inference costs O(n^3) through a Cholesky factorisation.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
x = np.sort(rng.uniform(-3, 3, 20))
y = np.sin(2 * x) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=20)
xs = np.linspace(-3, 3, 7)

def k(a, b, ell):                       # squared-exponential kernel
    return np.exp(-0.5 * (a[:, None] - b[None, :]) ** 2 / ell ** 2)

def gp(ell, s2=0.01):
    Ky = k(x, x, ell) + s2 * np.eye(len(x))
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Ky)
    alpha = np.linalg.solve(L.T, np.linalg.solve(L, y))
    mean = k(xs, x, ell) @ alpha
    V = np.linalg.solve(L, k(x, xs, ell))
    var = 1.0 - (V ** 2).sum(0)          # k(x*, x*) = 1
    logev = -0.5 * y @ alpha - np.log(np.diag(L)).sum() - 0.5 * len(x) * np.log(2 * np.pi)
    return mean, var, logev

for ell in [0.1, 0.5, 2.0]:
    m, v, le = gp(ell)
    print(f"ell = {ell:3.1f}   log evidence = {le:8.2f}   max posterior sd = {np.sqrt(v.max()):.2f}")
m, v, _ = gp(0.5)
print("x      ", xs.round(1))
print("mean   ", m.round(2))
print("sd     ", np.sqrt(v).round(2))
print("sin(2x)", np.sin(2 * xs).round(2))

The log evidence is −17.7 for a length-scale of 0.1 (too flexible), −6.0 for 0.5 and −97.6 for 2 (too rigid). With length-scale 0.5, the posterior standard deviation is between 0.08 and 0.22 at the test inputs from −2 to 2, where the data lie. It reaches 0.48 at x = 3 and 0.66 at x = -3, beyond the extreme training inputs −2.49 and 2.74.

3.3 EM revisited

The proof of Theorem 2.3 used only the function \mathcal L(q, \theta). Neal and Hinton (1998) call \mathcal L the (negative) free energy and read EM as coordinate ascent on it.

Proposition 3.5 — EM as coordinate ascent on the free energy The E-step maximises \mathcal L(\cdot, \theta_t) over all distributions q, and the M-step maximises \mathcal L(q_{t+1}, \cdot) over \theta.

Moreover, if (q^\star, \theta^\star) is a local maximum of \mathcal L, then \theta^\star is a local maximum of \log p(x; \theta).

Proof. The first claim is Proposition 2.2: for fixed \theta, \mathcal L is maximal when the KL term is zero.

For the second, q^\star must be the posterior at \theta^\star. Hence \log p(x; \theta) \;\ge\; \mathcal L(q^\star, \theta) near \theta^\star, with equality at \theta^\star, where \mathcal L(q^\star, \cdot) is locally maximal. ∎

This view justifies several variants, since any step that increases \mathcal L keeps the monotonicity.

  • Generalised EM: the M-step only increases \mathcal L, for example by one gradient step.
  • Incremental EM: the E-step updates the posterior of one data point at a time.
  • MAP-EM: adding \log p(\theta) to \mathcal L gives the posterior mode instead of the MLE.
  • Variational EM: q is restricted to a tractable family, and the bound is no longer tight (ch. 7.2).

EM for the evidence of Bayesian regression. In ch. 3.1, treat w as the latent variable and \theta = (\alpha, \beta) as the parameter. The E-step is Proposition 3.1, q(w) = \mathcal N(m_N, S_N). With d weights and n observations, maximising the expected complete log-likelihood gives \alpha \leftarrow \frac{d}{\|m_N\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\, S_N}, \qquad \beta \leftarrow \frac{n}{\|y - Xm_N\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}(X S_N X^\top)} . 

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal as mvn
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture

# EM for the hyperparameters (alpha, beta) of Bayesian linear regression
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
N, M = 30, 10
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, N)
y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + 0.2 * rng.normal(size=N)        # true beta = 25
Phi = np.vander(x, M, increasing=True)                       # degree-9 polynomial
alpha, beta = 1.0, 1.0
for it in range(200):
    S = np.linalg.inv(alpha * np.eye(M) + beta * Phi.T @ Phi)  # E-step: q(w) = N(m, S)
    m = beta * S @ Phi.T @ y
    alpha = M / (m @ m + np.trace(S))                          # M-step
    beta = N / (np.sum((y - Phi @ m) ** 2) + np.trace(Phi @ S @ Phi.T))
    if it in (0, 9, 199):
        C = Phi @ Phi.T / alpha + np.eye(N) / beta
        print(f"iter {it + 1:3d}: alpha {alpha:7.3f}  beta {beta:6.2f}"
              f"  log evidence {mvn.logpdf(y, np.zeros(N), C):7.3f}")

# Model selection: BIC for the number of mixture components
X = np.r_[rng.normal(0, 1, (150, 2)), rng.normal([5, 0], 1, (150, 2)), rng.normal([2, 4], 1, (150, 2))]
bic = [GaussianMixture(K, n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(X).bic(X) for K in range(1, 7)]
print("BIC for K = 1..6:", np.round(bic, 1), "-> K =", 1 + int(np.argmin(bic)))

The log evidence climbs from -19.7 to -8.7. The noise precision reaches \beta \approx 35, of the order of the true value 25 (the flexible fit absorbs part of the noise), and \alpha falls to 0.01. A degree-9 polynomial fitted to 30 noisy points is thus regularised without any validation set.

3.4 Model selection

Comparing models \mathcal M_1, \mathcal M_2, \dots by their maximised likelihood always favours the largest. Three corrections are standard.

  • Evidence: the Bayesian answer compares the marginal likelihoods p(\mathcal D \mid \mathcal M), which penalise unused flexibility automatically (ch. 3.2–3.3).

    p(\mathcal D \mid \mathcal M) = \int p(\mathcal D \mid \theta, \mathcal M)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)\, d\theta .

  • BIC: a large-sample approximation of the log evidence (Schwarz, 1978).

  • AIC and cross-validation: estimates of the expected predictive log-likelihood (Akaike, 1974), and its direct empirical estimate.

Proposition 3.6 — Laplace approximation and BIC For a regular model with k free parameters and n i.i.d. observations: \log p(\mathcal D \mid \mathcal M) = \ell_n(\hat\theta) - \frac{k}{2}\log n + O_P(1), \qquad \mathrm{BIC} = -2\,\ell_n(\hat\theta) + k \log n .

Proof. Sketch. Expand \ell_n to second order around \hat\theta, where its gradient vanishes, with H_n = -\nabla^2 \ell_n(\hat\theta). Laplace’s method gives \int e^{\ell_n(\theta)} p(\theta)\, d\theta \approx e^{\ell_n(\hat\theta)}\, p(\hat\theta)\, (2\pi)^{k/2} |H_n|^{-1/2}.

Since H_n \approx n\, I(\hat\theta), we have \log |H_n| = k \log n + O_P(1). The other terms are O_P(1). ∎

AIC replaces k \log n by 2k. It targets prediction, while BIC is consistent for selecting the true model in a finite list. In the code above, BIC over Gaussian mixtures with K = 1, \dots, 6 components is minimal at K = 3, the true number. A full-covariance mixture in dimension 2 has 6K - 1 free parameters.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Gaussian prior plus Gaussian likelihood gives a Gaussian posterior; its mean is ridge regression.
  • A Gaussian process is Bayesian regression with a kernel; prediction is Gaussian conditioning, at cost O(n^3).
  • EM is coordinate ascent on the free energy, which justifies partial, incremental, MAP and variational variants.
  • Evidence, BIC, AIC and cross-validation all penalise complexity; BIC is the Laplace approximation of the evidence.

4. Directed and undirected graphical models

A joint distribution of n binary variables has 2^n - 1 free parameters, far too many to store or learn. Graphical models restrict the joint to a product of local factors, and the graph tells which conditional independences follow. Directed graphs encode generative stories; undirected graphs encode symmetric interactions.

4.1 Directed graphical models

Definition 4.1 — Directed graphical model (Bayesian network) Let G be a directed acyclic graph (DAG) on \{1, \dots, n\}, and write \pi_i for the parents of node i. A distribution factorises according to G if p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid x_{\pi_i}).

For binary variables the factorisation needs \sum_i 2^{|\pi_i|} parameters instead of 2^n - 1. The models of ch. 1–3 are all directed graphical models.

  • Naive Bayes: a class y with arrows to each feature x_j, so the features are independent given the class.
  • Gaussian mixture: z_i \to x_i, repeated over i; a plate drawn around the pair stands for the n copies.
  • Bayesian regression: w \to y_i \leftarrow x_i, with the plate over i.
  • Hidden Markov model (ch. 5.3): a chain z_1 \to z_2 \to \dots \to z_T, with an arrow z_t \to x_t at each step.

Proposition 4.2 — Local Markov property If p factorises according to G, each variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents.

Proof. A child of a descendant of i is again a descendant, so some topological order puts all the descendants of i last. Sum the factorisation over them, starting from the last node: each summed factor p(x_j \mid x_{\pi_j}) integrates to 1.

What remains is the product of the factors of i and of its non-descendants. No non-descendant has i as a parent, so x_i appears only in p(x_i \mid x_{\pi_i}). Proposition 0.2 then gives the independence of x_i and its non-descendants given x_{\pi_i}. ∎

4.2 d-separation and explaining away

Three structures on three nodes a, c, b contain all the cases.

  • Chain a \to c \to b: the joint is p(a)\,p(c \mid a)\,p(b \mid c), of the form f(a, c)\, g(b, c). So a \perp b \mid c by Proposition 0.2.
  • Fork a \leftarrow c \to b: the joint p(c)\,p(a \mid c)\,p(b \mid c) has the same form, so again a \perp b \mid c.
  • Collider a \to c \leftarrow b: summing over c gives p(a, b) = p(a)\, p(b), so a \perp b. Conditioning on c (or on a descendant of c) creates a dependence.

Definition 4.3 — d-separation A path between two nodes is blocked by a set C if it contains a node m such that:

  • the arrows meet head-to-tail or tail-to-tail at m, and m \in C; or
  • the arrows meet head-to-head at m (a collider), and neither m nor any descendant of m is in C.

Sets A and B are d-separated by C if every path from A to B is blocked by C.

Theorem 4.4 — Global Markov property of DAGs If p factorises according to G and C d-separates A from B, then: x_A \perp x_B \mid x_C .

Conversely, when C does not d-separate A from B, some distribution factorising according to G violates this independence.

Proof. Sketch. The first part follows from Proposition 4.11 below: d-separation is equivalent to ordinary separation in a moralised ancestral graph, where Proposition 4.7 applies. Completeness is due to Geiger, Verma and Pearl (1990); see Koller and Friedman (2009), chapter 3. ∎

Example 4.5 — Explaining away A burglary B and an earthquake E, independent with P(B = 1) = 0.01 and P(E = 1) = 0.02, can both trigger an alarm A: a collider B \to A \leftarrow E. Take P(A = 1 \mid B, E) = 0.001,\ 0.94,\ 0.29,\ 0.95 \quad \text{for } (B, E) = (0,0),\ (1,0),\ (0,1),\ (1,1).

  • Hearing the alarm raises the probability of a burglary from 0.01 to P(B = 1 \mid A = 1) \approx 0.583.
  • Learning that an earthquake happened brings it back down: P(B = 1 \mid A = 1, E = 1) \approx 0.032.

P(B = 1 \mid A = 1) \approx 0.583, \qquad P(B = 1 \mid A = 1, E = 1) \approx 0.032 .

The two causes were independent; given their common effect, they compete.

4.3 Undirected graphical models

Definition 4.6 — Undirected graphical model (Markov random field) Let G be an undirected graph and \mathcal C its cliques. A distribution factorises over G if, for non-negative potentials \psi_C: p(x) = \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{C \in \mathcal C} \psi_C(x_C), \qquad Z = \sum_x \prod_{C \in \mathcal C} \psi_C(x_C).

When all potentials are positive, p(x) = \exp(-E(x))/Z with energy E(x) = -\sum_C \log \psi_C(x_C): a Gibbs distribution.

The normalising constant Z, the partition function, is a sum over exponentially many states. It is the main computational obstacle of undirected models, and it returns with energy-based models in ch. 10.

Three Markov properties can hold with respect to G, from weakest to strongest:

  • pairwise: two non-adjacent variables are independent given all the others;
  • local: a variable is independent of the rest given its neighbours;
  • global: x_A \perp x_B \mid x_S whenever S separates A from B in G.

Proposition 4.7 — Factorisation implies the global Markov property If p factorises over G and S separates A from B, then x_A \perp x_B \mid x_S.

Proof. Let \tilde A be the union of the connected components of G \setminus S that meet A, and \tilde B the rest of V \setminus S, which contains B.

No edge joins \tilde A to \tilde B. So every clique lies in \tilde A \cup S or in \tilde B \cup S, and grouping the potentials gives p(x) = f(x_{\tilde A}, x_S)\; g(x_{\tilde B}, x_S).

Proposition 0.2 gives x_{\tilde A} \perp x_{\tilde B} \mid x_S, and the claim follows by marginalisation. ∎

4.4 Hammersley–Clifford, Ising and Gaussian graphical models

Theorem 4.8 — Hammersley–Clifford Let p(x) > 0 for all x. Then p satisfies the pairwise Markov property with respect to G if and only if it factorises over the cliques of G.

Proof. Sketch. The reverse direction is Proposition 4.7. For the direct one, fix a reference state x^\ast. For each set A, let x^A agree with x on A and with x^\ast elsewhere, and define by Möbius inversion \phi_A(x) = \sum_{B \subseteq A} (-1)^{|A \setminus B|} \log p(x^B), \qquad \text{so that} \qquad \log p(x) = \sum_{A} \phi_A(x).

If A contains two non-adjacent nodes i and j, group the terms of \phi_A in fours, according to whether B contains each of the two nodes. Each group is a difference of differences of \log p.

The pairwise property makes it vanish, because \log p is a sum of a term without x_i and a term without x_j.

So \phi_A = 0 unless A is a clique (Besag, 1974; Lauritzen, 1996, chapter 3). ∎

Positivity is needed: there are distributions with zeros that satisfy the Markov properties without factorising.

The Ising model. For spins x \in \{-1, +1\}^n on a graph: p(x) = \frac1Z \exp\Big(\sum_i h_i x_i + \sum_{(i,j) \in E} J_{ij}\, x_i x_j\Big).

It is the prototype of a pairwise Markov random field. Computing its Z on a general graph is #P-hard, which motivates the samplers of ch. 6 (Exercise 10).

Proposition 4.9 — Gaussian graphical models Let x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Theta^{-1}) with precision matrix \Theta. Then for i \ne j: x_i \perp x_j \mid x_{\text{rest}} \iff \Theta_{ij} = 0 .

Proof. The density is proportional to \exp\big(-\frac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Theta (x - \mu)\big). The only term coupling x_i and x_j is -\Theta_{ij}(x_i - \mu_i)(x_j - \mu_j). If \Theta_{ij} = 0, the density factorises as f(x_i, x_{\text{rest}})\, g(x_j, x_{\text{rest}}), and Proposition 0.2 applies.

Conversely, the conditional covariance of (x_i, x_j) given the rest is the inverse of the 2 \times 2 block of \Theta on \{i, j\}, by Proposition 0.3 and the block-inverse formula. Its off-diagonal entry is zero only if \Theta_{ij} = 0. ∎

So the graph of a Gaussian model is read off the zeros of its precision matrix. Estimating a sparse precision matrix with an \ell_1 penalty is the graphical lasso (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2008).

4.5 From directed to undirected: moralisation

Definition 4.10 — Moral graph The moral graph of a DAG is obtained by joining (“marrying”) every pair of parents of a common child, then dropping the directions.

Each factor p(x_i \mid x_{\pi_i}) is a potential on the set \{i\} \cup \pi_i, which is a clique of the moral graph. So a directed factorisation is a Gibbs factorisation on the moral graph, with Z = 1. The converse fails: moralisation hides the marginal independence of the two parents of a collider.

Proposition 4.11 — d-separation by moralisation Let H be the moral graph of the smallest ancestral set containing A \cup B \cup C.

Then d-separation of A and B by C in G is equivalent to separation by C in H.

Proof. Sketch. Summing out the nodes outside an ancestral set leaves a directed factorisation on that set, as in the proof of Proposition 4.2. Moralising it gives a Gibbs factorisation, and Proposition 4.7 applies.

The equivalence of the two separation criteria is a combinatorial argument on paths (Lauritzen, 1996, chapter 3). ∎

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • A DAG factorises the joint into conditionals of each node given its parents; the separation criterion is d-separation.
  • Colliders are special: conditioning on a common effect couples its causes (explaining away).
  • An undirected model factorises into clique potentials divided by the partition function Z.
  • For positive distributions, pairwise Markov and clique factorisation are equivalent (Hammersley–Clifford).
  • In a Gaussian model, missing edges are the zeros of the precision matrix.

5. Exact inference: the sum-product algorithm and hidden Markov models

Once a model is written, every question about it is a sum: a marginal, a conditional given evidence, the partition function. Done naively, these sums have exponentially many terms. On trees, the distributive law reduces them to messages passed along the edges, at linear cost.

5.1 Variable elimination and factor graphs

Take a chain x_1 - x_2 - \dots - x_n with K states each and pairwise potentials. The marginal of x_n is a sum over K^{n-1} configurations. Pushing each sum inside the product removes one variable at a time: \sum_{x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}} \prod_{t=1}^{n-1} \psi_t(x_t, x_{t+1}) = \sum_{x_{n-1}} \psi_{n-1}(x_{n-1}, x_n) \cdots \sum_{x_1} \psi_1(x_1, x_2), which costs O(nK^2) operations instead of O(K^n).

Definition 5.1 — Factor graph A factor graph is a bipartite graph between variable nodes i and factor nodes f, where f is linked to the variables x_f it depends on. It represents p(x) = \frac1Z \prod_f \psi_f(x_f).

Directed and undirected models both convert to factor graphs. On a general graph, the cost of elimination is exponential in the largest clique created during elimination (the treewidth). Exact inference is NP-hard in general (Cooper, 1990).

5.2 Sum-product and max-product on trees

The sum-product algorithm sends messages along the edges of a factor graph. A variable sends to a factor the product of its other incoming messages. A factor sends to a variable the sum, over its other variables, of its potential times their incoming messages: m_{i \to f}(x_i) = \prod_{g \ni i,\, g \ne f} m_{g \to i}(x_i), \qquad m_{f \to i}(x_i) = \sum_{x_{f \setminus i}} \psi_f(x_f) \prod_{j \in f,\, j \ne i} m_{j \to f}(x_j).

Theorem 5.2 — Sum-product is exact on trees If the factor graph is a tree, schedule messages from the leaves to a root, then back to the leaves; this uses two messages per edge. Then, for every variable: p(x_i) = \frac1Z \prod_{f \ni i} m_{f \to i}(x_i), \qquad Z = \sum_{x_i} \prod_{f \ni i} m_{f \to i}(x_i).

Proof. Removing the edge (f, i) splits the tree in two. Let T_{f \to i} be the part containing f. We show by induction on its size that m_{f \to i}(x_i) is the sum, over the variables of T_{f \to i} other than x_i, of the product of its factors.

  • For a leaf factor, this is the definition of the message.
  • In general, T_{f \to i} is f plus the disjoint subtrees hanging from its other variables j. These share no variable, so the sum of the product is a product of sums (the distributive law). Each sum is a message m_{j \to f}, by the induction hypothesis applied to the subtrees of j.

The factors around x_i partition the whole tree, so the product of incoming messages is the unnormalised marginal of x_i. ∎

Replacing sums by maxima gives max-product. It computes max-marginals, and backtracking the argmaxes returns a most probable configuration (MAP). On graphs with cycles, the same updates define loopy belief propagation, which often works well but has no general guarantee (Murphy, Weiss and Jordan, 1999).

5.3 Hidden Markov models: forward–backward and Viterbi

Definition 5.3 — Hidden Markov model A hidden chain z_1, \dots, z_T with K states and observations x_1, \dots, x_T: p(x_{1:T}, z_{1:T}) = \pi_{z_1} \prod_{t=2}^T A_{z_{t-1} z_t} \prod_{t=1}^T b_{z_t}(x_t), with initial law \pi, transition matrix A and emission densities b_k.

The HMM factor graph is a chain, so sum-product applies. Its two passes are the forward and backward recursions, for \alpha_t(k) = p(x_{1:t}, z_t = k) and \beta_t(k) = p(x_{t+1:T} \mid z_t = k): \alpha_{t+1}(k) = b_k(x_{t+1}) \sum_j \alpha_t(j) A_{jk}, \qquad \beta_t(j) = \sum_k A_{jk}\, b_k(x_{t+1})\, \beta_{t+1}(k).

Proposition 5.4 — Posterior marginals of an HMM The likelihood is p(x_{1:T}) = \sum_k \alpha_T(k). The posteriors of one state and of two consecutive states are \gamma_t(k) = \frac{\alpha_t(k)\, \beta_t(k)}{p(x_{1:T})}, \qquad \xi_t(j, k) = \frac{\alpha_t(j)\, A_{jk}\, b_k(x_{t+1})\, \beta_{t+1}(k)}{p(x_{1:T})} .

Proof. Given z_t, the past (x_{1:t}) and the future (x_{t+1:T}) are independent: z_t blocks every path of the chain (Theorem 4.4). So p(x_{1:T}, z_t = k) = p(x_{1:t}, z_t = k)\; p(x_{t+1:T} \mid z_t = k) = \alpha_t(k)\,\beta_t(k).

The same argument at the pair (z_t, z_{t+1}) gives \xi_t. The recursions are sum-product messages on the chain, or follow directly by conditioning on the neighbouring state. ∎

The cost is O(TK^2). The \alpha_t underflow for long sequences, so one normalises them at each step and keeps the normalisers c_t; then \log p(x_{1:T}) = \sum_t \log c_t. The Viterbi algorithm is max-product in the log domain. When the states and observations are Gaussian with linear dynamics, the same recursions become the Kalman filter and smoother.

import itertools
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0.9, 0.1], [0.2, 0.8]])        # transitions  A[i, j] = p(z_t+1 = j | z_t = i)
B = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1], [0.1, 0.3, 0.6]])  # emissions  B[i, o] = p(x_t = o | z_t = i)
pi0 = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
x = [0, 0, 2, 2, 1, 2]
T, K = len(x), 2

alpha, c = np.zeros((T, K)), np.zeros(T)       # scaled forward pass
for t in range(T):
    a = pi0 * B[:, x[t]] if t == 0 else (alpha[t - 1] @ A) * B[:, x[t]]
    c[t] = a.sum(); alpha[t] = a / c[t]
beta = np.ones((T, K))                         # scaled backward pass
for t in range(T - 2, -1, -1):
    beta[t] = A @ (B[:, x[t + 1]] * beta[t + 1]) / c[t + 1]
gamma = alpha * beta                           # p(z_t | x_1:T)

# brute force over the K**T hidden paths
joint = {z: pi0[z[0]] * np.prod([A[z[t - 1], z[t]] for t in range(1, T)])
            * np.prod([B[z[t], x[t]] for t in range(T)])
         for z in itertools.product(range(K), repeat=T)}
px = sum(joint.values())
g_bf = np.array([[sum(p for z, p in joint.items() if z[t] == k) / px for k in range(K)] for t in range(T)])
print(f"log p(x): forward {np.log(c).sum():.6f}   brute force {np.log(px):.6f}")
print("max |gamma - brute force| =", np.abs(gamma - g_bf).max())

delta, back = np.log(pi0 * B[:, x[0]]), []    # Viterbi (max-product in logs)
for t in range(1, T):
    s = delta[:, None] + np.log(A)
    back.append(s.argmax(0)); delta = s.max(0) + np.log(B[:, x[t]])
path = [int(delta.argmax())]
for bp in reversed(back):
    path.append(int(bp[path[-1]]))
print("Viterbi path:", path[::-1], "  brute force:", list(max(joint, key=joint.get)))

The forward pass gives \log p(x_{1:6}) = -6.651829, equal to the sum over the 2^6 = 64 hidden paths. The posteriors agree to 10^{-15}, and Viterbi returns the path (0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1) found by enumeration.

5.4 Learning an HMM: Baum–Welch

Baum–Welch is EM (ch. 2.2) for the HMM. The E-step computes \gamma and \xi by forward–backward. As for mixtures (Proposition 2.4), the M-step replaces counts of states and transitions by their expectations: \pi_k = \gamma_1(k), \qquad A_{jk} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \xi_t(j, k)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \gamma_t(j)}, \qquad b_k(o) = \frac{\sum_t \gamma_t(k)\, \mathbb 1[x_t = o]}{\sum_t \gamma_t(k)} .

With Gaussian emissions, the last update becomes a \gamma-weighted mean and covariance. By Theorem 2.3, each iteration does not decrease the likelihood. As for mixtures, the result depends on the initialisation, and several restarts are standard.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Exact inference is the distributive law: push sums inside products.
  • On a tree factor graph, two passes of sum-product give all marginals and Z; max-product gives the MAP.
  • Forward–backward and Viterbi are sum-product and max-product on the HMM chain, at cost O(TK^2).
  • Baum–Welch is EM with expected transition and emission counts.

6. Approximate inference I: Monte Carlo and MCMC

When the graph has cycles or the variables are continuous and non-Gaussian, exact sums are out of reach. Monte Carlo methods replace them by averages over samples. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) produces such samples from a density known only up to its normalising constant, which is exactly what posteriors and Gibbs distributions provide.

6.1 Monte Carlo and importance sampling

For x_1, \dots, x_n i.i.d. from p, the average of f(x_i) is unbiased for \mathbb E_p[f], with variance \mathrm{Var}_p(f)/n, whatever the dimension.

When the target is hard to sample, draw from a proposal q and reweight.

Proposition 6.1 — Importance sampling If q > 0 wherever fp \ne 0, then \mathbb E_p[f(X)] = \mathbb E_q\big[f(X)\, w(X)\big], \qquad w = \frac{p}{q} .

If p = \tilde p / Z is known only up to Z, the self-normalised estimator with w_i = \tilde p(x_i) / q(x_i) is consistent: \hat I_n = \frac{\sum_i w_i\, f(x_i)}{\sum_i w_i} .

Proof. The first identity is \int f\, \frac{p}{q}\, q = \int f p.

For the second, the numerator and the denominator, divided by n, converge almost surely to Z\, \mathbb E_p[f] and Z, by the law of large numbers. ∎

The quality of the weights is summarised by the effective sample size (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i w_i^2. In high dimension, the weights of a fixed proposal degenerate: a few samples carry all the mass. Importance sampling returns in ch. 8.3, inside the IWAE bound.

6.2 Metropolis–Hastings

Definition 6.2 — Metropolis–Hastings algorithm Target \pi \propto \tilde\pi, proposal kernel q(y \mid x). From the current state x:

  1. draw y \sim q(\cdot \mid x);
  2. accept y with probability a(x, y) = \min\Big(1,\; \frac{\tilde\pi(y)\, q(x \mid y)}{\tilde\pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)}\Big);
  3. otherwise stay at x.

Theorem 6.3 — Metropolis–Hastings leaves the target invariant The Metropolis–Hastings kernel P satisfies detailed balance with respect to \pi, hence \pi P = \pi .

Proof. For y \ne x, the transition density is P(x, y) = q(y \mid x)\, a(x, y). Then \pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)\, a(x, y) = \min\big(\pi(x) q(y \mid x),\; \pi(y) q(x \mid y)\big), which is symmetric in (x, y). So \pi(x) P(x, y) = \pi(y) P(y, x); the rejection mass at x satisfies this trivially.

Integrating detailed balance over x gives \int \pi(x) P(x, y)\, dx = \pi(y). ∎

Invariance is not convergence. If the chain is also irreducible and aperiodic, the law of x_t converges to \pi, and ergodic averages satisfy a law of large numbers (Robert and Casella, 2004). For a random-walk proposal in high dimension, a classical result recommends tuning the step to an acceptance rate near 0.234 (Roberts, Gelman and Gilks, 1997).

6.3 Gibbs sampling

The Gibbs sampler updates one variable at a time from its full conditional p(x_i \mid x_{-i}), cycling over i.

Proposition 6.4 — Gibbs is Metropolis–Hastings with acceptance one The Gibbs update of coordinate i is a Metropolis–Hastings step with proposal q(y \mid x) = p(y_i \mid x_{-i}) for y_{-i} = x_{-i}, and its acceptance probability is 1.

Proof. With y_{-i} = x_{-i}, the factorisation p(x) = p(x_{-i})\, p(x_i \mid x_{-i}) gives \frac{p(y)\, q(x \mid y)}{p(x)\, q(y \mid x)} = \frac{p(x_{-i})\, p(y_i \mid x_{-i})\, p(x_i \mid x_{-i})}{p(x_{-i})\, p(x_i \mid x_{-i})\, p(y_i \mid x_{-i})} = 1.

∎

In a graphical model, the full conditional depends only on the Markov blanket: the neighbours in an undirected graph, or the parents, children and co-parents in a DAG. For the Ising model of ch. 4.4, with \sigma the logistic function: p(x_i = +1 \mid x_{-i}) = \sigma\Big(2\big(h_i + \textstyle\sum_{j} J_{ij} x_j\big)\Big).

Gibbs mixes slowly when variables are strongly coupled: in a highly correlated Gaussian, coordinate moves are tiny. Blocking correlated variables or reparameterising helps.

6.4 Langevin dynamics

Write the target as \pi \propto e^{-U}. The overdamped Langevin diffusion follows the gradient of \log \pi plus noise: dX_t = -\nabla U(X_t)\, dt + \sqrt 2\, dW_t .

Proposition 6.5 — The Langevin diffusion leaves \pi invariant The law \rho_t of X_t solves the Fokker–Planck equation, and \pi is one of its stationary solutions: \partial_t \rho = \nabla \cdot (\rho\, \nabla U) + \Delta \rho = \nabla \cdot \big(\rho\, \nabla U + \nabla \rho\big).

Proof. For \rho = \pi = e^{-U}/Z, we have \nabla \pi = -\pi \nabla U. So the flux \pi \nabla U + \nabla \pi is zero, and \partial_t \pi = 0. Under growth conditions on U, \rho_t \to \pi (Roberts and Tweedie, 1996). ∎

The Euler discretisation with step h is the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA): x_{k+1} = x_k - h\, \nabla U(x_k) + \sqrt{2h}\; \xi_k, \qquad \xi_k \sim \mathcal N(0, I).

ULA is biased: its stationary law differs from \pi by an amount controlled by h (Durmus and Moulines, 2017). Using the ULA move as the proposal of a Metropolis–Hastings step removes the bias: this is MALA.

Example 6.6 — The bias of ULA, computed For \pi = \mathcal N(0, 1), U(x) = x^2/2 and ULA reads x_{k+1} = (1 - h)\,x_k + \sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k. A stationary variance v must satisfy v = (1 - h)^2 v + 2h, hence v = \frac{1}{1 - h/2} .

For h = 0.5, this gives v = 4/3 \approx 1.333 instead of 1.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
U = lambda x: 0.5 * x ** 2            # target pi = N(0, 1), potential U = -log pi
gradU = lambda x: x

def run(h, n=200_000, adjust=False):
    x, xs, acc = 0.0, np.empty(n), 0
    for i in range(n):
        y = x - h * gradU(x) + np.sqrt(2 * h) * rng.normal()      # Langevin proposal
        if adjust:                                                 # MALA: MH correction
            logq = lambda a, b: -(a - b + h * gradU(b)) ** 2 / (4 * h)
            if np.log(rng.random()) < U(x) - U(y) + logq(x, y) - logq(y, x):
                x, acc = y, acc + 1
        else:
            x = y                                                  # ULA: always accept
        xs[i] = x
    return xs.var(), acc / n

for h in [0.1, 0.5]:
    v_ula, _ = run(h)
    v_mala, a = run(h, adjust=True)
    print(f"h = {h}: ULA variance {v_ula:.3f} (theory {1 / (1 - h / 2):.3f})"
          f" | MALA variance {v_mala:.3f}, acceptance {a:.2f}")

With h = 0.5, ULA’s empirical variance is 1.338, as Example 6.6 predicts, while MALA gives 0.995 with 92 % acceptance. Langevin dynamics need only the gradient of \log \pi, the score. Score-based diffusion models (ch. 10.3) run it with a learned score.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Importance sampling reweights samples from a proposal; its weights degenerate in high dimension.
  • Metropolis–Hastings satisfies detailed balance for any proposal and needs \pi only up to a constant.
  • Gibbs sampling is Metropolis–Hastings with full conditionals, which depend only on the Markov blanket.
  • Langevin dynamics follow the score; discretised, they are biased (ULA) unless corrected (MALA).

7. Approximate inference II: variational methods, stochastic block models and expectation propagation

Variational methods turn inference into optimisation: choose, in a tractable family, the distribution closest to the posterior. They are deterministic and fast, and they plug directly into EM. This chapter covers mean field, variational EM, its application to stochastic block models for networks, and expectation propagation, which minimises the KL divergence in the other direction.

7.1 Variational inference and mean field

By Proposition 2.2, maximising the ELBO \mathcal L(q) over a family \mathcal Q is minimising \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p(z \mid x)) over \mathcal Q. The mean-field family makes the latent coordinates independent: q(z) = \prod_{j=1}^m q_j(z_j).

Proposition 7.1 — Coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI) With all factors but q_j fixed, the ELBO is maximised by q_j^\star(z_j) \;\propto\; \exp\Big(\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}\big[\log p(x, z)\big]\Big), where the expectation is over all coordinates except z_j.

Proof. Let \tilde q_j be the right-hand side, normalised. Isolating q_j in the ELBO gives \mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_{q_j}\Big[\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}[\log p(x, z)]\Big] - \mathbb E_{q_j}[\log q_j] + \text{const} = -\mathrm{KL}(q_j \,\|\, \tilde q_j) + \text{const}.

This is maximal at q_j = \tilde q_j, by Gibbs’ inequality. ∎

Cycling over j increases the ELBO at every step, and converges to a local optimum.

Because \mathrm{KL}(q \| p) penalises mass of q where p is small, the solution is mode-seeking: mean field underestimates posterior variances. For a Gaussian target with precision \Lambda, the optimal factor q_j has variance 1/\Lambda_{jj}, never larger than the true marginal variance (Exercise 11).

7.2 Variational EM

When the E-step of EM is intractable, restrict q to \mathcal Q and alternate:

  1. VE-step: maximise \mathcal L(\cdot, \theta_t) over q \in \mathcal Q, for example by CAVI, giving q_{t+1};
  2. M-step: maximise \mathcal L(q_{t+1}, \cdot) over \theta, giving \theta_{t+1}.

By Proposition 3.5, this is coordinate ascent on the free energy over a smaller set. The ELBO increases at each step. The likelihood itself need not increase, because the gap \mathrm{KL}(q \| p(z \mid x; \theta)) no longer vanishes.

7.3 Stochastic block models

Definition 7.2 — Stochastic block model (SBM) Each of n nodes has a latent class z_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}, drawn i.i.d. with p(z_i = k) = \alpha_k. Given the classes, the edges X_{ij}, i < j, are independent: X_{ij} \mid z_i = k,\, z_j = l \;\sim\; \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi_{kl}).

The model was introduced by Holland, Laskey and Leinhardt (1983); its estimation by Nowicki and Snijders (2001).

Diagonal-dominant \pi gives communities, but the model also captures hubs, bipartite and core–periphery structures. Exact EM fails: every edge X_{ij} is a common child of z_i and z_j, so moralisation couples all the labels. The posterior p(z \mid X) does not factorise, and its normaliser sums over K^n labellings.

Proposition 7.3 — Variational EM for the SBM (Daudin, Picard and Robin, 2008) Take the mean-field family q(z) = \prod_i \mathrm{Cat}(z_i; \tau_i). The VE-step solves the fixed-point equations \tau_{ik} \;\propto\; \alpha_k \prod_{j \ne i} \prod_{l} \Big[\pi_{kl}^{X_{ij}} (1 - \pi_{kl})^{1 - X_{ij}}\Big]^{\tau_{jl}} .

The M-step is \alpha_k = \frac1n \sum_i \tau_{ik}, \qquad \pi_{kl} = \frac{\sum_{i \ne j} \tau_{ik}\, \tau_{jl}\, X_{ij}}{\sum_{i \ne j} \tau_{ik}\, \tau_{jl}} .

Proof. The complete log-likelihood is \log p(X, z) = \sum_{i,k} z_{ik} \log \alpha_k + \sum_{i < j} \sum_{k,l} z_{ik} z_{jl} \big[X_{ij} \log \pi_{kl} + (1 - X_{ij}) \log(1 - \pi_{kl})\big], with z_{ik} = \mathbb 1[z_i = k].

Proposition 7.1 takes the expectation over z_{-i}, replacing each z_{jl} by \tau_{jl}, which gives the VE-step.

The M-step maximises the same expression with z_{ik} z_{jl} replaced by \tau_{ik} \tau_{jl}: weighted multinomial and Bernoulli likelihoods. ∎

The number of classes is chosen by the integrated classification likelihood (ICL). For an undirected graph, Daudin et al. (2008) penalise the complete-data log-likelihood, evaluated at the estimated labels, by \frac{K - 1}{2} \log n + \frac{K(K+1)}{4} \log \frac{n(n-1)}{2} .

The first term counts the K - 1 free proportions; the second counts the K(K+1)/2 connection probabilities, estimated from n(n-1)/2 pairs.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score as ari

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
n, K = 300, 3
z = rng.choice(K, n, p=[0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
P = np.full((K, K), 0.04) + 0.10 * np.eye(K)                 # assortative SBM
A = np.triu(rng.random((n, n)) < P[z][:, z], 1).astype(float)
A = A + A.T                                                   # undirected, no self-loop

U = np.linalg.eigh(A)[1][:, -K:]                              # spectral initialisation
z0 = KMeans(K, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(U)
tau = np.eye(K)[z0] * 0.9 + 0.1 / K
off = 1 - A - np.eye(n)                                       # non-edges, j != i
for it in range(50):
    alpha = tau.mean(0)                                       # M-step
    Pi = (tau.T @ A @ tau) / (tau.T @ (1 - np.eye(n)) @ tau)
    Pi = Pi.clip(1e-6, 1 - 1e-6)
    for _ in range(5):                                        # E-step: fixed point on tau
        S = np.log(alpha) + A @ tau @ np.log(Pi).T + off @ tau @ np.log(1 - Pi).T
        tau = np.exp(S - S.max(1, keepdims=True))
        tau /= tau.sum(1, keepdims=True)

print("ARI spectral init:", round(ari(z, z0), 3))
print("ARI after VEM:    ", round(ari(z, tau.argmax(1)), 3))
print("estimated alpha", np.round(np.sort(alpha), 2), "\nestimated Pi\n", np.round(Pi, 3))

Spectral clustering of the adjacency matrix gives an adjusted Rand index of 0.86. Variational EM, started from it, reaches 0.975, and recovers the proportions (0.23, 0.29, 0.48) and connection probabilities within 0.005 of the truth (0.14 inside, 0.04 between).

7.4 Expectation propagation

Expectation propagation (EP; Minka, 2001) targets posteriors of the form p(\theta \mid \mathcal D) \;\propto\; p_0(\theta) \prod_{i=1}^n t_i(\theta), and approximates each site t_i by a member \tilde t_i of an exponential family. Its building block is a KL projection in the direction opposite to variational inference.

Proposition 7.4 — Projection by moment matching Let \mathcal F be an exponential family with sufficient statistic T (Definition 0.5). If the minimiser q^\star = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal F} \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) exists, it matches the expected sufficient statistics: \mathbb E_{q^\star}[T(X)] = \mathbb E_p[T(X)].

Proof. For q_\eta \in \mathcal F, expanding the logarithm gives, with c independent of \eta: \mathrm{KL}(p \| q_\eta) = -\eta^\top \mathbb E_p[T] + A(\eta) + c .

This is convex in \eta, and by Proposition 0.6 its gradient is \mathbb E_{q_\eta}[T] - \mathbb E_p[T]. It vanishes at the minimiser. ∎

For a Gaussian family, q^\star matches the mean and the covariance of p. EP iterates over the sites:

  1. cavity: remove site i, giving q^{\setminus i} \propto q / \tilde t_i;
  2. tilted distribution: put back the exact site, giving \hat p_i \propto q^{\setminus i}\, t_i;
  3. projection: set q_{\text{new}} to the moment-matched projection of \hat p_i onto \mathcal F (Proposition 7.4);
  4. site update: \tilde t_i \propto q_{\text{new}} / q^{\setminus i}.

Each step needs only one-dimensional or low-dimensional integrals against one exact site, which are often available in closed form, as for probit likelihoods. A single pass is assumed density filtering. EP has no general convergence guarantee. Where it converges, it tends to cover the posterior mass better than mean field, which is why it is a standard choice for Gaussian-process classification (Rasmussen and Williams, 2006, chapter 3).

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Variational inference maximises the ELBO over a tractable family; CAVI updates each factor to the exponential of an expected log-joint.
  • Mean field is mode-seeking and underestimates variances.
  • Variational EM increases the ELBO, not necessarily the likelihood.
  • SBM labels are coupled by every edge; mean-field VEM and ICL make the model usable on networks.
  • EP projects local tilted distributions by moment matching, minimising KL in the other direction.

8. Deep latent variable models, variational auto-encoders and amortised inference

Replace the linear map of probabilistic PCA (ch. 2.4) by a neural network, and the model can generate images, sounds or molecules. The price is that neither the likelihood nor the posterior has a closed form any more. This chapter combines the ELBO of ch. 2, an inference network shared by all data points, and a gradient estimator that goes through sampling.

8.1 Deep latent variable models

Definition 8.1 — Deep latent variable model (DLVM) A prior p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I_q) and an observation model whose parameters are the output of a neural network f_\theta, the decoder: for example x \mid z \sim \mathcal N(f_\theta(z), \sigma^2 I) for real data, or independent Bernoulli pixels with logits f_\theta(z). The likelihood is p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz .

The integral has no closed form, and neither does the posterior p_\theta(z \mid x), so the E-step of EM is impossible. A further subtlety: with a Gaussian decoder whose variance is also learned, the likelihood of a DLVM can be unbounded, like that of a Gaussian mixture (ch. 2.1). Mattei and Frellsen (2018) analyse this degeneracy.

8.2 Amortised variational inference and the VAE

Variational EM would optimise one distribution q_i(z) per data point. Amortised inference replaces them by a single network, the encoder, that maps x to the parameters of q_\phi(z \mid x), typically a Gaussian with mean \mu_\phi(x) and diagonal variances \sigma^2_\phi(x). The variational auto-encoder (VAE; Kingma and Welling, 2014; Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra, 2014) maximises over (\theta, \phi) the sum over data points of

Key result — the VAE objective \mathcal L(\theta, \phi; x) = \mathbb E_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big) \;\le\; \log p_\theta(x).

The first term is a reconstruction error; the second keeps the codes close to the prior. For a diagonal Gaussian q and a standard normal prior, the KL term is explicit: \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal N(\mu, \mathrm{diag}\,\sigma^2) \,\|\, \mathcal N(0, I)\big) = \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^q \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2\big).

Proposition 8.2 — The reparameterisation trick Write z as a differentiable function of \phi and of a noise \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I) whose law does not depend on \phi: z = g_\phi(\varepsilon, x) = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \varepsilon .

For a differentiable h, under domination conditions: \nabla_\phi\, \mathbb E_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\big[h(z)\big] = \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_\phi\, h(g_\phi(\varepsilon, x))\big].

Proof. The law of g_\phi(\varepsilon, x) is q_\phi(\cdot \mid x), so the two expectations are equal for every \phi.

The law of \varepsilon does not depend on \phi; differentiation under the integral sign, justified by dominated convergence, moves the gradient inside. ∎

A single draw of \varepsilon gives an unbiased gradient of the ELBO, usually with much lower variance than the score-function estimator \mathbb E_q[h(z)\, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z \mid x)], which also works for discrete z.

The gap between \log p_\theta(x) and the ELBO splits in two parts (Cremer, Li and Duvenaud, 2018):

  • the approximation gap: even the best Gaussian q for this x differs from the posterior;
  • the amortisation gap: the encoder’s output is not that best Gaussian.

A known failure is posterior collapse: with a very flexible decoder, the optimum can ignore z and set q_\phi(z \mid x) = p(z), making the KL term zero.

8.3 Importance-weighted bounds

The ELBO is \mathbb E_q[\log w] with w = p_\theta(x, z) / q_\phi(z \mid x). Averaging several weights inside the logarithm gives a tighter bound.

Theorem 8.3 — Importance-weighted bounds (Burda, Grosse and Salakhutdinov, 2016) For z_1, \dots, z_K i.i.d. from q_\phi(\cdot \mid x) and w_k = p_\theta(x, z_k) / q_\phi(z_k \mid x), define \mathcal L_K = \mathbb E\Big[\log \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^K w_k\Big].

Then \mathcal L_1 is the ELBO and \mathcal L_1 \le \mathcal L_K \le \mathcal L_{K+1} \le \log p_\theta(x).

If the weights are bounded, \mathcal L_K \to \log p_\theta(x) as K \to \infty.

Proof. Upper bound. Each w_k has expectation p_\theta(x), by Proposition 6.1. Jensen’s inequality for the concave logarithm gives \mathcal L_K \le \log p_\theta(x).

Monotonicity. Let I be a uniformly random subset of size K of \{1, \dots, K+1\}. Then \frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=1}^{K+1} w_k = \mathbb E_I\Big[\frac1K \sum_{k \in I} w_k\Big].

By Jensen again, the logarithm of the left side is at least \mathbb E_I[\log \frac1K \sum_{k \in I} w_k]. Its expectation is \mathcal L_K, by exchangeability.

Limit. The average of the weights tends to p_\theta(x) almost surely. Bounded weights make the logarithm dominated, and the expectation converges. ∎

Training on \mathcal L_K gives the IWAE; even a VAE trained on \mathcal L_1 is best evaluated with a large K. The code trains a small VAE on the 8×8 digits bundled with scikit-learn, binarised, then evaluates \mathcal L_K on held-out images.

import math
import torch
from torch import nn
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits

torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)
X = torch.tensor(load_digits().data / 16.0, dtype=torch.float32)   # 1797 images 8x8 in [0, 1]
X = (X > 0.5).float()                                              # binarise: Bernoulli pixels
Xtr, Xte = X[:1500], X[1500:]
d, q, h = 64, 4, 128
enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, h), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(h, 2 * q))   # amortised q(z|x)
dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(q, h), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(h, d))       # logits of p(x|z)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([*enc.parameters(), *dec.parameters()], lr=1e-3)

def log_weights(x, K):
    mu, logvar = enc(x).chunk(2, -1)
    z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * torch.randn(K, *mu.shape)      # reparameterisation
    log_px_z = -nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        dec(z), x.expand(K, *x.shape), reduction="none").sum(-1)
    log_pz = -0.5 * (z ** 2 + math.log(2 * math.pi)).sum(-1)
    log_qz = -0.5 * (((z - mu) ** 2) / logvar.exp() + logvar + math.log(2 * math.pi)).sum(-1)
    return log_px_z + log_pz - log_qz                                  # shape (K, batch)

for epoch in range(60):
    for xb in Xtr[torch.randperm(1500)].split(100):
        loss = -log_weights(xb, 1).mean()                              # negative ELBO
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    for K in [1, 10, 100]:
        lw = log_weights(Xte, K)
        bound = (torch.logsumexp(lw, 0) - math.log(K)).mean()          # IWAE bound
        print(f"K = {K:3d}: test bound on log p(x) = {bound:.2f} nats")

On the 297 test images, the bound rises from −19.84 nats (K = 1) to −19.66 (K = 10) and −19.62 (K = 100): the ELBO underestimates the log-likelihood by at least 0.2 nats per image. Training takes a few seconds on a CPU.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • A DLVM is PPCA with a neural decoder; its likelihood and posterior are intractable.
  • A VAE maximises the ELBO with an amortised Gaussian encoder, using the reparameterisation trick for low-variance gradients.
  • The ELBO gap is an approximation gap plus an amortisation gap.
  • Importance-weighted bounds increase with K and converge to \log p_\theta(x).

9. Deep generative models beyond VAEs: autoregressive models, normalizing flows and GANs

A VAE gives only a bound on the likelihood. Three other families make different trade-offs. Autoregressive models and normalizing flows give the exact likelihood, at a price on sampling or on architecture. GANs give up the likelihood altogether and learn to sample by competing with a classifier.

9.1 Autoregressive models

By the chain rule, any distribution on x = (x_1, \dots, x_D) factorises without assumption: p(x) = \prod_{d=1}^D p(x_d \mid x_{<d}).

An autoregressive model parameterises every conditional with one network, whose weights are shared across positions. As a directed graphical model, it is the complete DAG in a fixed order.

  • Likelihood: exact, a sum of D log-conditionals; training is maximum likelihood (teacher forcing).
  • Architectures: masks enforce the ordering, in MADE (Germain et al., 2015) and PixelCNN (van den Oord, Kalchbrenner and Kavukcuoglu, 2016); causal transformers do the same for text.
  • Sampling: sequential, D network evaluations per sample.

The large language models of Foundations of Large Language Models are autoregressive models of token sequences.

9.2 Normalizing flows

Theorem 9.1 — Change of variables Let u \sim p_U on \mathbb R^D and x = g(u), with g a C^1 diffeomorphism. Then \log p_X(x) = \log p_U\big(g^{-1}(x)\big) + \log \big|\det J_{g^{-1}}(x)\big| .

For a composition g = g_L \circ \dots \circ g_1, the log-determinants of the inverse maps add up.

Proof. For a Borel set B, substitute u = g^{-1}(x): P(x \in B) = \int_{g^{-1}(B)} p_U(u)\, du = \int_B p_U(g^{-1}(x))\, |\det J_{g^{-1}}(x)|\, dx .

The chain rule turns the Jacobian of a composition into a product of Jacobians, whose determinants multiply. ∎

The determinant of a general D \times D Jacobian costs O(D^3). Flows use layers whose Jacobian is triangular.

Proposition 9.2 — Affine coupling layers (Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio, 2017) Split x = (x_a, x_b), and let s and t be arbitrary networks. The map y_a = x_a, \qquad y_b = x_b \odot \exp\big(s(x_a)\big) + t(x_a) is invertible, and its log-Jacobian determinant is the sum of the coordinates of s(x_a).

Proof. Inverse: x_a = y_a and x_b = (y_b - t(y_a)) \odot \exp(-s(y_a)), which never inverts s or t.

The Jacobian is block lower-triangular, with the identity in the a block and the diagonal matrix \mathrm{diag}(\exp s(x_a)) in the b block. Its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries. ∎

Alternating which half is transformed lets every coordinate depend on every other after a few layers. Flows keep the dimension and the topology of the base distribution. To send a Gaussian onto two separate moons, a flow must stretch space strongly between them.

import math
import torch
from torch import nn
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons

torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)
X = torch.tensor(make_moons(2000, noise=0.05, random_state=0)[0], dtype=torch.float32)
X = (X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0)

class Coupling(nn.Module):                     # affine coupling: x_b -> x_b * exp(s(x_a)) + t(x_a)
    def __init__(self, swap):
        super().__init__()
        self.swap = swap
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 2))
    def forward(self, x):                      # data -> latent, returns log|det J|
        a, b = (x[:, 1:], x[:, :1]) if self.swap else (x[:, :1], x[:, 1:])
        s, t = self.net(a).chunk(2, -1)
        s = torch.tanh(s)
        b = b * torch.exp(s) + t
        out = torch.cat([b, a], 1) if self.swap else torch.cat([a, b], 1)
        return out, s.sum(-1)

layers = nn.ModuleList([Coupling(i % 2 == 1) for i in range(6)])
opt = torch.optim.Adam(layers.parameters(), lr=3e-3)

def log_prob(x):
    logdet = torch.zeros(len(x))
    for f in layers:
        x, ld = f(x)
        logdet = logdet + ld
    return -0.5 * (x ** 2).sum(-1) - math.log(2 * math.pi) + logdet   # change of variables

for step in range(1500):
    loss = -log_prob(X[torch.randint(0, 2000, (256,))]).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 500 == 0 or step == 1499:
        print(f"step {step:4d}: average log-likelihood {-loss.item():.3f}")
C = torch.cov(X.T)                              # best Gaussian (maximum likelihood) for comparison
print(f"best Gaussian: average log-likelihood {-0.5 * (2 * (1 + math.log(2 * math.pi)) + torch.logdet(C).item()):.3f}")

Six coupling layers raise the average log-likelihood from −3.04 to −1.16 per point. The best Gaussian on the same standardised data reaches only −2.73. Each layer here maps data to the latent space, which is the direction the likelihood needs; sampling uses the inverse of Proposition 9.2.

9.3 Generative adversarial networks

A generator G maps noise z \sim p(z) to samples with law p_G. A discriminator D : x \mapsto [0, 1] tries to tell data from samples. The GAN of Goodfellow et al. (2014) solves \min_G \max_D \; V(D, G) = \mathbb E_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(x)\big] + \mathbb E_{z \sim p(z)}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big].

Theorem 9.3 — Optimal discriminator and Jensen–Shannon divergence For a fixed generator, the optimal discriminator is D^\star(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_G(x)}, and then V(D^\star, G) = -\log 4 + 2\, \mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_G).

The global minimum over G is reached exactly when p_G = p_{\text{data}}.

Proof. Write V as an integral over x of a \log D(x) + b \log(1 - D(x)), with a = p_{\text{data}}(x) and b = p_G(x).

For a, b > 0, the function y \mapsto a \log y + b \log(1 - y) is maximal at y = a/(a + b).

Substituting D^\star and setting m = (p_{\text{data}} + p_G)/2: V(D^\star, G) = \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \| m) + \mathrm{KL}(p_G \| m) - \log 4 .

The two KL terms sum to 2\,\mathrm{JS}, which is non-negative and zero only when the two laws are equal (Gibbs’ inequality). ∎

In practice D is never optimal, and the generator usually maximises \log D(G(z)) instead, which gives stronger gradients early in training. GANs need no likelihood and produce sharp samples. They can also miss modes (mode collapse) and are hard to evaluate. The Wasserstein GAN (Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017) replaces the JS divergence by a transport distance.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Autoregressive models factorise by the chain rule: exact likelihood, sequential sampling.
  • Normalizing flows use the change of variables; coupling layers make the Jacobian triangular.
  • A GAN with an optimal discriminator minimises a Jensen–Shannon divergence; it samples well but has no likelihood.

10. Energy-based models and score-based diffusion models

An energy-based model specifies a density up to its normalising constant, like the undirected models of ch. 4.3, with a neural network as the energy. Its likelihood gradient needs samples from the model, which MCMC provides only slowly. Score matching avoids the constant altogether. Learning the score of noisy versions of the data, then running a reverse diffusion, gives today’s diffusion models.

10.1 Energy-based models

Definition 10.1 — Energy-based model (EBM) For an energy network E_\theta : \mathbb R^D \to \mathbb R: p_\theta(x) = \frac{\exp(-E_\theta(x))}{Z_\theta}, \qquad Z_\theta = \int \exp(-E_\theta(x))\, dx < \infty .

The Ising model (ch. 4.4) is an EBM with an energy linear in its parameters. A neural energy can express any shape, but Z_\theta is intractable.

Proposition 10.2 — Likelihood gradient of an EBM \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x) + \mathbb E_{X \sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(X)\big].

Proof. Differentiate \log Z_\theta under the integral: \nabla_\theta \log Z_\theta = \frac{1}{Z_\theta}\int -\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\, e^{-E_\theta(x)}\, dx = -\mathbb E_{p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta E_\theta\big].

∎

Averaged over the data, the first term lowers the energy of observed points (positive phase). The second raises the energy of model samples (negative phase), and needs MCMC from p_\theta, typically Langevin (ch. 6.4). Contrastive divergence (Hinton, 2002) starts the chains at the data and runs only a few steps. This is cheap but biased.

10.2 Score matching

The score \nabla_x \log p(x) does not involve the normalising constant. Score matching fits a model score s_\theta(x) to the data score by minimising the Fisher divergence J(\theta) = \frac12\, \mathbb E_{p}\Big[\big\|s_\theta(x) - \nabla_x \log p(x)\big\|^2\Big].

Theorem 10.3 — Score matching without the data score (Hyvärinen, 2005) If p\, s_\theta vanishes at infinity and the expectations are finite, then, up to a constant independent of \theta: J(\theta) = \mathbb E_{p}\Big[\mathrm{tr}\, \nabla_x s_\theta(x) + \frac12 \|s_\theta(x)\|^2\Big] + \text{const}.

Proof. Expanding the square, the term in \|\nabla \log p\|^2 does not depend on \theta. For the cross term, integrate by parts in each coordinate j, using p\, \partial_j \log p = \partial_j p: \mathbb E_p\big[s_{\theta,j}\, \partial_j \log p\big] = \int s_{\theta,j}\, \partial_j p\, dx = -\int \partial_j s_{\theta,j}\; p\, dx .

The boundary term vanishes by assumption. Summing over j gives the trace. ∎

The trace of a Jacobian is expensive for networks in high dimension. Denoising avoids it. Perturb the data with Gaussian noise, \tilde x = x + \sigma \varepsilon, and let p_\sigma be the law of \tilde x.

Theorem 10.4 — Denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011) Up to a constant independent of \theta: \mathbb E_{p_\sigma}\big\|s_\theta(\tilde x) - \nabla \log p_\sigma(\tilde x)\big\|^2 = \mathbb E_{x, \varepsilon}\Big\|s_\theta(x + \sigma\varepsilon) + \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma}\Big\|^2 + \text{const}.

Proof. Write q(\tilde x \mid x) = \mathcal N(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I), whose score in \tilde x is -\varepsilon / \sigma. Expanding both squares, the terms in \|s_\theta\|^2 agree. The cross terms agree too: \int s_\theta \cdot \nabla p_\sigma\, d\tilde x = \iint s_\theta(\tilde x) \cdot \nabla_{\tilde x} q(\tilde x \mid x)\, p(x)\, dx\, d\tilde x = \mathbb E_{x, \tilde x}\big[s_\theta(\tilde x) \cdot \nabla_{\tilde x} \log q(\tilde x \mid x)\big].

The remaining terms do not depend on \theta. ∎

So the network learns to predict the noise: -\sigma s_\theta(\tilde x) regresses \varepsilon. The minimiser is linked to the best denoiser by Tweedie’s formula (Exercise 14): \mathbb E[x \mid \tilde x] = \tilde x + \sigma^2\, \nabla \log p_\sigma(\tilde x).

10.3 Score-based diffusion models

Diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020; Song et al., 2021) learn scores at all noise levels at once. A forward SDE carries the data distribution p_0 to a simple law p_T: dx = f(x, t)\, dt + g(t)\, dW_t .

In the variance-exploding case, f = 0 and x_t = x_0 + \sigma(t)\,\varepsilon with \sigma(t) increasing; then p_T is nearly \mathcal N(0, \sigma(T)^2 I).

In the variance-preserving case, f(x, t) = -\frac12 \beta(t)\, x, and p_T is nearly \mathcal N(0, I); its discretisation is DDPM.

Theorem 10.5 — Reverse-time SDE and probability-flow ODE Let p_t be the marginals of the forward SDE. The SDE run backwards in time from x_T \sim p_T, dx = \big[f(x, t) - g(t)^2\, \nabla_x \log p_t(x)\big]\, dt + g(t)\, d\bar W_t , has the same marginals p_t (Anderson, 1982). So does the deterministic probability-flow ODE: \frac{dx}{dt} = f(x, t) - \frac12 g(t)^2\, \nabla_x \log p_t(x).

Proof. Marginals. The forward Fokker–Planck equation is \partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (f\, p_t) + \tfrac12 g^2 \Delta p_t .

Since \Delta p_t = \nabla \cdot (p_t \nabla \log p_t), it can be written as a pure transport equation: \partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot \Big(\big(f - \tfrac12 g^2 \nabla \log p_t\big)\, p_t\Big).

This is the continuity equation of the ODE, which therefore transports p_t. The same rewriting, with the time reversed, checks the reverse SDE. The statement on path laws is Anderson’s. ∎

The unknown score is replaced by a network s_\theta(x, t), trained by denoising score matching (Theorem 10.4) over noise levels. Sampling integrates the reverse SDE or the ODE. Because the ODE is a continuous normalizing flow (ch. 9.2), it also gives a likelihood.

import math
import torch
from torch import nn

torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)
# data: mixture of 8 Gaussians on a circle of radius 2
ang = 2 * math.pi * torch.randint(0, 8, (4000,)) / 8
X = 2 * torch.stack([ang.cos(), ang.sin()], 1) + 0.1 * torch.randn(4000, 2)

sig_min, sig_max = 0.01, 3.0                       # variance-exploding noise scales
net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(3, 128), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(128, 128), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(128, 2))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
score = lambda x, s: net(torch.cat([x, s.log()], 1)) / s   # s_theta(x, sigma)

for step in range(3000):                           # denoising score matching
    x0 = X[torch.randint(0, 4000, (512,))]
    s = sig_min * (sig_max / sig_min) ** torch.rand(512, 1)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    loss = ((s * score(x0 + s * eps, s) + eps) ** 2).sum(1).mean()   # sigma^2-weighted DSM
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():                              # reverse-time SDE, Euler-Maruyama
    N = 500
    sig = sig_min * (sig_max / sig_min) ** torch.linspace(1, 0, N + 1)
    x = sig_max * torch.randn(2000, 2)
    for i in range(N):
        g2 = sig[i] ** 2 - sig[i + 1] ** 2          # g(t)^2 dt for this step
        s = torch.full((2000, 1), sig[i].item())
        x = x + g2 * score(x, s) + g2.sqrt() * torch.randn_like(x)
r = x.norm(dim=1)
print(f"radius of samples: mean {r.mean():.3f}, sd {r.std():.3f} (data: 2.0, ~0.1)")
k = torch.remainder(torch.round(torch.atan2(x[:, 1], x[:, 0]) / (2 * math.pi / 8)), 8)
print("samples per mode:", torch.bincount(k.long(), minlength=8).tolist())

The 2000 samples have mean radius 1.986 and standard deviation 0.115, against 2 and about 0.1 for the data. All eight modes are populated, with between 220 and 347 samples each (250 expected): no mode collapse, but the weights are not perfectly balanced.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • An EBM’s likelihood gradient is a positive phase on data minus a negative phase on model samples.
  • Score matching removes the partition function; denoising score matching reduces it to predicting the added noise.
  • A diffusion model learns the scores of noisy data and samples with the reverse SDE or the probability-flow ODE.

11. Causal inference: potential outcomes, treatment effects and policy learning

A predictive model learns p(y \mid x, w); a decision needs to know what would happen if we set the treatment w. The two coincide in a randomised experiment, and differ when treatment depends on variables that also affect the outcome. This chapter states when causal effects are identified from observational data, how to estimate them, and how to learn whom to treat.

11.1 Potential outcomes

Definition 11.1 — Potential outcomes and treatment effects Each unit i has covariates X_i, a binary treatment W_i and two potential outcomes Y_i(0) and Y_i(1). Only Y_i = Y_i(W_i) is observed. This assumes no interference between units and a single version of the treatment (SUTVA). The average and conditional average treatment effects are \tau = \mathbb E\big[Y(1) - Y(0)\big], \qquad \tau(x) = \mathbb E\big[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x\big].

The fundamental problem of causal inference is that Y_i(0) and Y_i(1) are never observed together. Causal effects must therefore be identified from assumptions on how treatment was assigned.

11.2 Identification: randomisation and ignorability

In a randomised experiment, W is independent of (Y(0), Y(1)). Then \mathbb E[Y \mid W = 1] = \mathbb E[Y(1)], and the difference of the two group means is unbiased for \tau.

In observational data, write the propensity score e(x) = P(W = 1 \mid X = x) and the outcome regressions \mu_w(x) = \mathbb E[Y \mid X = x, W = w].

Theorem 11.2 — Identification under ignorability and overlap Assume ignorability, (Y(0), Y(1)) \perp W \mid X, and overlap, \eta \le e(X) \le 1 - \eta for some \eta > 0. Then \tau = \mathbb E\big[\mu_1(X) - \mu_0(X)\big] = \mathbb E\Big[\frac{W Y}{e(X)} - \frac{(1 - W)\, Y}{1 - e(X)}\Big].

Proof. By ignorability, then because Y = Y(1) when W = 1: \mathbb E[Y(1) \mid X] = \mathbb E[Y(1) \mid X, W = 1] = \mu_1(X).

Overlap makes the conditioning event non-negligible. Average over X; the same holds for Y(0).

For the weighted form, condition on X and use WY = WY(1): \mathbb E\Big[\frac{W Y(1)}{e(X)} \,\Big|\, X\Big] = \frac{\mathbb E[W \mid X]\; \mathbb E[Y(1) \mid X]}{e(X)} = \mathbb E[Y(1) \mid X], where ignorability allowed the factorisation. ∎

In the language of ch. 4, ignorability holds when X blocks every back-door path from W to Y in a causal DAG, as in W \leftarrow X \to Y; the first formula is then Pearl’s back-door adjustment (Pearl, 2009). Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) show that conditioning on the scalar e(X) suffices.

11.3 Inverse-propensity weighting and doubly robust estimation

Plugging estimates into Theorem 11.2 gives two estimators.

  • Inverse-propensity weighting (IPW) averages W_i Y_i / \hat e(X_i) - (1 - W_i) Y_i / (1 - \hat e(X_i)). Its variance explodes when \hat e approaches 0 or 1.
  • Regression adjustment averages \hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i). It is biased when \hat\mu_w is misspecified.

The augmented IPW (AIPW) estimator combines them (Robins, Rotnitzky and Zhao, 1994). It averages the scores \Gamma_i = \hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i) + \frac{W_i\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_1(X_i))}{\hat e(X_i)} - \frac{(1 - W_i)\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_0(X_i))}{1 - \hat e(X_i)} .

Theorem 11.3 — Double robustness Let \hat\mu_w and \hat e be fixed functions, with \hat e satisfying overlap. If either \hat\mu_w = \mu_w for w \in \{0, 1\}, or \hat e = e, then \mathbb E[\Gamma] = \tau .

Proof. Consider the treated part. Conditioning on X, ignorability gives \mathbb E\Big[\hat\mu_1(X) + \frac{W (Y - \hat\mu_1(X))}{\hat e(X)} \,\Big|\, X\Big] = \hat\mu_1(X) + \frac{e(X)}{\hat e(X)}\big(\mu_1(X) - \hat\mu_1(X)\big).

  • If \hat\mu_1 = \mu_1, the correction term is zero.
  • If \hat e = e, the ratio is 1 and the expression equals \mu_1(X).

Either way, its expectation is \mathbb E[Y(1)]. The control part is symmetric. ∎

With machine-learning estimates of the nuisances, fitted on separate folds (cross-fitting), AIPW is \sqrt n-consistent and asymptotically normal as soon as both nuisance errors decrease faster than n^{-1/4}: this is double/debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). The code simulates confounded data with a true ATE of 1.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression, LinearRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 20_000
X = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))
e = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(1.5 * X[:, 0] - X[:, 1])))          # propensity: confounded assignment
W = rng.random(n) < e
tau = 1.0 + X[:, 1]                                        # heterogeneous effect, ATE = 1
Y = 2 * X[:, 0] + X[:, 1] + W * tau + rng.normal(size=n)

naive = Y[W].mean() - Y[~W].mean()
e_hat = LogisticRegression().fit(X, W).predict_proba(X)[:, 1]
ipw = np.mean(W * Y / e_hat - (~W) * Y / (1 - e_hat))
m1 = LinearRegression().fit(X[W], Y[W]).predict(X)        # outcome models mu_1, mu_0
m0 = LinearRegression().fit(X[~W], Y[~W]).predict(X)
psi = m1 - m0 + W * (Y - m1) / e_hat - (~W) * (Y - m0) / (1 - e_hat)
aipw, se = psi.mean(), psi.std() / np.sqrt(n)               # AIPW and its standard error
print(f"true ATE 1.000 | naive {naive:.3f} | IPW {ipw:.3f} | AIPW {aipw:.3f} +/- {1.96 * se:.3f}")

The naive difference of means gives 1.946, because treated units have larger X_1, which also raises Y. IPW gives 1.109, unbiased but noisy. AIPW gives 1.002, with a 95 % confidence half-width of 0.054.

11.4 Policy learning

A policy \pi : x \mapsto \{0, 1\} decides whom to treat. Its value is V(\pi) = \mathbb E[Y(\pi(X))]. The best unrestricted policy treats exactly when \tau(x) > 0. Under the assumptions of Theorem 11.2, the advantage of \pi over treating nobody is identified and estimated with the AIPW scores: V(\pi) - V(0) = \mathbb E\big[\pi(X)\, \tau(X)\big], \qquad \hat A(\pi) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \pi(X_i)\, \Gamma_i .

Policy learning maximises \hat A over an interpretable class \Pi, such as shallow decision trees. Athey and Wager (2021) show that, with cross-fitted doubly robust scores, the regret of the learned policy against the best policy in \Pi is of order \sqrt{\mathrm{VC}(\Pi)/n}. In the simulation above, \tau(x) = 1 + x_2, so the best policy treats when x_2 > -1 (Exercise 15).

Key takeaways — chapter 11

  • Causal effects compare potential outcomes, of which only one is observed per unit.
  • Ignorability and overlap identify the ATE, by regression adjustment or by inverse-propensity weighting.
  • AIPW is unbiased if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correct; with cross-fitting it supports machine-learning nuisances.
  • Policy learning maximises a doubly robust estimate of the value over a class of policies.

12. Research directions and open problems

This self-study chapter follows five threads of the course into current research. Each paragraph names the question, a few papers that shaped it, and what remains open. It assumes ch. 0–11.

12.1 Tighter bounds and better amortisation

The importance-weighted bound of Theorem 8.3 raised a question about training. Rainforth and coauthors (2018) show that, as the number of samples K grows, the signal-to-noise ratio of the encoder’s gradient estimate decreases. A tighter bound for the decoder can thus mean a worse-trained encoder.

Cremer, Li and Duvenaud (2018) measure the amortisation gap separately from the approximation gap. On their benchmarks, the amortisation gap is often the larger of the two.

  • Open: how to train inference networks whose gap is controlled, and how much the encoder family matters once the decoder is expressive.

12.2 Deep latent variable models with missing data

Work by Mattei and Frellsen (2018) shows that the likelihood of a DLVM with Gaussian outputs and learned variances can be unbounded, as for Gaussian mixtures (ch. 2.1). They also give conditions under which the maximum-likelihood problem is well posed.

The same authors’ MIWAE (Mattei and Frellsen, 2019) trains a DLVM on data with entries missing at random, by an importance-weighted bound over the observed coordinates only. It then imputes the missing entries by self-normalised importance sampling (Proposition 6.1).

When missingness depends on the missing values themselves (missing not at random), not-MIWAE (Ipsen, Mattei and Frellsen, 2021) adds a model of the missingness mechanism.

  • Open: identifiability of the data model when the missingness mechanism is also learned, and guarantees for the imputations.

12.3 Network models

For stochastic block models, Celisse, Daudin and Pierre (2012) prove that the maximum-likelihood and variational estimators of the parameters are consistent in dense graphs. Latouche, Birmelé and Ambroise (2012) develop variational Bayes inference for the SBM, with a model selection criterion derived from the variational bound.

In sparse graphs, recovery has sharp thresholds. Decelle, Krzakala, Moore and Zdeborová (2011) predicted, by statistical-physics arguments, that for two symmetric communities with expected degrees a inside and b across, detection is possible only when (a - b)^2 > 2(a + b). Abbe (2018) surveys the proofs that followed.

  • Open: guarantees for selecting the number of classes, and models for networks with node attributes, weights or time stamps.

12.4 Theory of diffusion models

Convergence guarantees for diffusion models now hold under weak assumptions. Chen, Chewi, Li, Li, Salim and Zhang (2023) show that, if the learned score is accurate in L^2, the sampled distribution is close in total variation to the data distribution. Their bound grows polynomially in the dimension, without log-concavity. De Bortoli (2022) gives bounds when the data lie on a low-dimensional manifold.

A second question is why trained models generalise instead of reproducing their training set, since the exact score of the empirical distribution would only return training points. Kadkhodaie, Guth, Simoncelli and Mallat (2024) observe that two networks trained on disjoint halves of a data set generate nearly identical images, and relate this to adaptive harmonic bases.

Training without simulating SDEs is a third thread. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023), rectified flows (Liu, Gong and Liu, 2023) and stochastic interpolants (Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023) regress a velocity field directly. Consistency models (Song, Dhariwal, Chen and Sutskever, 2023) aim at one-step sampling.

  • Open: a theory of generalisation for score networks, and sharp trade-offs between the number of sampling steps and accuracy.

12.5 Causal inference with machine learning

Double/debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) made the AIPW construction of ch. 11.3 the default for using flexible regressions as nuisances. For heterogeneous effects, causal forests (Wager and Athey, 2018), the R-learner (Nie and Wager, 2021) and the DR-learner (Kennedy, 2023) estimate \tau(x) with guarantees. Policy learning with doubly robust scores is analysed by Athey and Wager (2021).

All of these rely on ignorability, which data cannot test. Sensitivity analysis quantifies how much hidden confounding would overturn a conclusion. Causal representation learning (Schölkopf et al., 2021) asks how to find causal variables when only high-dimensional observations, such as images, are available.

  • Open: inference with weak overlap, combining randomised and observational data, and identifiability results for learned causal representations.

Key takeaways — chapter 12

  • Tighter variational bounds help the decoder but can hurt the encoder’s gradient.
  • DLVMs extend to missing data, through importance-weighted bounds and models of the missingness.
  • Block models have consistency results in dense graphs and sharp detection thresholds in sparse ones.
  • Diffusion models have convergence guarantees given an accurate score; why they generalise is still open.
  • Machine-learning nuisances fit inside doubly robust estimators; untestable ignorability remains the weak point.

  10. ★★ Gibbs sampling for the Ising model. On a 16 \times 16 periodic lattice with h = 0 and couplings J_{ij} = \beta, run a Gibbs sampler and estimate the mean absolute magnetisation for \beta \in \{0.2, 0.44, 0.6\}. Explain why all sites of one colour of a checkerboard can be updated at once.

    Solution: each site’s Markov blanket is its four neighbours, all of the other colour. Given the other colour, the sites of one colour are therefore independent (Proposition 4.7), and updating them together is an exact block Gibbs step.

    import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
L = 16
ii, jj = np.indices((L, L))
colour = (ii + jj) % 2                                  # checkerboard: two conditionally independent halves

def sweep(x, beta):
    for c in (0, 1):
        nb = np.roll(x, 1, 0) + np.roll(x, -1, 0) + np.roll(x, 1, 1) + np.roll(x, -1, 1)
        p_up = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * beta * nb))         # p(x_i = +1 | neighbours), h = 0
        new = np.where(rng.random((L, L)) < p_up, 1, -1)
        x = np.where(colour == c, new, x)
    return x

for beta in [0.2, 0.44, 0.6]:
    x = rng.choice([-1, 1], (L, L))
    mags = []
    for t in range(2000):
        x = sweep(x, beta)
        if t >= 500:
            mags.append(abs(x.mean()))
    print(f"beta = {beta:4.2f}: mean |magnetisation| = {np.mean(mags):.3f}")

    The output is about 0.085, 0.73 and 0.975: disordered at high temperature, ordered at low temperature. The change happens near Onsager’s critical value \beta_c = \frac12 \log(1 + \sqrt 2) \approx 0.441.

  14. ★★ Tweedie’s formula. Let y = x + \sigma\varepsilon with \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1) independent of x, and let p_\sigma be the density of y.     Solution: p_\sigma(y) = \int \varphi_\sigma(y - x)\, p(x)\, dx, and \varphi_\sigma'(u) = -u\, \varphi_\sigma(u)/\sigma^2. Differentiating under the integral: p_\sigma'(y) = \int \frac{x - y}{\sigma^2}\, \varphi_\sigma(y - x)\, p(x)\, dx = \frac{\mathbb E[x \mid y] - y}{\sigma^2}\; p_\sigma(y). 

    import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
w, m, s, sigma = np.array([0.3, 0.7]), np.array([-2.0, 1.0]), np.array([0.5, 0.8]), 0.7
n = 400_000
k = rng.choice(2, n, p=w)
x = rng.normal(m[k], s[k])                       # clean data: two-component mixture
y = x + sigma * rng.normal(size=n)               # noisy observation

def score_noisy(y):                              # exact score of p_sigma (mixture of N(m, s^2 + sigma^2))
    v = s ** 2 + sigma ** 2
    dens = w * norm.pdf(y[:, None], m, np.sqrt(v))
    return (dens * (m - y[:, None]) / v).sum(1) / dens.sum(1)

for y0 in [-2.0, 0.0, 1.5]:
    near = np.abs(y - y0) < 0.02                 # Monte Carlo estimate of E[x | y = y0]
    tweedie = y0 + sigma ** 2 * score_noisy(np.array([y0]))[0]
    print(f"y = {y0:4.1f}: E[x|y] by Monte Carlo {x[near].mean():6.3f}, by Tweedie {tweedie:6.3f}")

    The two estimates agree to within 0.012 at the three points, for example 1.279 and 1.283 at y = 1.5.

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: importance-weighted auto-encoders. Reproduce the central comparison of Burda, Grosse and Salakhutdinov (2016) at small scale: does training on \mathcal L_K improve the test log-likelihood over a VAE?
    • Work: extend the code of ch. 8.3; train with K \in \{1, 5, 50\} on the binarised scikit-learn digits; evaluate every model with \mathcal L_{5000} on held-out images; count active latent units.
    • Extension: measure the signal-to-noise ratio of the encoder gradient as K grows (Rainforth et al., 2018).
    • Deliverables: code, 4–6 page report, 10-min talk. Chapters: 2, 6.1, 8.
    • Success: a table of test bounds with standard errors over 3 seeds, and a clear verdict.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Stochastic block models: variational EM, spectral clustering and ICL. When does variational EM improve on spectral clustering, and does ICL find the number of classes?
    • Work: implement Proposition 7.3 and the ICL criterion; test on synthetic assortative, disassortative and core–periphery SBMs; apply to Zachary’s karate club (bundled with NetworkX).
    • Deliverables: code, 4–6 page report, 10-min talk. Chapters: 4, 7 (and 3.4 for model selection).
    • Success: adjusted Rand index curves against the signal strength, and the ICL choice of the number of classes on each synthetic family.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 15 h] Hidden Markov models for regime switching. How much does modelling time help clustering?
    • Work: implement Baum–Welch with Gaussian emissions and scaled forward–backward (ch. 5.3–5.4); simulate a 3-regime series; compare Viterbi decoding with a Gaussian mixture that ignores time (ch. 2.3); choose the number of states by BIC.
    • Deliverables: code, 4-page report, 10-min talk. Chapters: 2, 3.4, 5.
    • Success: decoding accuracy above the mixture’s, and BIC selecting 3 states on most of 20 simulated series.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Paper study: score-based generative modelling at small scale. Reproduce the main mechanisms of Song et al. (2021), with Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020) as the discrete-time reference, on 2D data and the 8×8 digits of scikit-learn.
    • Split: member A trains variance-exploding and variance-preserving models by denoising score matching; member B implements the samplers (reverse SDE, probability-flow ODE, Langevin corrector from ch. 6.4); member C evaluates (likelihood through the ODE with a trace estimator, a digit classifier for sample quality).
    • Deliverables: shared code, 8–10 page report, 20-min talk. Chapters: 6.4, 9.2, 10.
    • Success: sample-quality curves against the number of steps, and a test likelihood compared with the flow of ch. 9.2.
  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Treatment effects and policies from observational data. How reliable are IPW, AIPW and policy learning when overlap weakens or models are misspecified?
    • Split: member A builds a semi-synthetic generator with known effects and controllable overlap; member B implements regression adjustment, IPW and cross-fitted AIPW with scikit-learn nuisances and confidence intervals; member C learns depth-2 tree policies from the AIPW scores (Athey and Wager, 2021).
    • Deliverables: shared code, 8–10 page report, 20-min talk. Chapters: 4.2, 11.
    • Success: empirical coverage of the 95 % intervals over 200 replications, and policy regret against the known optimum.

Written exam (proposal)

Format (proposal, not an official rule): 3 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic device, four exercises.

  1. Question (5 points). State the ELBO decomposition of \log p(x; \theta) for a latent-variable model, describe the two steps of EM, and prove that EM never decreases the likelihood. When is the bound tight?

    Expected: Proposition 2.2 with its two-line proof; the E-step sets q to the posterior and the M-step maximises the expected complete log-likelihood; the chain of inequalities of Theorem 2.3; tightness exactly when q is the posterior.

  2. Question (6 points). For the HMM of ch. 5.3 and the observations x = (0, 2, 2): compute the forward variables and p(x); compute p(z_1 \mid x); find the Viterbi path. Comment on the two answers.

    Expected: the forward variables are (0.35, 0.05), (0.0325, 0.045) and (0.003825, 0.02355), so p(x) = 0.027375. Then \beta_1 = (0.0435, 0.243) and p(z_1 \mid x) \approx (0.556, 0.444).

    The Viterbi path is (1, 1, 1), with joint probability 0.01152. The most probable path does not start with the most probable first state: marginal and joint maximisation differ.

  3. Question (4 points). Prove that the AIPW estimator of the average treatment effect is unbiased when the propensity score is correct, whatever the outcome models.

    Expected: condition on X; use ignorability to write the expectation of the treated correction as e/\hat e times \mu_1 - \hat\mu_1; with \hat e = e the outcome model cancels; the control part is symmetric; conclude with Theorem 11.2.

  4. Question (5 points). Draw the stochastic block model as a directed graphical model, moralise it, and explain why the E-step of EM is intractable. Derive the mean-field update of \tau_i.

    Expected: nodes z_i with arrows into each X_{ij}; moralisation links every pair (z_i, z_j), so the posterior couples all labels and its normaliser sums over K^n labellings. The update follows from Proposition 7.1, giving the fixed point of Proposition 7.3 with a short justification.
