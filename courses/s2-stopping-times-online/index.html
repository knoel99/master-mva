0. Review: stopping times and competitive ratios

A sequential rule needs a precise meaning of “using only the past”, and a score against an agent who sees the whole input. This chapter fixes both before any algorithm.

Rounds are discrete and run from time 0 to time n. A filtration lists the information available at the end of each round. A process is adapted when the value at that round is measurable with respect to that information.

Later chapters take the round-t sigma-field to be the one generated by the arrivals seen so far.

Definition 0.1 — Stopping time

A random variable \tau with values in \{0, 1, \ldots, n\} is a stopping time of (F_t) when \{\tau \le t\} lies in F_t for every t.

Equivalently, the event \{\tau = t\} lies in F_t. The decision to stop at time t is a function of the history up to t.

Hiring on the spot, accepting a bid, and moving a server are stopping decisions in this sense. The rule may use the current arrival and nothing later. A constant time is a stopping time, and so is the first entrance to a set, once it is capped at a fixed horizon.

Theorem 0.1 — Optional stopping on a bounded clock

Let (X_t)_{t=0}^{N} be a martingale adapted to (F_t), and let \tau be a stopping time with values in \{0, \ldots, N\}.

Then the stopped variable has the same expectation as the value at time 0. E[X_\tau] = E[X_0].

Proof. Decompose the stopped value into the start plus the increments that occur before stopping.

X_\tau = X_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{N} 1_{\{\tau \ge t\}} (X_t - X_{t-1}).

The event \{\tau \ge t\} is the complement of \{\tau \le t-1\}, so it lies in F_{t-1}.

On that event the increment is still a martingale increment. Its conditional expectation given F_{t-1} is zero, whether or not the indicator is on.

Taking expectations, every term of the sum vanishes.

E[X_\tau] = E[X_0].

The horizon N keeps the sum finite, so no extra domination argument is required. ∎

The horizon hypothesis is not decorative. Let S_t be simple symmetric random walk and let \sigma be the first time S_t = 1. Then \sigma is finite with probability 1, but it is not a bounded stopping time. The stopped value is 1 almost surely, so its expectation is 1, while E[S_0] = 0. Theorem 0.1 does not apply, and the conclusion is false.

Definition 0.2 — Competitive ratio

On a maximisation problem, an algorithm is c-competitive when its expected value is at least c times the expected offline optimum, for a constant c in (0, 1], on every instance of the model.

On a minimisation problem, the algorithm is c-competitive when its cost on every sequence is at most c times the offline cost, plus an additive constant that does not grow with the sequence. Here c \ge 1.

The two inequalities point in opposite directions. Prophet inequalities and matching use the maximisation form, and a larger c is better. Paging and k-server use the minimisation form, and a smaller c is better. These notes always say which one is in force.

The ratio of expectations is not the expectation of the ratio. A rule can be excellent on a typical input and poor on a rare input, and still have a good ratio of expectations. The prophet benchmark in ch. 2 is a ratio of expectations.

Example 0.1 — Eight paths of a fair walk

Start at 0. Each step is +1 or -1 with equal probability. Stop at the first time the position equals 1, and stop by time 3 in any case.

There are eight paths of length 3, each of probability 1/8. The four paths that begin with +1 stop at time 1, on the value 1.

The other four paths stop at time 3. Their positions are 1, -1, -1 and -3.

The average of the eight stopped values is 0, in agreement with Theorem 0.1.

\frac{1}{8}(1+1+1+1+1-1-1-3) = 0.

The identity to keep

On a bounded clock, optional stopping is the equality E[X_\tau] = E[X_0].

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A stopping time decides to halt at time t using only F_t.
  • A martingale stopped on a bounded clock keeps its initial expectation.
  • Without a bound on the clock, the equality can fail. The first hit of level 1 is the counter-example.
  • Maximisation ratios are at most 1 and should be large. Minimisation ratios are at least 1 and should be small.

1. The secretary problem

An employer interviews n candidates in uniformly random order and must hire on the spot. Only relative ranks are observed. The goal is to hire the single best candidate.

This is the classical secretary problem. Lindley (1961) gave a dynamic-programming analysis, and Ferguson (1989) surveys the history. The input is a random permutation. The algorithm sees ranks one by one and may not recall a rejected candidate.

Definition 1.1 — Record rule

Fix an integer r with 0 \le r < n. Reject the first r candidates automatically. Then hire the first later candidate who is better than all candidates seen so far.

If no such candidate appears, the rule hires nobody. Success means that the hired candidate is the best of all n.

The first r interviews are a sample. They are never hired. Their only role is to set a bar. After that, the rule takes the first left-to-right maximum. The bar is a stopping time of the filtration of relative ranks.

Theorem 1.1 — Success probability

Under the record rule with rejection length r \ge 1, the probability of hiring the best candidate is

\frac{r}{n} \sum_{j=r}^{n-1} \frac{1}{j}.

If r/n tends to 1/e with r \ge 2, this probability tends to 1/e.

Proof. The best candidate is equally likely to stand in any position k. If k \le r, the rule has already rejected the best candidate, and it fails.

If k > r, the rule selects position k exactly when the best of the first k-1 candidates lies among the first r. That event has probability r/(k-1), because the best of those k-1 is uniform among them.

Summing over the position of the overall best candidate gives

\sum_{k=r+1}^{n} \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{r}{k-1} = \frac{r}{n} \sum_{j=r}^{n-1} \frac{1}{j}.

For the limit, compare the sum with integrals, for r \ge 2.

\log\frac{n}{r} < \sum_{j=r}^{n-1} \frac{1}{j} < \log\frac{n-1}{r-1}.

Multiply through by r/n. Along any sequence with r/n \to 1/e, both the lower bound and the upper bound tend to (1/e) \cdot 1. ∎

The integral comparison is the only analytic step. The finite-n formula is only counting. For r = 1 the integral bound above is not stated, and the exact formula still applies. It equals H_{n-1}/n, which tends to 0. Skipping a single candidate is not the asymptotic optimum.

Example 1.1 — Three candidates, skip the first

Take n = 3 and r = 1. The formula gives

\frac{1}{3}\left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}.

The six permutations of the ranks, with 3 best, confirm the count. The rule succeeds on 132, 213 and 231, and fails on 123, 312 and 321.

\frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}.

For large n the rejection length \lfloor n/e \rfloor is the usual choice. Exercise 2 simulates n = 100, where that length equals 36, and the hit rate sits next to 1/e \approx 0.3679.

The constant to keep

The record rule with r \sim n/e hires the best candidate with probability tending to 1/e.

The model is rigid. Values are never seen, only comparisons, the order is uniform, and exactly one candidate is hired. ch. 2 keeps the irrevocable choice and changes the information. Distributions are known, values are numeric, and the benchmark becomes an expectation of the maximum rather than the probability of selecting the maximum.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • The record rule rejects a prefix and then takes the first candidate who beats that prefix.
  • The success probability is (r/n) times a harmonic sum.
  • The harmonic sum is a logarithm, so r \sim n/e yields the limit 1/e.
  • Relative ranks are not numeric values. Prophets, in the next chapter, see numeric values.

2. Prophet inequalities

Independent nonnegative random variables are shown one by one. A stopping rule may take the current variable and then must stop. A prophet, who sees every variable, takes the maximum. The question is how close a causal rule can come, in expectation.

Krengel and Sucheston (1977) formulated the comparison. Samuel-Cahn (1984) showed that a single threshold already achieves the factor 1/2. The course description writes the score as a ratio of the stopped value to the maximum. These notes use the ratio of expectations, which is the standard form of the inequality.

Definition 2.1 — Single-threshold rule

Fix t \ge 0. Let \tau be the smallest index i such that X_i \ge t. If no such index exists, the reward is 0.

The variables X_1, \ldots, X_n are independent and nonnegative. Their distributions may differ. The threshold may depend on those distributions. It may not depend on future values.

Let M be the maximum of the variables. Write q_i for the probability that the first i variables all lie strictly below the threshold, and set q_0 = 1.

The sequence q_i decreases, and q_n equals P(M < t).

Theorem 2.1 — A median threshold gives one half

Suppose t satisfies P(M < t) = 1/2. Let the reward R be X_\tau if the threshold rule stops, and 0 otherwise.

Then the expected reward is at least half the prophet’s expectation. E[R] \ge \frac{1}{2} E[M].

Proof. Independence factors the history from the current variable.

E[R] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_{i-1}\, E[X_i 1_{\{X_i \ge t\}}].

Split the summand into the excess over t and a piece of size t.

X_i 1_{\{X_i \ge t\}} = (X_i - t)_+ + t\, 1_{\{X_i \ge t\}}.

The probabilities q_{i-1} P(X_i \ge t) telescope. Their sum equals 1 - q_n = 1/2. This contributes t/2 to E[R].

Since q_{i-1} \ge q_n = 1/2, the excess part contributes at least half the sum of E[(X_i - t)_+].

For the prophet, M \le t + (M - t)_+, and the positive part of the maximum is at most the sum of the positive parts. Therefore

E[M] \le t + \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[(X_i - t)_+].

Combining the two lower bounds yields E[R] \ge E[M]/2. ∎

The hypothesis P(M < t) = 1/2 is natural when M is continuous. A distribution with atoms need not hit the level 1/2 exactly. A randomised tie break can restore it. These notes prove the continuous case and do not spell out the coin flip.

Taking X_n when every variable falls below t can only raise a nonnegative reward. The same factor 1/2 holds for that fallback rule.

Example 2.1 — A two-point gap

Let X_1 = 1 and let X_2 equal 1/\varepsilon with probability \varepsilon, and equal 0 otherwise. Then E[X_2] = 1 and

E[M] = 2 - \varepsilon.

Stopping at X_1 yields 1. Continuing yields E[X_2] = 1. Every mixture of those two actions also yields 1.

The ratio of expectations is 1/(2-\varepsilon). For \varepsilon = 1/100 this equals 100/199, which is about 0.503. No universal constant strictly larger than 1/2 works for every instance.

For identical distributions the factor 1/2 is not sharp. The optimal constant is approximately 0.745. Correa, Foncea, Hoeksma, Oosterwijk and Vredeveld (Mathematics of Operations Research, 2021) attain it. The worst-case factor over all independent distributions remains 1/2, by Example 2.1.

Kleinberg and Weinberg (STOC 2012) extend the factor 1/2 from a single choice to a matroid constraint. The one-choice theorem is the case used below for a posted price.

The factor to keep

A median threshold satisfies E[R] \ge E[M]/2 for independent nonnegative variables.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The prophet sees every variable. The stopping rule sees them one by one.
  • A threshold with P(M < t) = 1/2 keeps at least half the expected maximum.
  • The proof splits the reward into a piece of size t and an excess, then uses q_i \ge 1/2.
  • A two-point instance shows that 1/2 cannot be replaced by a larger universal constant.

3. Online bipartite matching

One side of a bipartite graph is present from the beginning. Call it the offline side. Vertices on the online side arrive one by one, together with their edges. On arrival, the algorithm may match the new vertex to a free neighbour, or leave it unmatched. The decision is permanent.

The score is the number of matched edges divided by the size of a maximum matching. This is the maximisation form of Definition 0.2. Karp, Vazirani and Vazirani (STOC 1990) introduced the model in this online form.

Definition 3.1 — Greedy matching

When an online vertex arrives, if it has a free neighbour, match it to an arbitrary free neighbour. Otherwise leave it unmatched.

The choice among free neighbours may be adversarial. The arrival order may be adversarial. The whole graph is fixed before the arrivals, but the algorithm sees each online vertex only when it arrives.

Proposition 3.1 — Greedy is one half

On every bipartite graph and every arrival order, the greedy matching M and a maximum matching M^\star satisfy |M^\star| \le 2|M|.

The competitive ratio is therefore at least 1/2.

Proof. Take any optimal edge with online end u and offline end v. Suppose neither end lies in the greedy matching.

When u arrived, v was still free, and u had v as a neighbour. Greedy would have matched u. So at least one end of the optimal edge lies in the greedy matching.

If u was matched, charge the optimal edge to u’s greedy edge. Otherwise charge it to the greedy edge that took v.

Each greedy edge receives at most two charges, one at each end. Therefore the optimum has size at most twice the greedy matching. ∎

The factor 1/2 is tight for greedy. On the two-vertex instance in the introduction, greedy keeps one edge and the optimum keeps two.

Theorem 3.2 — RANKING

Draw a uniform random permutation of the offline vertices, once, at the beginning. When an online vertex arrives, match it to the free neighbour of highest rank, if any.

This algorithm is (1 - 1/e)-competitive. No randomised algorithm has a better worst-case ratio.

The theorem is due to Karp, Vazirani and Vazirani (1990). A shorter analysis, through a randomised primal-dual argument, is due to Devanur, Jain and Kleinberg (SODA 2013). These notes do not reproduce that argument. ch. 6 writes the linear program that the argument uses, and proves again the factor 1/2 in dual language.

An equivalent description helps simulation. Give each offline vertex an independent Uniform(0,1) priority and match the arrival to the free neighbour of smallest priority. The induced order is a uniform permutation.

Example 3.1 — Two offline vertices

Offline vertices are a and b. Online vertices are u, joined to both, and v, joined only to a. The optimum has size 2.

Greedy matches u to a and then loses v, for size 1.

RANKING matches u to the higher-ranked neighbour. With probability 1/2, b outranks a, so u takes b and v takes a. The expected size is

\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 + \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = \frac{3}{2}.

The ratio on this single graph is 3/4, already above 1/2. The worst-case guarantee 1-1/e is about 0.632, and it has to hold for every graph.

The two ratios

Greedy guarantees 1/2. RANKING guarantees 1 - 1/e.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Online vertices arrive with their edges and must be matched immediately or not at all.
  • Greedy is 1/2-competitive by a charging map that hits each greedy edge at most twice.
  • A two-edge instance shows that 1/2 is tight for arbitrary tie breaks.
  • RANKING uses one random permutation and reaches 1-1/e. The proof is cited, not reproduced.

4. Budgets, AdWords and b-matching

Matching with unit capacities is the skeleton. Advertisers, however, have budgets, and a vertex on the offline side may accept several arrivals. The course description names b-matching, AdWords and delayed matching as the variants, with campaign budgets as the motivation.

Definition 4.1 — Three budgeted models

In unweighted b-matching, offline vertex v may be used up to b_v times. Each use covers one online vertex.

In the AdWords problem, offline vertex v has a budget B_v. An arrival is a query. Bidder v offers a bid on that query. Assigning the query to v consumes the bid from the budget and collects that bid as revenue, provided the remaining budget covers it.

In delayed matching, an online vertex may wait. The algorithm pays a waiting cost until the vertex is matched or discarded.

Proposition 4.1 — Greedy b-matching

Greedy b-matching gives an online vertex any neighbour with remaining capacity. On every instance the number of assignments is at least half the maximum.

The charging of Proposition 3.1 applies to units of capacity. Each greedy assignment is charged at most twice.

Proof. Let an optimal assignment send an online vertex u to an offline vertex v. If u is unmatched and v still had a free slot when u arrived, greedy would have used that slot.

So each optimal assignment meets either the online vertex’s greedy assignment or one slot of v that greedy filled. Each greedy slot is met at most twice in this accounting. ∎

AdWords needs a different rule once bids are small and budgets are large. Spending the entire budget of one bidder on early cheap queries can block a later query that only that bidder could have paid for. The balance idea is to prefer a bidder who has spent a smaller fraction of budget.

Theorem 4.2 — The MSVV weight

Let x_v be the fraction of budget B_v already spent. On a query, award it to the eligible bidder maximising the product of the bid and \psi(x_v), where

\psi(x) = 1 - e^{x-1}, \qquad x \in [0, 1].

When every bid is negligible beside its budget, the competitive ratio of this rule approaches 1 - 1/e.

The theorem is due to Mehta, Saberi, Vazirani and Vazirani (Journal of the ACM, 2007). The small-bid hypothesis matters. When each bid is comparable to the budget, the guarantee can drop, and the b-matching factor 1/2 is the relevant warning. These notes do not prove the 1-1/e limit. They record the weight and compute it.

The function \psi is strictly decreasing on [0, 1], because its derivative is -e^{x-1} < 0. At equal bids, the bidder with the smaller spent fraction wins. That is the balance effect. At the right endpoint, \psi(1) = 0, so an exhausted bidder is never chosen.

Example 4.1 — Two spent fractions

Both bidders offer bid 1. One has spent nothing. The other has spent half of a still-positive budget.

\psi(0) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632, \qquad \psi(1/2) = 1 - e^{-1/2} \approx 0.393.

The untouched bidder wins the query. Exercise 7 repeats the comparison on three queries with budgets equal to 2.

Delayed matching, as named in the course description, adds a clock. An unmatched online vertex may stay in a queue, and the cost grows with the wait. These notes define the model and do not claim a competitive ratio for it. Ride-sharing and kidney exchange are the usual applied pictures. The ratio depends on how the waiting cost is charged, which is left to ch. 8.

Vertex weights, unlike budgets, keep the ratio 1-1/e under a ranking rule (Aggarwal, Goel, Karande and Mehta, SODA 2011). Weights sit on vertices. Budgets are spent down by bids.

The weight to keep

At equal bids, \psi(x) = 1 - e^{x-1} prefers the bidder who has spent less of the budget.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • b-matching allows b_v uses of an offline vertex, and greedy remains 1/2-competitive.
  • AdWords spends budgets. The MSVV weight is the bid times 1 - e^{x-1}.
  • The ratio approaching 1-1/e needs bids that are small relative to budgets.
  • Delayed matching allows a wait. No ratio is claimed in these notes.

5. Paging, the k-server problem and metrical tasks

A request arrives in a metric. The algorithm moves until the request is served, and it pays the distance. The offline optimum sees the whole sequence. Costs use the minimisation form from ch. 0.

Definition 5.1 — The k-server problem

A metric space carries k servers. On a request, the algorithm moves servers and must cover the request. Every distance travelled is paid.

The benchmark is the cheapest schedule for the same requests.

Paging is the uniform metric. Distinct pages lie at distance 1, so a fault costs 1 and a hit costs 0. The cache holds k pages. Sleator and Tarjan (1985) proved that the deterministic ratio is exactly k, and that LRU attains it.

Definition 5.2 — LRU and phases

LRU evicts the least recently used page on a fault.

A phase is a longest contiguous block with at most k distinct pages. The next page outside that block opens a new phase.

Proposition 5.1 — A phase bound of 2k

LRU faults at most 2k times as often as an optimal cache, plus an additive k for a possible unfinished phase.

Sleator and Tarjan replace 2k by k. The windows below are disjoint, which is why this write-up stops at 2k.

Proof. A full phase contains k distinct pages. After LRU has fetched one of them, at most k-1 further pages of the phase can arrive, so the fetched page stays until the phase ends. Each page faults at most once, and the phase costs at most k.

Pair the full phases. The first phase of a pair contributes k pages, and the opening request of the next phase is a new page. The window holds at least k+1 distinct pages, so every cache of size k faults at least once.

Different pairs use disjoint windows. A pair costs LRU at most 2k and costs the optimum at least 1. ∎

Theorem 5.2 — The k-server conjecture

Manasse, McGeoch and Sleator (1990) conjectured a deterministic k-competitive algorithm on every metric.

The work-function algorithm is (2k-1)-competitive on every metric (Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou, 1995). On trees, Chrobak and Larmore (1991) achieve the ratio k. Paging meets k as well. A general metric remains open.

The work function of a configuration is the minimum cost of serving the requests so far and ending there. The algorithm moves toward a minimiser of that cost plus the travel from the current servers. The factor 2k-1 is cited, not proved here.

A metrical task system charges the move and then a cost that depends on the chosen state. Configurations of k servers are one choice of state space.

Convex body chasing, introduced by Friedman and Linial (1993), moves a point into each new convex body and pays the distance. Online transportation moves supply to each new demand and pays the ground distance. Ratios that depend only on the dimension are discussed in ch. 8.

Example 5.1 — Six requests

The requests are A, B, C, A, B, C, and the cache has size 2. LRU faults on all six. An optimal cache faults four times.

\frac{6}{4} = \frac{3}{2}.

The phase bound on this cache is 4, and the sharp paging ratio is 2. A short list does not force either number.

The ratio to remember

Paging has deterministic ratio k. The conjecture asks for that ratio on every metric. The work-function algorithm reaches 2k-1.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • k-server pays the distance travelled to meet each request.
  • Paging is the uniform metric, and LRU is k-competitive.
  • Disjoint phase pairs give the factor 2k proved in these notes.
  • On a general metric the cited deterministic bound is 2k-1.

6. Primal-dual updates, stochastic approximation and trees

The course description names linear optimisation, stochastic approximation and tree reductions. The matching bound of ch. 3 supplies the linear program. A budget price supplies the stochastic step. An embedding supplies the tree step.

Definition 6.1 — Fractional matching

Maximise the sum of the edge variables. At each vertex the incident variables sum to at most 1, and every variable is nonnegative.

The dual minimises the sum of vertex weights. The two ends of every edge must sum to at least 1.

Weak duality says that a feasible dual is at least the integral optimum. A matching together with a feasible dual of twice its size proves the factor 1/2.

Proposition 6.1 — Dual certificate for greedy

When greedy matches an online vertex to an offline vertex, set both dual weights to 1. Unmatched vertices keep weight 0.

Those weights are feasible, and the dual value is twice the number of greedy edges.

Proof. If an edge had both weights equal to 0, neither end was matched. The offline end was free when the online vertex arrived, so greedy would have matched it. Every edge is covered.

Each greedy edge lifts two weights from 0 to 1. Weak duality then yields the factor 1/2. ∎

Buchbinder and Naor (2009) survey the method. The analysis of RANKING raises weights continuously and replaces 1/2 by 1-1/e.

Definition 6.2 — Pacing update

A campaign may spend a budget B across T arrivals. Let g be the spend on the current arrival and let \lambda be the price.

\lambda \leftarrow \max(0,\; \lambda + \eta (g - B/T)).

The step \eta is positive. The price rises when spend exceeds the pace B/T, and it is cut off at 0.

Robbins and Monro (1951) find a root of an unknown mean from noisy samples. The arrival is the noise. The root is a price at which expected spend equals the pace. No competitive ratio is claimed for this recursion. It is the budget technique named in the course description.

Theorem 6.2 — Dominating trees

Fakcharoenphol, Rao and Talwar (2004) show that every metric on n points is within an expected factor O(\log n) of a random dominating tree.

Tree distance is at least the original distance on every pair. Its expectation is at most a logarithmic multiple of that distance.

Proof. The construction of the random tree is in the cited paper and is not reproduced here.

A c-competitive algorithm for a distance-sum problem on trees gives an expected ratio O(c \log n) on the original metric when the tree algorithm can be run on the embedded instance. The k-server bounds do not follow from that sentence. The randomised results use the algorithm of Bubeck, Cohen, Lee, Lee and Mądry, recorded in ch. 8. ∎

Example 6.1 — The tight graph

On the two-edge greedy example, set the dual weights of the matched online vertex and of its offline partner equal to 1. The dual value is 2, which equals the optimum, so the certificate is tight.

The certificate

A feasible dual worth twice the matching proves the factor 1/2.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Greedy matching is certified by a dual whose value is twice the number of edges.
  • A pacing price increases when spend exceeds the pace B/T.
  • A random dominating tree stretches distances by an expected factor O(\log n).
  • That embedding does not by itself prove a k-server ratio.

7. Pandora’s box, posted prices and bandits

Opening a box has a cost. A seller may post a price instead of running a full auction. Both decisions are stopping times. A bandit instead repeats an action. The course description keeps those questions apart.

Definition 7.1 — Reservation of a box

A box hides a prize X with a known law and costs c to open. The reservation \sigma is defined by

E[(X - \sigma)_+] = c.

A decision maker who already holds the value \sigma is indifferent between opening and walking away.

Theorem 7.1 — One box, then many

Suppose the reward in hand is r, and one box remains. Opening raises the expected net reward when r is below the reservation, and lowers it when r is above.

For independent boxes, Weitzman (Econometrica, 1979) orders them by decreasing reservation and opens the next box while the best prize in hand lies below that reservation.

Proof. Opening pays the cost and replaces the reward in hand by the larger of the prize and that reward. The expected gain equals the expected excess of the prize over the reward in hand, minus the cost.

The expected excess falls as the reward in hand rises, and it equals the cost at the reservation. The gain is positive below that level and negative above it.

The ordering rule for several boxes is Weitzman’s theorem. It is cited, not reproved. ∎

The order depends only on the laws and the costs. Once the order is fixed, stopping is a threshold on the best prize seen so far, so it is a stopping time.

Example 7.1 — Two boxes

Box A costs 1 and pays 0 or 8 with equal probability. The reservation equation gives 6. Box B costs 1/2 and pays 0 or 4 with equal probability. Its reservation is 3.

Start from 0, which lies below 6, so the rule opens A. If the prize is 8, it stops, and the net is 7. If the prize is 0, it opens B. A prize of 4 in B nets 5/2 after both costs. A prize of 0 in B nets -3/2.

\frac{1}{2}\cdot 7 + \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{5}{2} + \frac{1}{4}\cdot\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) = \frac{15}{4}.

A posted price is the threshold of Theorem 2.1. Buyers with independent values arrive, and the first value that meets the price takes the item. Expected welfare is at least half the expected maximum.

The offer is truthful, because the price does not depend on the buyer’s report (Hajiaghayi, Kleinberg and Sandholm, AAAI 2007). These notes treat a single item. The factor 1/2 bounds welfare, not the seller’s revenue. Feldman, Gravin and Lucier (SODA 2015) treat richer valuations. That extension is not proved here.

Bandits are a different benchmark

A bandit picks an arm and sees only that arm. The benchmark is the best fixed arm, and the score is regret.

A stopping rule picks a time. The benchmark is an offline optimum, and the score is a constant ratio. Regret does not charge a decision to quit. The arguments of ch. 1 through ch. 6 are competitive-analysis arguments.

The index

Open a box when the reward in hand lies below the reservation.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • The reservation equates the expected excess of the prize with the opening cost.
  • One box is opened when the reward in hand is below that level.
  • Weitzman orders independent boxes by reservation and then applies a threshold.
  • A posted price inherits the prophet factor 1/2 for welfare. Regret is a different score.

8. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is self-study, outside the 24 h of lectures. It lists questions that stay open, or that were settled recently enough that the proofs sit in research papers. The voice is that of a reader of those papers. Nothing here is claimed as original work.

The deterministic k-server conjecture of Manasse, McGeoch and Sleator (1990) asks for a k-competitive algorithm on every metric. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou (1995) reach 2k-1 with the work function. Chrobak and Larmore (1991) settle the tree case at the ratio k. The gap between k and 2k-1 on a general metric is still open.

Randomised algorithms do better. Bubeck, Cohen, J. R. Lee, Y. T. Lee and Mądry (STOC 2018) obtain O((\log k)^2) on hierarchically separated trees and O((\log k)^2 \log n) on n-point metrics. Whether a randomised ratio of O(\log k), independent of the number of points, holds on every metric is open. The paper’s dynamic embedding gives a ratio that still depends on the aspect ratio of the metric.

Prophet inequalities with little distributional information are only partly understood. Azar, Kleinberg and Weinberg (SODA 2014) show that a single sample from each distribution already supports a constant-factor guarantee. The best constant under one sample, and the right statement when the variables are correlated, are not settled by the one-choice proof of ch. 2.

For identical distributions the optimal factor is approximately 0.745. Correa, Foncea, Hoeksma, Oosterwijk and Vredeveld (Mathematics of Operations Research, 2021) attain it with adaptive thresholds. Their argument is not the median rule of Theorem 2.1. Extending that constant to matroid constraints, where Kleinberg and Weinberg (2012) have 1/2, is open in general.

Posted prices beyond a single item are in Feldman, Gravin and Lucier (SODA 2015) for XOS valuations, again at the factor 1/2. Welfare guarantees for valuations outside that class, under the same arrival model, remain a research topic. Revenue, as opposed to welfare, is a different objective and is not settled by Theorem 2.1.

Delayed matching, named in the course description, charges for making an online vertex wait. The right competitive ratio depends on the waiting cost and on whether the matching has to be perfect. These notes do not select a theorem. A careful reading starts from the cost definition rather than from the unit-capacity proof of ch. 3.

Convex body chasing asks for a ratio that depends on the dimension and not on the length of the path. Friedman and Linial (1993) posed the problem. The nested special case and the general case have both seen strong progress since then. Any account should separate the dimension dependence from the path length, and should not borrow the paging ratio k.

Tree embeddings remain a tool rather than a closed theory for servers. Theorem 6.2 gives stretch O(\log n). Using it as a black box does not prove the randomised k-server bounds of Bubeck and coauthors. A reader who wants the missing step should start from their multiscale entropy, not from a generic dominating tree.

The separation from bandits is conceptual and also technical. Regret bounds quantify a gap to the best fixed arm. Competitive ratios quantify a gap to an offline sequence. Producing one inequality that specialises to both, under hypotheses a student can check, is not done in these notes and is a real research question rather than a translation exercise.

Exercises

Levels follow the session plan. One star applies a formula from a single chapter. Two stars combine two ideas or write a short proof. Three stars ask for a judgement that uses several chapters. Exercise 2 and Exercise 6 are the computational ones.

  1. ★ Optional stopping on the eight paths. In Example 0.1 the fair walk stops at level 1 or at time 3. List the eight paths, mark the stopping time on each, and compute the average of the stopped values.

    Solution: the paths that start with +1 stop at time 1 on the value 1. There are four of them. The paths that start with -1 stop at time 3, on the values 1, -1, -1 and -3. The sum of the eight values is 0, so the average is 0.

  2. ★ A Monte Carlo check of the secretary limit. The block below runs the record rule with n = 100 and rejection length \lfloor n/e \rfloor, for 4000 independent permutations. The best candidate is the value n-1.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
n = 100
r = int(numpy.floor(n / numpy.e))
trials = 4000
wins = 0
for _ in range(trials):
    perm = rng.permutation(n)
    best_so_far = perm[:r].max()
    chosen = None
    for value in perm[r:]:
        if value > best_so_far:
            chosen = value
            break
    if chosen == n - 1:
        wins += 1
rate = wins / trials
print(r)
print(f"{rate:.4f}")
*Solution*: the block prints 36 and then 0.3723. The rejection length is $\lfloor 100/e \rfloor = 36$. The hit rate 0.3723 sits next to $1/e \approx 0.3679$. Four thousand trials do not reproduce the limit digit by digit. They show that the finite-$n$ rule is already close.

  1. ★ The two-point prophet ratio. Let X_1 = 1 and let X_2 equal 100 with probability 1/100, and equal 0 otherwise. Compute E[M], the expectation of every online policy, and the ratio of the two.

    Solution: E[X_2] = 1 and E[M] = 2 - 1/100 = 199/100. Stopping at once yields 1. Continuing yields 1. Every mixture yields 1. The ratio is 100/199.

  2. ★ LRU against an optimal cache. Cache size is 2. The request sequence is A, B, C, A, B, C, and the cache starts empty. List the LRU cache after each request, count its faults, and give one offline schedule with 4 faults.

    Solution: the LRU cache after each request is A, then A and B, then B and C, then C and A, then A and B, then B and C. Every request faults, so there are 6 faults.

    One optimal schedule faults on the first A, on B, on C and on the later B. It keeps C through the last request and hits the fourth request and the final C. That is 4 faults.

  3. ★★ The greedy charging map. Prove Proposition 3.1. State the map from an optimal edge to a greedy edge, and explain why each greedy edge has at most two preimages.

    Solution: if an optimal edge had both ends free of the greedy matching, the offline end was free when the online end arrived, and greedy would have matched it. So at least one end is matched by greedy.

    Send the optimal edge to the online vertex’s greedy edge when that vertex was matched, and otherwise to the greedy edge that occupied the offline vertex. Each greedy edge receives at most one charge at each end.

  4. ★★ A median threshold on eight uniform draws. Each trial draws eight independent numbers, uniform on the unit interval. The threshold is the number t with t^8 = 1/2, so the maximum lies below t with probability 1/2. The rule takes the first draw that meets the threshold, and otherwise takes the last draw. Five thousand trials use the seed below.

import numpy

rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
n = 8
trials = 5000
t = 0.5 ** (1.0 / n)
alg = numpy.empty(trials)
prophet = numpy.empty(trials)
for i in range(trials):
    x = rng.random(n)
    prophet[i] = x.max()
    above = numpy.flatnonzero(x >= t)
    alg[i] = x[above[0]] if above.size else x[-1]
ratio = alg.mean() / prophet.mean()
print(f"{t:.4f}")
print(f"{alg.mean():.4f}")
print(f"{prophet.mean():.4f}")
print(f"{ratio:.4f}")
*Solution*: the block prints 0.9170, then 0.7029, then 0.8893, then 0.7903. The simulated ratio 0.7903 is above $1/2$. Identical uniforms are not a worst-case independent family. The factor $1/2$ in Theorem 2.1 is a guarantee for every independent family, not a prediction for this one.

  1. ★★ Who wins under the MSVV weight. Budgets are B_A = B_B = 2. Three queries arrive, each with bid 1 from both bidders. Ties go to A. Using \psi(x) = 1 - e^{x-1}, decide the winner of each query and the revenue.

    Start from \psi(0) > \psi(1/2), which holds because \psi is strictly decreasing. You may use \psi(0) \approx 0.632 and \psi(1/2) \approx 0.393.

    Solution: query 1 is a tie at spent fraction 0, so A wins and has spent 1. Query 2 gives B the higher weight, the value of \psi at 0 against the value at 1/2, so B wins.

    Query 3 is a tie at spent fraction 1/2, so A wins. Revenue equals 3. Both budgets still had room for a unit bid at each award.

  2. ★★ The harmonic formula. Prove the finite-n identity in Theorem 1.1. Do not reprove the limit. Assume the best candidate is equally likely to stand in any position, and that the relative order of any prefix is uniform.

    Solution: if the best candidate stands at position k with k \le r, the rule has already rejected that candidate.

    If k > r, the rule succeeds with probability r/(k-1). That is the chance that the best of the first k-1 candidates lies in the sample. Average over those positions and reindex the sum.

  3. ★★★ Reservations for the two boxes of Example 7.1. Derive the reservations 6 and 3 from the defining equation, recompute the expected net reward 15/4, and then change the outside option from 0 to 7/2. Say whether box A is still opened, and whether box B is opened after a zero prize from A.

    Hints and expected result: the new outside option 7/2 is still below the reservation 6 of box A, so A is opened. It is above the reservation 3 of box B, so a zero prize in A does not open B.

    The held reward stays 7/2 when A pays 0. Opening A and then stopping has expected net reward 19/4, after the cost 1. The rule compares 7/2 with 6.

  4. ★★★ What paging does not settle. State the k-server conjecture, the (2k-1) bound of the work-function algorithm, and the ratio k for paging. Explain, in a short paragraph, why a k-competitive paging algorithm does not prove the conjecture on every metric.

    Solution: the conjecture asks for a deterministic algorithm whose ratio is k on every metric. The work-function algorithm reaches 2k-1 on every metric (Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou, 1995).

    Paging is the uniform metric, and there Sleator and Tarjan attain the ratio k. A proof that uses equal distances does not apply to a line or to a tree. Chrobak and Larmore settle trees by a different algorithm. General metrics stay open.

  1. Conceptual question (6 points). The course description says that online algorithms are related to bandits, and that the question here is when to decide rather than which decision to repeat. In a few sentences, describe the benchmark of a competitive ratio and the benchmark of regret. Name one problem from ch. 1 to ch. 5 that is not a bandit problem.

    Expected: the competitive ratio compares an online rule with an offline optimum that sees the whole sequence. Regret compares a bandit policy with the best fixed arm. Secretary, prophet, matching, AdWords and k-server use the offline benchmark. Any one of those names is enough.

  2. Computation (7 points). Four candidates arrive in random order. The record rule rejects the first and then takes the first later record. Compute the probability that it hires the best candidate. Give a fraction in lowest terms.

  3. Short proof (7 points). Let X_1, \ldots, X_n be random variables and let M be their maximum. Prove that for every real t,

    E[M] \le t + \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[(X_i - t)_+].

    Independence is not required. Say where the argument would fail if the sum were replaced by a single term E[(M - t)_+] inside a later step that needs a sum of separate excesses. You do not need to rebuild the whole prophet proof.

    Expected: M \le t + (M - t)_+ holds pathwise, and (M - t)_+ \le \sum_i (X_i - t)_+ because the excess of the maximum is one of the excesses. Take expectations. The separate sum is what lets the threshold proof factor each variable. Replacing it by E[(M-t)_+] is true and tighter, but it does not by itself produce one term per index i.
