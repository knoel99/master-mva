0. Review: maxima, conditioning, linear programming and matchings

Every proof of the course compares an online algorithm with an omniscient optimum. This chapter gathers the M1-level tools of those comparisons: the expectation of a maximum, the independence of the future, weak LP duality, matchings and metrics.

0.1 Expectations of maxima

Definition 0.1 — Positive part and tail formula For a real number a, write a^+ = \max(a, 0). For a non-negative random variable X and a level T, \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^\infty \mathbb{P}(X > t)\, dt, \qquad \mathbb{E}[(X - T)^+] = \int_T^\infty \mathbb{P}(X > t)\, dt .

Proposition 0.2 — A bound on the expected maximum For any random variables X_1, \dots, X_n (independent or not) and any level T,

Key inequality — the maximum is at most a threshold plus the excesses \mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{i} X_i\Big] \;\leq\; T + \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\big[(X_i - T)^+\big].

Proof. Pointwise, \max_i X_i \leq T + \max_i (X_i - T)^+, and a maximum of non-negative numbers is at most their sum. Take expectations. ∎

Example 0.3 — Maxima of uniform and exponential samples For n independent uniform variables on [0, 1], the maximum has distribution function t^n, so \mathbb{E}[\max_i U_i] = \int_0^1 (1 - t^n)\, dt = \frac{n}{n+1} . For n independent exponential variables of mean 1, the gaps between consecutive order statistics are independent exponentials of means 1/n, 1/(n-1), \dots, 1, so the expected maximum is the harmonic number H_n \approx \ln n.

0.2 Independence of the future

An online algorithm decides at step i with what it saw before. With independent values, “still active at step i” says nothing about X_i.

Proposition 0.4 — Independence of the future Let X_1, \dots, X_n be independent and let A be an event determined by X_1, \dots, X_{i-1} and by an independent randomization. Then, for every integrable function g, \mathbb{E}[g(X_i)\, \mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[g(X_i)]\; \mathbb{P}(A) . This is the definition of independence applied to g(X_i) and \mathbf{1}_A; it is the single probabilistic step of the prophet inequalities of ch. 3.

0.3 Linear programming and weak duality

Definition 0.5 — Primal and dual programs For a matrix A and vectors b, c, the primal and dual programs are (P)\ \max \{ c^\top x : A x \leq b,\ x \geq 0 \}, \qquad (D)\ \min \{ b^\top y : A^\top y \geq c,\ y \geq 0 \}.

Theorem 0.6 — Weak and strong duality If x is feasible for (P) and y is feasible for (D), then c^\top x \leq b^\top y . If (P) has a finite optimum, so does (D), and the two optimal values are equal.

Proof. Weak duality: c^\top x \leq (A^\top y)^\top x = y^\top A x \leq y^\top b, using x \geq 0, y \geq 0 and both sets of constraints. Strong duality follows from Farkas’ lemma; see Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis (1997, ch. 4) or the optimization refresher. ∎

Weak duality drives the primal–dual method of ch. 5 and 6: an online algorithm builds a primal solution and a dual one of value at most c times larger. If the dual is feasible, it bounds the optimum, and the algorithm is 1/c-competitive.

Definition 0.7 — Matching, vertex cover and their LPs In a graph G = (V, E), a matching is a set of edges without common vertex. Its fractional relaxation and the dual, a fractional vertex cover, are \max \Big\{ \sum_{e} x_e : \sum_{e \ni v} x_e \leq 1\ \forall v,\ x \geq 0 \Big\}, \qquad \min \Big\{ \sum_v y_v : y_u + y_v \geq 1\ \forall uv \in E,\ y \geq 0 \Big\}. For bipartite graphs both programs have integral optima, and the maximum matching size equals the minimum vertex cover size (König’s theorem).

0.4 Maximal matchings and metric spaces

Proposition 0.8 — Maximal matchings are half-optimal A matching M to which no edge can be added satisfies |M| \geq \tfrac12\, |M^\star| \quad \text{for every matching } M^\star .

Proof. Every edge of M^\star has an endpoint covered by M, otherwise it could be added. Each edge of M covers two vertices, each of which belongs to at most one edge of M^\star. Hence |M^\star| \leq 2|M|. ∎

A metric space (X, d) is a set with a distance that is symmetric, zero exactly on the diagonal and satisfies the triangle inequality. The servers of ch. 8 and the requests of ch. 9 live in such spaces; the line, with d(x, y) = |x - y|, and the uniform metric, with all distances equal to 1, are the two running examples.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The expected maximum is at most a threshold plus the sum of the expected excesses over it (Proposition 0.2).
  • With independent values, being still active at step i is independent of the value at step i.
  • Weak duality turns any feasible dual solution into an upper bound on the offline optimum.
  • A maximal matching has at least half the size of a maximum matching.

1. Online algorithms and competitive analysis

An online algorithm receives its input one piece at a time and must answer each piece before seeing the next. This chapter defines how such an algorithm is judged, solves the simplest non-trivial example, ski rental, and gives the standard tool for lower bounds.

1.1 The online model

Definition 1.1 — Online problem An input is a sequence of requests \sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_T). An online algorithm chooses its decision for \sigma_t knowing only \sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_t, and the decision cannot be undone. The offline optimum \mathrm{OPT}(\sigma) is the best cost, or the best gain, achievable with the whole of \sigma known in advance.

Definition 1.2 — Competitive ratio For a cost minimization problem, an algorithm \mathrm{ALG} is c-competitive if there is a constant \alpha with, for every input \sigma, \mathrm{ALG}(\sigma) \leq c \cdot \mathrm{OPT}(\sigma) + \alpha . For a gain maximization problem, it is c-competitive, with c \leq 1, if \mathrm{ALG}(\sigma) \geq c \cdot \mathrm{OPT}(\sigma) for every \sigma.

A randomized algorithm replaces \mathrm{ALG}(\sigma) by its expectation over the coins of the algorithm. The input is fixed before the coins are tossed: this is the oblivious adversary.

Ratios are above 1 for costs (paging, k-server) and below 1 for gains (prophets, matching). Against an adaptive offline adversary, who sees the coins as they fall, randomization brings nothing (Ben-David, Borodin, Karp, Tardos and Wigderson, 1994).

1.2 Ski rental

Renting skis costs 1 per day and buying them costs B days of rent. The trip lasts d days, unknown in advance, and the skier learns each morning whether the trip goes on. The offline optimum is \mathrm{OPT}(d) = \min(d, B).

A deterministic algorithm is a day k on which to buy if the trip is still going. It pays d if d < k, and k - 1 + B otherwise.

Theorem 1.3 — Deterministic ski rental Buying on day B is (2 - 1/B)-competitive, and no deterministic algorithm has a smaller ratio. The ratio is strict, \alpha = 0: with B fixed, an additive constant B would hide everything.

Proof. Upper bound. If d < B, the algorithm pays d = \mathrm{OPT}. If d \geq B, it pays 2B - 1 while \mathrm{OPT} = B.

Lower bound. Against “buy on day k”, the adversary stops the trip right after the purchase, so d = k. The algorithm pays k - 1 + B and \mathrm{OPT} = \min(k, B).

  • If k \leq B, the ratio is 1 + (B - 1)/k \geq 2 - 1/B.
  • If k > B, the ratio is (k - 1 + B)/B \geq 2. ∎

Randomization helps. In continuous time, buy at a random time t \in [0, B] with density p(t) = \frac{e^{t/B}}{B\,(e - 1)} . For a trip of length d \leq B, the expected cost is \int_0^d (t + B)\, p(t)\, dt + d \int_d^B p(t)\, dt = \frac{e}{e-1}\, d , by an integration by parts, and for d > B it is the value at d = B. The ratio e/(e - 1) \approx 1.582 is optimal (Karlin, Manasse, McGeoch and Owicki, 1994); the lower bound comes from the principle below.

1.3 Yao’s principle

Theorem 1.4 — Yao’s principle for competitive ratios Let D be a probability distribution on a finite set of inputs, and suppose that every deterministic algorithm A satisfies \mathbb{E}_{\sigma \sim D}[A(\sigma)] \geq c\; \mathbb{E}_{\sigma \sim D}[\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)] . Then every randomized algorithm R has an input \sigma with \mathbb{E}[R(\sigma)] \geq c \cdot \mathrm{OPT}(\sigma) .

Proof. A randomized algorithm is a probability distribution over deterministic algorithms. By Fubini, \mathbb{E}_{\sigma \sim D}\, \mathbb{E}_{A \sim R}[A(\sigma)] = \mathbb{E}_{A \sim R}\, \mathbb{E}_{\sigma \sim D}[A(\sigma)] \geq c\, \mathbb{E}_{\sigma \sim D}[\mathrm{OPT}(\sigma)] . The random variable \mathbb{E}_R[R(\sigma)] - c\, \mathrm{OPT}(\sigma) therefore has a non-negative mean under D, so it is non-negative for at least one \sigma. ∎

A randomized lower bound thus only needs one distribution of hard inputs. Exercise 8 applies it to ski rental with B = 2, where the optimal randomized ratio is 4/3.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

B = 10                      # price of the skis, in days of rent
days = np.arange(1, B + 1)  # d >= B behaves like d = B (OPT = B)

def cost(k, d):             # buy on the morning of day k, ski for d days
    return d if d < k else (k - 1) + B

def opt(d):
    return min(d, B)

# Deterministic: worst-case ratio of "buy on day k"
for k in [1, 5, B, 15]:
    worst = max(cost(k, d) / opt(d) for d in range(1, 3 * B))
    print(f"buy on day {k:2d}: worst ratio {worst:.3f}")

# Randomized: the best distribution over k = 1..B solves an LP
# variables (p_1..p_B, c); minimise c s.t. E[cost(d)] <= c * opt(d) for all d
A, b = [], []
for d in days:
    A.append([cost(k, d) for k in days] + [-opt(d)])
    b.append(0.0)
res = linprog(c=[0] * B + [1], A_ub=A, b_ub=b,
              A_eq=[[1] * B + [0]], b_eq=[1], bounds=[(0, None)] * (B + 1), method="highs")
p, ratio = res.x[:B], res.x[B]
print("optimal randomized ratio:", round(ratio, 4))
print("closed form 1/(1-(1-1/B)^B):", round(1 / (1 - (1 - 1 / B) ** B), 4), " limit e/(e-1):", round(np.e / (np.e - 1), 4))
print("p_k ratios p_{k+1}/p_k:", np.round(p[1:] / p[:-1], 4)[:4])

With B = 10, buying on day 10 has worst ratio 1.900, while buying on day 1 or day 15 is far worse. The LP over distributions of the buying day returns 1.5353, the discrete form of e/(e-1): \frac{1}{1 - (1 - 1/B)^B} = 1.5353 . The optimal probabilities grow geometrically, by the factor 1/(1 - 1/B) per day, the discrete twin of the density p(t).

1.4 Online algorithms are not online learning

The course description insists on a disclaimer: online algorithms are related to online learning and bandits, but the question is when to make a decision rather than which decision to make.

  • Online learning repeats the same decision problem. The benchmark is the best fixed action in hindsight, decisions do not change future problems, and regret grows sublinearly (see Sequential Learning).
  • Online algorithms carry a state: a budget spent, a vertex matched, a server position. The benchmark is the best sequence of decisions in hindsight, with a multiplicative guarantee on every input.

In ski rental there is no fixed action to learn: the whole problem is one decision of when to buy. Bridges exist all the same, and ch. 6.3 and ch. 8.4 use online convex optimization inside online algorithms.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • An online algorithm is judged by its competitive ratio against the offline optimum, on every input.
  • Randomized algorithms are judged against an oblivious adversary, who fixes the input before the coins are tossed.
  • Ski rental: buying on day B gives 2 - 1/B deterministically; randomization gives e/(e-1), and both are optimal.
  • Yao’s principle turns a distribution of hard inputs into a lower bound for randomized algorithms.
  • Competitive analysis compares with the best sequence of decisions; online learning compares with the best fixed action.

2. Stopping times and the secretary problem

A stopping problem asks for the best moment to take an irrevocable action while information keeps arriving. This chapter solves it by backward induction and applies the method to the secretary problem.

2.1 Filtrations and stopping times

Definition 2.1 — Filtration and stopping time Let X_1, \dots, X_n be the observations and \mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_t) the information available at time t. A random time \tau with values in \{1, \dots, n\} \cup \{\infty\} is a stopping time if, for every t, \{\tau = t\} \in \mathcal{F}_t . The decision to stop at t uses the past and the present, never the future. A randomized stopping time may also use a random variable drawn at the start, independent of the observations.

The first time X_t exceeds a fixed threshold is a stopping time. The time of the overall maximum is not, since recognizing it requires later values; the prophet of ch. 3 is the player allowed to use it.

2.2 Backward induction

Let Y_t be the reward for stopping at time t, a function of X_1, \dots, X_t, with \mathbb{E}|Y_t| < \infty. The problem is to maximize \mathbb{E}[Y_\tau] over stopping times \tau \leq n.

Theorem 2.2 — Snell envelope Define backwards V_n = Y_n and V_t = \max\big(Y_t,\ \mathbb{E}[V_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\big), \qquad t = n-1, \dots, 1. The stopping time \tau^\star = \min\{t : Y_t = V_t\} is optimal, and \max_{\tau} \mathbb{E}[Y_\tau] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{\tau^\star}] = \mathbb{E}[V_1].

Proof.

  1. For any stopping time \tau \leq n, write the telescoping sum V_\tau = V_1 + \sum_{t=1}^{n-1} (V_{t+1} - V_t)\, \mathbf{1}\{\tau > t\} .
  2. The event \{\tau > t\} belongs to \mathcal{F}_t, and \mathbb{E}[V_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] \leq V_t by definition. Each term has a non-positive expectation, so \mathbb{E}[V_\tau] \leq \mathbb{E}[V_1].
  3. Since Y_\tau \leq V_\tau, every stopping time gives \mathbb{E}[Y_\tau] \leq \mathbb{E}[V_1].
  4. For \tau^\star, on \{\tau^\star > t\} we have Y_t < V_t, hence V_t = \mathbb{E}[V_{t+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]. Every term of step 2 has zero expectation, and Y_{\tau^\star} = V_{\tau^\star}, so equality holds. ∎

The process (V_t), the Snell envelope, is the smallest supermartingale above (Y_t).

When the X_t are independent and non-negative and Y_t = X_t, the conditional expectations are constants. With v_{n+1} = 0, the values satisfy v_t = \mathbb{E}\big[\max(X_t,\ v_{t+1})\big], and the optimal rule stops at the first t with X_t \geq v_{t+1}, a sequence of decreasing thresholds.

Example 2.3 — Three uniform offers Three independent offers, uniform on [0, 1], arrive in turn.

  • At the last step, v_3 = 1/2.
  • Then v_2 = \mathbb{E}[\max(U, 1/2)] = 5/8: accept the second offer if it exceeds 1/2.
  • Then v_1 = \mathbb{E}[\max(U, 5/8)] = 89/128: accept the first offer if it exceeds 5/8.

The optimal value is v_1 = 89/128 \approx 0.695, against \mathbb{E}[\max] = 3/4 for a player who sees all three offers (Example 0.3).

2.3 The secretary problem

Definition 2.4 — Secretary problem Candidates 1, \dots, n have distinct qualities and arrive in a uniformly random order. After each interview, the employer knows only how the current candidate compares with those already seen, and hires or rejects for good. The goal is to maximize the probability of hiring the best candidate.

Theorem 2.5 — The 1/e law (Lindley, 1961; Dynkin, 1963) The rule that rejects the first r - 1 candidates and then hires the first one better than all before succeeds with probability P_n(r) = \frac{r-1}{n} \sum_{i=r}^{n} \frac{1}{i-1} \qquad (2 \leq r \leq n). The best r satisfies r/n \to 1/e, and

Key result — the 1/e law \max_r P_n(r) \longrightarrow \frac1e \approx 0.368 .

Proof.

  1. The best candidate is at position i with probability 1/n.
  2. The rule hires it if and only if i \geq r and nobody before position i triggers the rule first.
  3. That happens exactly when the best of the first i - 1 candidates sits among the first r - 1, an event of probability (r-1)/(i-1).
  4. Summing over i gives P_n(r).
  5. With r \approx xn, the sum is a Riemann sum, and P_n(r) \to x \int_x^1 \frac{dt}{t} = -x \ln x , maximal at x = 1/e with value 1/e. ∎

The threshold rule is optimal among all stopping rules. Call candidate i a record if it beats everybody before it; the indicators of records are independent, with probabilities 1/i (Rényi). A record at position i is the overall best with probability i/n, increasing in i, while the value of continuing decreases, so backward induction stops at the first record after a fixed position.

The same structure solves a larger family. Bruss’s odds theorem (2000) considers independent events I_1, \dots, I_n of probabilities p_j and odds r_j = p_j / (1 - p_j), and the goal of stopping on the last event that occurs. The optimal rule stops at the first event from index s on, where s is the largest index with \sum_{j \geq s} r_j \geq 1 . It wins exactly when one event occurs among I_s, \dots, I_n, with probability \prod_{j \geq s}(1 - p_j) \sum_{j \geq s} r_j, and this probability is at least 1/e whenever the total odds reach 1 (Bruss, 2003). Records give p_j = 1/j and recover Theorem 2.5.

2.4 The secretary problem as a linear program

Buchbinder, Jain and Singh (2014) write every stopping rule as a point of a polytope. Let x_i be the probability that a rule hires the candidate at position i. Hiring candidate i requires that nobody was hired before and that i is a record, two independent events, so x_i \leq \frac{1}{i} \Big(1 - \sum_{j < i} x_j\Big). A rule that only hires records wins with probability \sum_i (i/n)\, x_i. Conversely, every feasible x is realized by the rule that hires a record at position i with the right conditional probability.

Proposition 2.6 — The secretary LP The best success probability equals the value of \max \Big\{ \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{i}{n}\, x_i \ :\ i\, x_i + \sum_{j < i} x_j \leq 1\ \ \forall i,\ \ x \geq 0 \Big\}. Its optimal solution is x_i = 0 before the threshold and x_i = (r - 1)/(i(i-1)) from r on, the threshold rule; a dual solution certifies its optimality.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

n = 100

def success(r, n):          # reject the first r-1, then take the first record
    if r == 1:
        return 1 / n
    return (r - 1) / n * sum(1 / (i - 1) for i in range(r, n + 1))

r_star = max(range(1, n + 1), key=lambda r: success(r, n))
print(f"n={n}: best r = {r_star}, P(best) = {success(r_star, n):.4f}, 1/e = {1/np.e:.4f}")

# The LP of Buchbinder, Jain and Singh: x_i = P(select the i-th candidate)
#   max sum_i (i/n) x_i   s.t.   i * x_i + sum_{j<i} x_j <= 1,  x >= 0
A = np.tril(np.ones((n, n)), -1) + np.diag(np.arange(1, n + 1))
res = linprog(-np.arange(1, n + 1) / n, A_ub=A, b_ub=np.ones(n),
              bounds=[(0, None)] * n, method="highs")
x = res.x
print(f"LP value = {-res.fun:.4f}; first index with x_i > 0: {np.argmax(x > 1e-9) + 1}")

# Monte Carlo check of the threshold rule
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
trials, wins = 100_000, 0
for _ in range(trials):
    ranks = rng.permutation(n)          # rank 0 = best candidate
    best_seen = ranks[: r_star - 1].min()
    later = ranks[r_star - 1:]
    pick = later[np.argmax(later < best_seen)] if (later < best_seen).any() else None
    wins += (pick == 0)
print(f"simulated success: {wins / trials:.4f}")

For n = 100, the best rule rejects the first 37 candidates and wins with probability 0.3710, slightly above 1/e. The LP returns the same value, positive from position 38 on, and 100,000 simulated orders give 0.3703.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • A stopping time decides with the past and present only; the time of the maximum is not one.
  • Backward induction (the Snell envelope) solves every finite-horizon stopping problem.
  • With independent values, the optimal rule uses decreasing thresholds v_{t+1}.
  • The secretary problem: skip about n/e candidates, then take the first record; success tends to 1/e.
  • The odds theorem and the secretary LP generalize the 1/e law.

3. Prophet inequalities

A gambler sees independent values one at a time and may stop once; a prophet sees them all and takes the largest. This chapter shows that a stopping rule secures half of the prophet’s expected gain in general, and 1 - 1/e for identically distributed values.

3.1 The gambler and the prophet

Definition 3.1 — Prophet inequality setting Let X_1, \dots, X_n be independent, non-negative and integrable, with known distributions, observed in this order. The gambler chooses a (possibly randomized) stopping time \tau and receives X_\tau, or 0 if he never stops. The prophet receives \max_i X_i. The competitive ratio of a rule is the worst case, over all distributions, of \frac{\mathbb{E}[X_\tau]}{\mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i]} .

The course description writes the ratio as \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] / \max_i X_i; the standard benchmark is the expectation of the maximum. Backward induction (Theorem 2.2) gives the optimal gambler, but its value is hard to compare with the prophet. Threshold rules, which stop at the first X_i \geq T, are easier to analyse and nearly as good.

3.2 The factor one half

Theorem 3.2 — Prophet inequality (Krengel and Sucheston, 1977; Samuel-Cahn, 1984) Let \tau stop at the first X_i \geq T, and let p be the probability that it stops at all. Then \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] \geq p\, T + (1 - p)\, \big(\mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i] - T\big). With T = \mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i]/2 (Kleinberg and Weinberg, 2012), or, for continuous distributions, with T chosen so that p = 1/2 (Samuel-Cahn, 1984),

Key result — the prophet inequality \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] \geq \tfrac12\, \mathbb{E}\big[\max_i X_i\big].

Proof.

  1. Split the gain into the threshold and the excess over it. When the rule stops at i, X_i = T + (X_i - T)^+, so \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = p\, T + \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\big[(X_i - T)^+\, \mathbf{1}\{\tau \geq i\}\big].
  2. The event \{\tau \geq i\}, that no earlier value reached T, depends on X_1, \dots, X_{i-1} only. By Proposition 0.4 the i-th term is \mathbb{E}[(X_i - T)^+]\, \mathbb{P}(\tau \geq i).
  3. Each \mathbb{P}(\tau \geq i) is at least the probability 1 - p of never stopping.
  4. By Proposition 0.2, the sum of the expected excesses is at least \mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i] - T. This gives the first inequality.
  5. With T = \mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i]/2, both terms carry the factor T and add up to T. With p = 1/2, the right side is T/2 + (\mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i] - T)/2. ∎

The rule with T = \mathbb{E}[\max]/2 needs only one number about the distributions, a robustness that matters for the auctions of ch. 4.

Proposition 3.3 — The factor 1/2 is tight No stopping rule, even the optimal one, guarantees more than 1/2. Take n = 2, X_1 = 1, and X_2 = 1/\varepsilon with probability \varepsilon, 0 otherwise. The prophet gets \mathbb{E}[\max(X_1, X_2)] = (1 - \varepsilon) + 1 = 2 - \varepsilon . The gambler gets 1 whether he stops at X_1 or waits, since \mathbb{E}[X_2] = 1. The ratio 1/(2 - \varepsilon) tends to 1/2.

Example 3.4 — Three uniform offers again For the offers of Example 2.3, \mathbb{E}[\max] = 3/4 and the optimal gambler gets 0.695, a ratio of 0.927. The threshold T = 3/8 stops at the first offer above it: \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = \frac{1 - T^2}{2}\,(1 + T + T^2) = \frac{55}{128} \cdot \frac{97}{64} \approx 0.651, a ratio of 0.868, well above the guarantee of 1/2. The median threshold 2^{-1/3} \approx 0.794 is too demanding here and gets only 0.598 of the prophet.

3.3 Identically distributed values

For i.i.d. values, a single threshold already does better than 1/2.

Theorem 3.5 — The i.i.d. prophet inequality with one threshold Let X_1, \dots, X_n be i.i.d. with a continuous distribution, and let T satisfy \mathbb{P}(X_1 \geq T) = 1/n. The threshold rule satisfies \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] \geq \Big(1 - \big(1 - \tfrac1n\big)^n\Big)\, \mathbb{E}\big[\max_i X_i\big] \geq \Big(1 - \frac1e\Big)\, \mathbb{E}\big[\max_i X_i\big].

Proof.

  1. Write q = 1/n and p = 1 - (1 - q)^n for the probability of stopping. Step 1 of Theorem 3.2 with \mathbb{P}(\tau \geq i) = (1 - q)^{i-1} gives \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = p\, T + \mathbb{E}[(X_1 - T)^+] \sum_{i=1}^n (1 - q)^{i-1} = p \Big(T + \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X_1 - T)^+]}{q}\Big).
  2. Since 1/q = n, the bracket is T + n\, \mathbb{E}[(X_1 - T)^+], which is at least \mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i] by Proposition 0.2.
  3. Finally (1 - 1/n)^n \leq 1/e. ∎

Adaptive thresholds do even better in the i.i.d. case. Hill and Kertz (1982) began the study of the optimal i.i.d. constant, Kertz (1986) identified the candidate value \approx 0.745, and Correa, Foncea, Hoeksma, Oosterwijk and Vredeveld (2017) proved that it is the exact worst-case ratio of the optimal gambler.

3.4 Random order: the prophet secretary

In the prophet secretary, the distributions differ but the values arrive in a uniformly random order. Esfandiari, Hajiaghayi, Liaghat and Monemizadeh (2015) reached 1 - 1/e with decreasing thresholds, and Correa, Saona and Ziliotto (2019) went beyond it with “blind” strategies. The optimal constant is open, as in the free order model, where the gambler chooses the order.

3.5 The ex-ante relaxation and contention resolution

The linear-optimisation view of ch. 2.4 also bounds the prophet. Let x_i be the probability that the prophet takes X_i, so that \sum_i x_i \leq 1.

For continuous distributions, among all events of probability x_i, the one that maximizes the expected gain from X_i is “X_i lies in its top x_i-quantile”; call r_i(x_i) that gain. This gives the ex-ante relaxation: \mathbb{E}\big[\max_i X_i\big] \leq \max \Big\{ \sum_{i=1}^n r_i(x_i) : \sum_i x_i \leq 1,\ x \geq 0 \Big\}.

An online algorithm then tries to select each i with probability close to x_i. Call i active if X_i falls in its top x_i-quantile.

Proposition 3.6 — A 1/2 online contention resolution scheme If nothing has been selected before step i and i is active, select it with probability \frac{1}{2\,\big(1 - \sum_{j<i} x_j/2\big)} . Then each i is selected with probability exactly x_i/2, and the expected gain is half the value of the ex-ante relaxation, hence at least \mathbb{E}[\max_i X_i]/2.

Proof.

  1. By induction, nothing is selected before i with probability 1 - \sum_{j<i} x_j/2 \geq 1/2, so the selection probability above is at most 1.
  2. Activity of i is independent of the past (Proposition 0.4). Multiplying the three probabilities gives x_i/2.
  3. The decision does not depend on where X_i lies inside its top quantile, so the gain from i is r_i(x_i)/2. ∎

Feldman, Svensson and Zenklusen (2016) extend contention resolution to matroids, matchings and knapsacks, the route to prophet inequalities for many items.

import numpy as np

n = 3                        # X_1, X_2, X_3 i.i.d. uniform on [0, 1]
E_max = n / (n + 1)

# Optimal gambler (backward induction of ch. 2.2): v_n = 1/2, v_t = E[max(U, v_{t+1})]
v = 0.5
for _ in range(n - 1):
    v = (1 + v ** 2) / 2
print(f"prophet E[max] = {E_max:.4f}, optimal gambler = {v:.4f}, ratio {v / E_max:.3f}")

def threshold_value(T):      # E[X_tau] for 'stop at the first X_i >= T'
    return (1 - T ** 2) / 2 * sum(T ** i for i in range(n))

rules = {"T = E[max]/2": E_max / 2,
         "median of max": 0.5 ** (1 / n),
         "P(X >= T) = 1/n": 1 - 1 / n}
Ts = np.linspace(0, 1, 100_001)
rules["best single T"] = Ts[np.argmax(threshold_value(Ts))]
for name, T in rules.items():
    print(f"{name:16s} T = {T:.4f}  E[X_tau] = {threshold_value(T):.4f}  ratio {threshold_value(T) / E_max:.3f}")

# Ex-ante LP + online contention resolution scheme (OCRS), by simulation
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = np.full(n, 1 / n)                       # LP solution (symmetric)
LP = n * (x[0] - x[0] ** 2 / 2)             # sum_i E[X_i ; X_i >= 1 - x_i]
X = rng.random((200_000, n))
gain = np.zeros(len(X))
free = np.ones(len(X), dtype=bool)
for i in range(n):
    active = X[:, i] >= 1 - x[i]
    accept = rng.random(len(X)) < 1 / (2 * (1 - x[:i].sum() / 2))
    take = free & active & accept
    gain[take] = X[take, i]
    free &= ~take
print(f"ex-ante LP = {LP:.4f} >= E[max]; OCRS value = {gain.mean():.4f} (LP/2 = {LP / 2:.4f})")

The run confirms Example 3.4. The 1/n-quantile threshold of Theorem 3.5 gets 0.782 of the prophet, above its guarantee 0.704, and the best single threshold, T \approx 0.455, gets 0.879. The ex-ante LP is worth 5/6, and contention resolution collects half of it, 0.416: weak here, but robust to combinatorial constraints.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Prophet inequality: one threshold, T = \mathbb{E}[\max]/2 or the median of the maximum, secures half the prophet’s gain.
  • The proof splits the gain into threshold and excess and uses only independence and Proposition 0.2.
  • The factor 1/2 is tight, already with two values.
  • For i.i.d. values, the 1/n-quantile threshold gives 1 - 1/e, and adaptive thresholds reach about 0.745.
  • The ex-ante LP bounds the prophet; online contention resolution rounds it with a factor 1/2.

4. Pandora’s box, posted prices and online auctions

In Pandora’s problem, the decision maker chooses which value to inspect next and pays for each inspection. This chapter derives the optimal policy, then turns stopping rules into prices, the modern motivation of prophet inequalities.

4.1 Pandora’s box

Definition 4.1 — Pandora’s problem and reservation values Box i \in \{1, \dots, n\} contains an independent value V_i \geq 0 with known distribution, and opening it costs c_i > 0. Boxes are opened one at a time in an adaptive order; at any moment Pandora may stop and keep the largest value found. Her utility is that value minus the total cost paid. The reservation value \sigma_i of box i solves \mathbb{E}\big[(V_i - \sigma_i)^+\big] = c_i .

The reservation value is the price at which Pandora is indifferent between holding a sure value \sigma_i and paying c_i to see whether the box beats it.

Theorem 4.2 — Weitzman’s rule (Weitzman, 1979) The following policy is optimal: open the boxes in decreasing order of \sigma_i, and stop as soon as the best value found is at least the largest reservation value among the closed boxes. Its expected utility is \mathbb{E}\Big[\max_i \kappa_i\Big], \qquad \kappa_i = \min(V_i, \sigma_i).

Proof. The proof of Kleinberg, Waggoner and Weyl (2016) compares every policy with the capped values \kappa_i. Let I_i indicate that box i is opened and A_i \leq I_i that its value is kept.

  1. The decision to open box i is taken before seeing V_i, so I_i is independent of V_i and \mathbb{E}[I_i\, c_i] = \mathbb{E}\big[I_i\, (V_i - \sigma_i)^+\big].
  2. Hence the utility of any policy satisfies \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_i A_i V_i - I_i c_i\Big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_i A_i V_i - I_i (V_i - \sigma_i)^+\Big] \leq \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_i A_i \kappa_i\Big], using A_i \leq I_i and V_i - (V_i - \sigma_i)^+ = \kappa_i.
  3. At most one box is kept, so the right side is at most \mathbb{E}[\max_i \kappa_i], for every policy.
  4. Step 2 is an equality when every opened box with V_i > \sigma_i is kept. Under Weitzman’s rule, such a box beats every remaining reservation value, and the best value seen before it, which was below \sigma_i; the rule stops at once and keeps it.
  5. Step 3 is an equality when the kept box s has the largest capped value. If V_s \leq \sigma_s, then \kappa_s = V_s dominates the values opened and the reservation values left. Otherwise s was kept right after being opened, and every other \kappa_j lies below \sigma_s = \kappa_s. ∎

Example 4.3 — Two boxes Box 1 holds a uniform value on [0, 1] and costs 0.08; box 2 holds 2 or 0 with probability 1/2 each and costs 0.4.

  • Box 1: (1 - \sigma_1)^2/2 = 0.08 gives \sigma_1 = 0.6. Box 2: (2 - \sigma_2)/2 = 0.4 gives \sigma_2 = 1.2.
  • Open box 2 first. If it holds 2, stop, since 2 \geq 0.6. Otherwise open box 1 and keep its value.
  • The utility is -0.4 + 0.5 \cdot 2 + 0.5\,(0.5 - 0.08), and the capped values give the same number: \mathbb{E}[\max(\kappa_1, \kappa_2)] = 0.5 \cdot 1.2 + 0.5 \cdot 0.42 = 0.81 . Opening box 1 first is worse: box 1 never reveals more than \sigma_2, so box 2 is always opened, and the utility drops to 0.77.

The reservation value of a box depends on that box only. Variants break this: when Pandora may take a box without opening it (Doval, 2018; Beyhaghi and Kleinberg, 2019), index-based policies only approximate the optimum.

4.2 From stopping rules to posted prices

A seller has one item and buyers arrive one by one, with independent private values V_i \sim F_i. The seller posts a price p_i to buyer i, who buys if V_i \geq p_i. One price T for everybody is the threshold rule of ch. 3, seen from the buyers’ side.

  • Welfare. By Theorem 3.2, the price T = \mathbb{E}[\max_i V_i]/2 gives the item to a buyer worth at least half the best one in expectation, the welfare of an auction with all buyers present (Hajiaghayi, Kleinberg and Sandholm, 2007).
  • Simplicity. Each buyer faces a take-it-or-leave-it offer, so truth-telling is trivial and buyers need not be present together (Lucier, 2017).

4.3 Revenue and virtual values

For revenue, Myerson’s theory supplies the right variables. Let F_i have a density f_i and define the virtual value \varphi_i(v) = v - \frac{1 - F_i(v)}{f_i(v)} . The distribution is regular if \varphi_i is non-decreasing.

Theorem 4.4 — Myerson’s lemma (Myerson, 1981) For every truthful mechanism in which a buyer of value 0 pays nothing, the expected revenue equals the expected virtual value of the winner. Consequently, for regular distributions, the optimal auction gives the item to the buyer with the largest virtual value when it is non-negative, and its revenue is \mathbb{E}\Big[\max_i\, \varphi_i(V_i)^+\Big].

Apply the prophet inequality to the independent variables \varphi_i(V_i)^+. A threshold T on virtual values is the price \varphi_i^{-1}(T) for buyer i, and its revenue is the virtual value it collects. Chawla, Hartline, Malec and Sivan (2010) deduce that sequential posted prices earn half the optimal revenue, and extend this to several items.

Example 4.5 — Two uniform buyers With two buyers uniform on [0, 1], \varphi(v) = 2v - 1. The optimal auction is a second-price auction with reserve price 1/2, and its revenue is 5/12 \approx 0.417.

  • One price p for both buyers earns p\,(1 - p^2), maximal at p = 1/\sqrt{3} with 0.385.
  • Adapted prices, p_1 for the first buyer and then the single-buyer optimum 1/2 for the second, earn p_1 (1 - p_1) + p_1/4, maximal at p_1 = 5/8: \text{revenue} = 25/64 \approx 0.391, that is 94 % of the optimal auction, from prices computed by backward induction (Theorem 2.2).
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
N = 400_000
# Box 1: V ~ U[0,1], cost 0.08.  Box 2: V = 2 w.p. 1/2, else 0, cost 0.4.
samplers = [lambda m: rng.random(m), lambda m: 2.0 * (rng.random(m) < 0.5)]
costs = np.array([0.08, 0.4])
excess = [lambda s: max(1 - s, 0) ** 2 / 2, lambda s: 0.5 * max(2 - s, 0)]   # E[(V - s)^+]
sigma = np.array([brentq(lambda s, i=i: excess[i](s) - costs[i], 0, 2) for i in range(2)])
print("reservation values sigma =", np.round(sigma, 4))

V = np.column_stack([f(N) for f in samplers])

def run(order):              # open in 'order', stop when best >= max remaining sigma
    best, paid = np.zeros(N), np.zeros(N)
    stopped = np.zeros(N, dtype=bool)
    for j, i in enumerate(order):
        rest = sigma[order[j:]].max()
        stopped |= best >= rest
        go = ~stopped
        paid[go] += costs[i]
        best[go] = np.maximum(best[go], V[go, i])
    return (best - paid).mean()

print(f"Weitzman order {np.argsort(-sigma) + 1}: utility {run(np.argsort(-sigma)):.4f}")
print(f"reverse order: utility {run(np.argsort(sigma)):.4f}")
kappa = np.minimum(V, sigma)
print(f"E[max capped value] = {kappa.max(axis=1).mean():.4f}")

# Posted prices for one item and two buyers with values U[0,1]
p = np.linspace(0, 1, 100_001)
single = p * (1 - p ** 2)                          # same price for both buyers
adaptive = p * (1 - p) + p * 0.25                  # price p, then 1/2 to buyer 2
print(f"one price: best p = {p[single.argmax()]:.4f}, revenue {single.max():.4f}")
print(f"two prices: best p1 = {p[adaptive.argmax()]:.4f}, revenue {adaptive.max():.4f}")
print(f"Myerson optimal auction revenue = {5 / 12:.4f}")

The simulation recovers the reservation values 0.6 and 1.2, a utility of 0.809 for Weitzman’s order against 0.769 for the reverse order, and \mathbb{E}[\max_i \kappa_i] = 0.810; the pricing part reproduces Example 4.5.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • The reservation value of a box solves \mathbb{E}[(V - \sigma)^+] = c and depends on that box only.
  • Weitzman’s rule, by decreasing reservation values with a stopping test, is optimal, and its value is the expected maximum capped value.
  • A threshold stopping rule is a posted price; the prophet inequality makes one price half-optimal for welfare.
  • With Myerson’s virtual values, the same argument makes sequential posted prices half-optimal for revenue.

5. Online bipartite matching

In online matching, one side of a bipartite graph is known and the other arrives vertex by vertex, each matched at once or never. Deterministic algorithms lose half of the optimum; one random permutation recovers 1 - 1/e.

5.1 The model

Definition 5.1 — Online bipartite matching Let G = (U \cup V, E) be bipartite. The offline vertices U are present from the start; the online vertices of V arrive one at a time, each revealing its edges to U.

On arrival, an online vertex is matched to a still unmatched neighbour or left unmatched, irrevocably. The gain is the size of the final matching, compared with the maximum matching \mathrm{OPT} of G.

In advertising, offline vertices are advertisers, online vertices are queries, and an edge means the ad is relevant.

5.2 Greedy and its limit

Proposition 5.2 — Greedy is 1/2-competitive, and no deterministic algorithm is better An algorithm that matches v whenever v has a free neighbour builds a maximal matching, hence |M| \geq \mathrm{OPT}/2 by Proposition 0.8.

Conversely, let U = \{u_1, u_2\} and let the first online vertex be adjacent to both. A deterministic algorithm matches it to some u, or not at all. The adversary then sends a second online vertex adjacent only to that u, or to u_1 if the first stayed unmatched. The algorithm ends with 1 edge while \mathrm{OPT} = 2.

A uniformly random free neighbour does not fix this: on some graphs that rule matches only about half of the optimum (Karp, Vazirani and Vazirani, 1990).

5.3 RANKING

RANKING (Karp, Vazirani and Vazirani, 1990) draws one random order of U at the start and matches every arriving vertex to its free neighbour of highest priority. Equivalently, each u \in U draws y_u uniform on [0, 1], independently, and smaller y_u means higher priority.

Theorem 5.3 — RANKING is (1 - 1/e)-competitive For every bipartite graph and every arrival order,

Key result — RANKING \mathbb{E}\big[|M_{\text{RANKING}}|\big] \geq \Big(1 - \frac1e\Big)\, \mathrm{OPT} .

Proof. The randomized primal–dual proof of Devanur, Jain and Kleinberg (2013) builds dual variables while RANKING runs. Let g(y) = e^{y - 1}. When an online vertex v is matched to u, set \alpha_u = g(y_u), \qquad \beta_v = 1 - g(y_u), and leave all other variables at 0. Each matched edge contributes exactly 1 to the dual objective, which therefore equals |M|.

  1. Goal. Suppose every edge uv satisfies \mathbb{E}[\alpha_u + \beta_v] \geq 1 - 1/e. Then the expected duals, divided by 1 - 1/e, form a fractional vertex cover of value \mathbb{E}|M| / (1 - 1/e), and weak duality (Theorem 0.6 for the programs of Definition 0.7) bounds \mathrm{OPT} by that value.
  2. Freeze all but one value. Fix an edge uv and the values of all offline vertices other than u. Run RANKING on the graph without u, and call y^c the value of the vertex that v gets in that run, or 1 if it stays unmatched.
  3. Monotonicity. Adding u back changes the run along a single alternating path, and every online vertex ends matched to a vertex of value at most its value without u (a lemma of the same paper). Hence \beta_v \geq 1 - g(y^c), whatever y_u.
  4. Dominance. If y_u < y^c, then u ends up matched. Otherwise the run with u never uses it and coincides with the run without it. But then, when v arrives, u is a free neighbour that beats the vertex v picks in that run: a contradiction.
  5. Integrate over y_u. Steps 3 and 4 give \mathbb{E}_{y_u}[\alpha_u + \beta_v] \geq \int_0^{y^c} e^{y - 1}\, dy + 1 - e^{y^c - 1} = 1 - \frac1e . Averaging over the frozen values proves the goal of step 1. ∎

Proposition 5.4 — No online algorithm beats 1 - 1/e Take |U| = |V| = n and let v_j be adjacent to u_{\pi(j)}, \dots, u_{\pi(n)} for a hidden uniform relabelling \pi; the graph has a perfect matching. Whatever an algorithm does, the free offline vertices it sees are exchangeable, and the expected size of its matching is at most (1 - 1/e)\, n + o(n). With Yao’s principle (Theorem 1.4), the bound holds for randomized algorithms (Karp, Vazirani and Vazirani, 1990).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, h, trials = 200, 100, 200

def trap():
    """v_i ~ u_i for all i; the first h online vertices also see u_h, ..., u_{n-1}."""
    return [np.r_[i, np.arange(h, n)] if i < h else np.array([i]) for i in range(n)]

def upper_triangular():
    """v_j sees u_pi(j), ..., u_pi(n-1) for a hidden random relabelling pi."""
    pi = rng.permutation(n)
    return [pi[j:] for j in range(n)]

def run(neigh, rule):
    rank = rng.permutation(n)          # used by RANKING only, drawn once
    matched, size = np.zeros(n, dtype=bool), 0
    for nb in neigh:                   # online vertices arrive one by one
        free = nb[~matched[nb]]
        if len(free) == 0:
            continue
        if rule == "greedy":           # a deterministic greedy rule
            u = free.max()
        elif rule == "random":         # uniformly random free neighbour
            u = rng.choice(free)
        else:                          # RANKING: smallest rank
            u = free[np.argmin(rank[free])]
        matched[u], size = True, size + 1
    return size

for name, make in [("trap", trap), ("upper triangular", upper_triangular)]:
    res = {r: np.mean([run(make(), r) for _ in range(trials)]) / n
           for r in ["greedy", "random", "ranking"]}
    print(f"{name:17s}", "  ".join(f"{r} {v:.3f}" for r, v in res.items()))
print(f"1 - 1/e = {1 - 1 / np.e:.3f}")

On the trap, early vertices may take shared vertices that late vertices need. Greedy, which prefers them, matches 0.500 of the optimum and the random rule 0.517, while RANKING reaches 0.684. On the graph of Proposition 5.4, all three are stuck at 0.633, the bound 1 - 1/e up to finite-size effects.

5.4 Beyond the adversary

The bound 1 - 1/e is a worst case over both the graph and the arrival order. Weaker adversaries allow more.

  • Random order. Goel and Mehta (2008) show that greedy is (1 - 1/e)-competitive when the online vertices arrive in random order, and Mahdian and Yan (2011) show that RANKING then reaches at least 0.696.
  • Known i.i.d. arrivals. When online vertices are drawn from a known distribution, Feldman, Mehta, Mirrokni and Muthukrishnan (2009) beat 1 - 1/e by solving offline the expected instance and following two matchings computed from it.
  • Open questions. The exact random-order ratio of RANKING, and the best ratios when both sides arrive online, are open (ch. 10).

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Online bipartite matching: one side known, the other arriving with its edges; decisions are irrevocable.
  • Every greedy rule is 1/2-competitive, and no deterministic algorithm does better.
  • RANKING, one random priority order drawn in advance, is (1 - 1/e)-competitive, and no online algorithm does better.
  • The randomized primal–dual proof splits each matched edge between its endpoints with g(y) = e^{y-1}.
  • Random order and stochastic arrivals allow ratios above 1 - 1/e.

6. Budgets: b-matching, AdWords, resource allocation and delays

In advertising, an offline vertex is an advertiser matched many times until a budget runs out: spending now leaves less for later. This chapter extends ch. 5 to capacities and bids, then studies learned prices and matching with delays.

6.1 b-matching and BALANCE

In b-matching, each offline vertex can be matched up to b times. Greedy is still 1/2-competitive (Exercise 10). BALANCE matches each arriving vertex to the neighbour that has used the smallest fraction of its capacity.

Theorem 6.1 — BALANCE (Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs, 2000) BALANCE is deterministic and 1 - \frac{1}{(1 + 1/b)^b}\text{-competitive}, a ratio that tends to 1 - 1/e as b \to \infty. No deterministic algorithm does better.

Large capacities let a deterministic algorithm match what RANKING achieves with randomness for b = 1.

6.2 AdWords

Definition 6.2 — AdWords Advertisers u have budgets B_u. Queries q arrive one at a time, revealing bids b_{uq} \geq 0. Each query is given to at most one advertiser, who pays his bid, or what remains of his budget if that is less. The goal is to maximize the total revenue. Under the small-bids assumption, every bid is small compared with every budget.

Greedy, which gives each query to the highest remaining bid, is 1/2-competitive. Mehta, Saberi, Vazirani and Vazirani (2007) trade off bid and spent fraction f_u: give q to the advertiser that maximizes b_{uq}\, \psi(f_u), \qquad \psi(f) = 1 - e^{f - 1} .

Theorem 6.3 — AdWords with small bids (Mehta et al., 2007; Buchbinder, Jain and Naor, 2007) When the ratio of the largest bid to the smallest budget tends to 0, the algorithm above is (1 - 1/e)-competitive, and no deterministic algorithm does better.

Proof. The primal–dual proof of Buchbinder, Jain and Naor works on the fractional LP, whose dual is \min \Big\{ \sum_u B_u\, \alpha_u + \sum_q \beta_q \ :\ \beta_q \geq b_{uq}\,(1 - \alpha_u)\ \ \forall u, q,\ \ \alpha, \beta \geq 0 \Big\}.

  1. Keep \alpha_u = (e^{f_u} - 1)/(e - 1), which grows from 0 to 1 as advertiser u spends his budget. Give q to the advertiser maximizing b_{uq}(1 - \alpha_u), which is the rule of Mehta et al. up to a constant factor, and set \beta_q to that maximum.
  2. Dual feasibility. \beta_q covers every advertiser at the time of q, and \alpha_u only grows afterwards, so every constraint holds at the end.
  3. Dual growth. When u receives a small bid b, f_u grows by b/B_u. The dual objective then grows by about B_u\, \alpha'(f_u)\, \frac{b}{B_u} + b\,(1 - \alpha_u) = \frac{b\, e^{f_u} + b\,(e - e^{f_u})}{e - 1} = \frac{e}{e - 1}\, b, while the revenue grows by b.
  4. Summing, the final dual is e/(e-1) times the revenue, up to the small-bid error, and weak duality bounds the optimum by the dual. ∎

The same LP with unit bids and budgets b is b-matching, and the rule becomes BALANCE. Aggarwal, Goel, Karande and Mehta (2011) handle weights on the offline vertices, with one bid per advertiser, by perturbing RANKING’s priorities with \psi.

6.3 Online resource allocation by stochastic approximation

In practice, a campaign spreads its budget over the day, and queries look like i.i.d. draws from an unknown distribution. The dual of ch. 6.2 suggests a policy driven by prices: with a price \alpha_u per unit of budget, give each query to the advertiser maximizing b_{uq}(1 - \alpha_u), if positive.

The right prices minimize the dual. With T i.i.d. queries and target spending rates \rho_u = B_u / T, the dual per query is the convex function D(\alpha) = \sum_u \rho_u\, \alpha_u + \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\max\big(0,\ \max_u b_{uq}(1 - \alpha_u)\big)\Big]. Its gradient is an expectation over an unknown distribution of queries, the setting of stochastic approximation.

Definition 6.4 — Stochastic approximation (Robbins and Monro, 1951) To find a zero of a mean field h(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\xi[H(\theta, \xi)] observed only through noisy samples, the Robbins–Monro scheme draws a fresh \xi_t at each step and sets \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t\, H(\theta_t, \xi_t), \qquad \sum_t \eta_t = \infty, \quad \sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty . When h is a subgradient field of a convex function, this is stochastic subgradient descent.

Here the noise is the query q. The advertiser who maximizes b_{uq}(1 - \alpha_u), if positive, spends its bid, ignoring the budget cap. The vector g = \rho - \text{spend}(q) is then an unbiased stochastic subgradient of D, and the projected step is \alpha_u \leftarrow \big[\alpha_u + \eta\, (\text{spend}_u(q) - \rho_u)\big]^+ . Overspending raises an advertiser’s price; underspending lowers it.

Proposition 6.5 — Dual descent approaches the best prices Let the stochastic subgradients satisfy \|g_t\| \leq G, start at \alpha_1 = 0 and take a constant step \eta. For every \alpha^\star \geq 0, \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}\big[D(\alpha_t)\big] - D(\alpha^\star) \leq \frac{\|\alpha^\star\|^2}{2\eta T} + \frac{\eta\, G^2}{2} . With \eta = \|\alpha^\star\| / (G \sqrt{T}), the gap is at most G\, \|\alpha^\star\| / \sqrt{T}.

Proof.

  1. Projecting on \{\alpha \geq 0\} does not increase the distance to \alpha^\star, so \|\alpha_{t+1} - \alpha^\star\|^2 \leq \|\alpha_t - \alpha^\star\|^2 - 2\eta\, g_t^\top (\alpha_t - \alpha^\star) + \eta^2 G^2 .
  2. The price \alpha_t depends on the past queries only, and q_t is a fresh draw, so \mathbb{E}[g_t \mid \alpha_t] is a subgradient of D at \alpha_t. By convexity, \mathbb{E}\big[g_t^\top (\alpha_t - \alpha^\star)\big] \geq \mathbb{E}\big[D(\alpha_t)\big] - D(\alpha^\star) .
  3. Sum over t: the distances telescope from \|\alpha_1 - \alpha^\star\|^2 = \|\alpha^\star\|^2. Divide by 2 \eta T. ∎

Balseiro, Lu and Mirrokni (2020) turn this bound, in mirror-descent form and with the budget caps, into a revenue within O(\sqrt{T}) of the optimum under i.i.d. arrivals, so the ratio tends to 1. Devanur and Hayes (2009) learn the prices once on a first fraction of a randomly ordered input.

Under an adversarial order, learned prices can be badly wrong (Exercise 14), while the rule of Theorem 6.3 keeps 1 - 1/e.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)

def allocate(bids, budgets, rule, eta=0.0):
    """Online AdWords: each query goes to one advertiser, who pays min(bid, remaining budget)."""
    T, m = bids.shape
    spent, alpha = np.zeros(m), np.zeros(m)
    rho = budgets / T                                # target spending rate per query
    for t in range(T):
        left = budgets - spent
        b = np.where(left > 0, np.minimum(bids[t], left), 0.0)
        if rule == "greedy":                         # highest bid
            score = b
        elif rule == "msvv":                         # b * psi(f), psi(f) = 1 - e^(f-1)
            score = b * (1 - np.exp(spent / budgets - 1))
        else:                                        # dual prices learned online
            score = b * (1 - alpha)
        u = int(np.argmax(score + 1e-9 * np.arange(m)))   # ties go to the last index
        pay = b[u] if score[u] > 0 else 0.0
        spent[u] += pay
        if rule == "dual":                           # stochastic subgradient step on the dual,
            x = np.zeros(m); x[u] = pay              # with the step of advertiser u scaled by 1/rho_u
            alpha = np.maximum(0.0, alpha + eta * (x - rho) / rho)
    return spent.sum()

def offline_opt(bids, budgets):                     # LP relaxation = upper bound on OPT
    T, m = bids.shape
    c = -bids.ravel()                                # x[t, u] flattened
    A_q = np.kron(np.eye(T), np.ones(m))             # each query allocated at most once
    A_b = np.kron(np.ones(T), np.eye(m)) * bids.ravel()   # budget of each advertiser
    res = linprog(c, A_ub=np.vstack([A_q, A_b]), b_ub=np.r_[np.ones(T), budgets],
                  bounds=(0, 1), method="highs")
    return -res.fun

# (a) adversarial: advertisers 0..m-1, budget B; query group j is bid on by advertisers j..m-1
m, B = 10, 50
bids = np.vstack([np.tile((np.arange(m) >= j).astype(float), (B, 1)) for j in range(m)])
budgets = np.full(m, float(B))
opt = m * B
for rule in ["greedy", "msvv"]:
    print(f"(a) {rule:6s} revenue / OPT = {allocate(bids, budgets, rule) / opt:.3f}")

# (b) i.i.d. queries of two types: type X (bids 1 and 0.99), type Y (only advertiser 0 bids)
T = 2000
is_x = rng.random(T) < 0.5
bids = np.column_stack([np.ones(T), np.where(is_x, 0.99, 0.0)])
budgets = np.array([500.0, 1000.0])
lp = offline_opt(bids, budgets)
print(f"(b) offline LP bound = {lp:.1f}")
for rule, eta in [("greedy", 0), ("msvv", 0), ("dual", 0.01)]:
    print(f"(b) {rule:6s} revenue / LP = {allocate(bids, budgets, rule, eta) / lp:.3f}")

On the adversarial instance (a), with ties broken against the algorithm, greedy earns 0.500 of the optimum and the rule of Mehta et al. 0.658, close to 1 - (1 - 1/10)^{10} = 0.651. On the i.i.d. instance (b), greedy spends advertiser 0’s budget on queries that advertiser 1 values almost as much and earns 0.849 of the LP bound; the rule of Mehta et al. earns 0.998 and the learned prices 1.000.

6.4 Matching with delays

Emek, Kutten and Wattenhofer (2016) let the algorithm wait. Requests appear over time at points of a metric space and must eventually be matched in pairs; a pair costs the distance between its points, and every request pays the time it waits.

Proposition 6.6 — Neither haste nor patience is competitive On two points at distance D, let one request appear at each point at time 0, and again at time \varepsilon.

  • Matching as soon as two requests are pending pays 2D, while waiting \varepsilon and pairing requests at the same point costs only the waiting time 2\varepsilon: the ratio D/\varepsilon is unbounded.
  • A rule that waits for a partner at the same point pays a waiting cost that grows without bound when no other request arrives, while the optimum pays D at once.

A good algorithm waits until the waiting cost of a candidate pair is comparable with its distance, a rent-or-buy rule as in ski rental. On general metrics, Emek et al. combine it with a random tree embedding (defined in ch. 8.4) and obtain a ratio polylogarithmic in the number of points and the aspect ratio; Azar, Chiplunkar and Kaplan (2017) improve the bounds.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Capacities and budgets let deterministic algorithms reach 1 - 1/e by balancing the spent fractions.
  • The AdWords rule weighs each bid by \psi(f) = 1 - e^{f-1}; its proof keeps a dual whose growth is e/(e-1) times the revenue.
  • With i.i.d. queries, prices learned by stochastic subgradient steps on the dual approach the offline optimum, but they are not robust to adversarial orders.
  • Matching with delays mixes waiting costs and distances; rent-or-buy rules and tree embeddings handle it.

7. Paging, caching and metrical task systems

The third family of the course moves from gains to costs. An algorithm holds a state, such as a cache content or server positions, and pays to serve requests or to change state. This chapter treats paging, then the general model of metrical task systems.

7.1 Paging

Definition 7.1 — Paging A cache holds k pages out of a larger set. Requests for pages arrive one at a time. A request for a page in the cache is free; otherwise it is a fault, which costs 1 and brings the page in, evicting another page if the cache is full. The algorithm chooses which page to evict.

The offline optimum is explicit: evicting the page whose next request is furthest in the future minimizes the number of faults (Belady, 1966), by an exchange argument.

The usual online rules are LRU (evict the least recently used page) and FIFO (evict the page that entered first). To compare them with the optimum, cut the request sequence into phases: each phase is a maximal segment containing requests to exactly k distinct pages.

Theorem 7.2 — LRU is k-competitive (Sleator and Tarjan, 1985) For every request sequence, \mathrm{LRU} \leq k \cdot \mathrm{OPT} + k , and no deterministic algorithm has a ratio below k.

Proof.

  1. LRU pays at most k per phase. Once a page is requested in a phase, it stays among the k most recently used distinct pages until the phase ends, so LRU does not evict it. Each of the k pages of the phase faults at most once.
  2. OPT pays at least 1 per phase, except the last. Let p be the first request of a phase. The rest of the phase and the first request of the next phase involve k distinct pages other than p. Right after serving p, the optimum holds p and only k - 1 other pages, so it faults at least once in that window. The windows of different phases are disjoint.
  3. Lower bound. With k + 1 pages, the adversary always requests the page missing from the algorithm’s cache, so the algorithm faults at every request. Belady’s rule faults at most once every k requests, since the evicted page is not requested for the next k - 1 requests at least. ∎

The same phase argument proves that FIFO is k-competitive. Yet FIFO is not monotone in k: on the sequence 1 2 3 4 1 2 5 1 2 3 4 5, FIFO makes 9 faults with 3 slots and 10 with 4 slots (Belady’s anomaly), while LRU makes 10 and 8.

7.2 Randomized paging

The marking algorithm works in phases too. A page is marked when it is requested; on a fault with a full cache, it evicts an unmarked page chosen uniformly at random, and when every cached page is marked, it unmarks them all and starts a new phase.

Theorem 7.3 — Randomized paging (Fiat et al., 1991; McGeoch and Sleator, 1991) The marking algorithm is 2H_k-competitive, where H_k = 1 + 1/2 + \dots + 1/k \approx \ln k. No randomized algorithm is better than H_k-competitive, and the bound H_k is achieved.

The proof counts, in each phase, the new pages, those not requested in the previous phase; say there are m of them. The optimum pays at least half of the total number of new pages over all phases.

The marking algorithm pays 1 for each new page. The j-th old page requested in the phase has been evicted with probability at most m/(k - j + 1), and summing over j bounds the expected cost of the phase by m H_k. Randomization thus brings the ratio from k down to a logarithm.

import numpy as np
from collections import OrderedDict

def lru(seq, k):
    cache, faults = OrderedDict(), 0
    for p in seq:
        if p in cache:
            cache.move_to_end(p)
            continue
        faults += 1
        if len(cache) == k:
            cache.popitem(last=False)        # least recently used
        cache[p] = True
    return faults

def fifo(seq, k):
    cache, order, faults = set(), [], 0
    for p in seq:
        if p not in cache:
            faults += 1
            if len(cache) == k:
                cache.remove(order.pop(0))   # first in, first out
            cache.add(p); order.append(p)
    return faults

def marking(seq, k, rng):
    cache, marked, faults = set(), set(), 0
    for p in seq:
        if p not in cache:
            faults += 1
            if len(cache) == k:
                if marked >= cache:          # every page marked: a new phase starts
                    marked = set()
                cache.remove(rng.choice(sorted(cache - marked)))
            cache.add(p)
        marked.add(p)
    return faults

def belady(seq, k):                          # offline optimum: evict the furthest next use
    cache, faults = set(), 0
    for t, p in enumerate(seq):
        if p in cache:
            continue
        faults += 1
        if len(cache) == k:
            def next_use(q):
                rest = seq[t + 1:]
                return rest.index(q) if q in rest else len(seq)
            cache.remove(max(cache, key=next_use))
        cache.add(p)
    return faults

seq = [1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
print("k=3:", {"LRU": lru(seq, 3), "FIFO": fifo(seq, 3), "OPT": belady(seq, 3)})
print("k=4:", {"LRU": lru(seq, 4), "FIFO": fifo(seq, 4), "OPT": belady(seq, 4)})

# A cyclic scan of k+1 pages: the worst case for LRU and FIFO
k = 10
scan = list(range(k + 1)) * 30
mark = np.mean([marking(scan, k, rng) for _ in range(20)])
print(f"scan, k={k}: LRU {lru(scan, k)}, FIFO {fifo(scan, k)}, "
      f"marking {mark:.0f}, OPT {belady(scan, k)}")

On the cyclic scan of 11 pages with 10 slots, LRU and FIFO fault on all 330 requests, the optimum 42 times, and marking about 102 times: ratios 7.9 and 2.4, within the bounds k = 10 and 2H_{10} \approx 5.9.

7.3 Metrical task systems

Definition 7.4 — Metrical task system (Borodin, Linial and Saks, 1992) A system has n states with a metric d and starts in state s_0. At each time t, a task arrives as a cost vector c_t over the states. The algorithm moves to a state s_t and pays d(s_{t-1}, s_t) + c_t(s_t).

Paging is a task system whose states are the possible cache contents. The offline optimum is computed by dynamic programming on the work function w_t(s), the least cost of serving the first t tasks and ending in state s: w_t(s) = c_t(s) + \min_{s'} \big( w_{t-1}(s') + d(s', s) \big), \qquad w_0(s) = d(s_0, s).

Theorem 7.5 — Task systems (Borodin, Linial and Saks, 1992; Bubeck, Coester and Rabani, 2023) On every metric with n states, the best deterministic competitive ratio is exactly 2n - 1. For randomized algorithms, a ratio O(\log^2 n) is achievable on every metric (Bubeck, Cohen, Lee and Lee, 2019), and some metrics force \Omega(\log^2 n).

Good task-system algorithms balance, in each state, the task costs paid there against the cost of moving away: the rent-or-buy principle of ski rental and of ch. 6.4, applied state by state.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Belady’s rule, evicting the page needed furthest in the future, is the offline optimum for paging.
  • LRU and FIFO are k-competitive by the phase argument, and k is optimal for deterministic algorithms.
  • Random eviction among unmarked pages brings the ratio down to O(\log k), and H_k is optimal.
  • Metrical task systems generalize paging; work functions give the offline optimum; the deterministic ratio is 2n - 1.

8. The k-server problem and tree reductions

In the k-server problem, k servers move in a metric space to cover requests, and the question is how much more than the optimum an online algorithm must move. This chapter proves the lower bound k, reaches it on the line, and introduces the tree reduction behind the randomized results.

8.1 The problem and the conjecture

Definition 8.1 — The k-server problem (Manasse, McGeoch and Sleator, 1990) In a metric space (X, d), k servers start at given points. Each request is a point r_t; the algorithm must move some server to r_t, and pays the total distance travelled by its servers.

Paging is the k-server problem on the uniform metric, where every distance is 1 and the points are the pages. The k-server problem is also a task system whose states are the configurations of the servers.

Theorem 8.2 — The lower bound k (Manasse, McGeoch and Sleator, 1990) On every metric space with at least k + 1 points, no deterministic online algorithm is better than k-competitive.

Proof. Restrict to k + 1 points; every configuration then misses exactly one point.

  1. Adversary. Each request r_{t+1} is the point the algorithm misses. The algorithm serves it from some point, which becomes the next request r_{t+2}; its cost is at least \sum_t d(r_{t+1}, r_{t+2}).
  2. Offline algorithms. Start k offline algorithms in the k configurations that contain r_1. On request r_{t+1}, the one missing it moves its server from r_t; the others stay.
  3. Invariant. The k configurations remain distinct and all contain the last request: the mover now misses r_t, which the others contained.
  4. Averaging. Exactly one of them pays d(r_t, r_{t+1}) at each step, so together they pay the algorithm’s cost up to one term. The cheapest pays at most a 1/k fraction, and the optimum is cheaper still. ∎

The k-server conjecture states that a ratio of k is achievable on every metric. It holds for k = 2, for k + 1 points, on trees and on the line, and is open in general.

8.2 Double Coverage on the line

Double Coverage (DC) serves a request outside the interval spanned by the servers with the nearest server. A request between two adjacent servers moves both towards it at the same speed, until one of them reaches it.

Theorem 8.3 — DC is k-competitive on the line (Chrobak, Karloff, Payne and Vishwanathan, 1991) For every request sequence, \mathrm{DC} \leq k \cdot \mathrm{OPT} + \Phi_0 , where \Phi_0 depends only on the initial positions.

Proof. Let M be the cost of a minimum matching between the servers of DC and those of OPT; on the line, matching them in sorted order is optimal. Let S be the sum of the distances between pairs of DC servers. Use the potential \Phi = k\, M + S \geq 0 . At each request, OPT moves first, then DC.

  1. OPT moves a server by x. M grows by at most x and S is unchanged, so \Phi grows by at most k x.
  2. DC moves one extreme server by \delta. Say the request lies left of all DC servers. OPT has a server there, so the leftmost OPT server, sorted partner of the moving server, lies at the request or further left, and M drops by \delta. The moving server gets further from the others, so S grows by (k - 1)\delta. In total, the potential drops by the cost paid.
  3. DC moves two neighbours by \delta each. In the sorted matching, at least one of them moves towards its partner, otherwise the OPT server at the request would be matched across them. So M does not grow. The two servers get closer by 2\delta, and for every other server one gets closer and one moves away, so S drops by 2\delta, the cost paid.
  4. Summing, the cost of DC is at most k times the cost of OPT plus \Phi_0 - \Phi_{\text{end}} \leq \Phi_0. ∎

Moving the nearest server is not competitive: two close alternating requests make greedy shuttle one server forever, while the optimum brings the second server once.

import numpy as np

def greedy(servers, reqs):                   # move the nearest server
    s, cost = np.array(servers, float), 0.0
    for r in reqs:
        i = np.argmin(abs(s - r))
        cost += abs(s[i] - r); s[i] = r
    return cost

def double_coverage(servers, reqs):          # DC on the line
    s, cost = np.sort(np.array(servers, float)), 0.0
    for r in reqs:
        if r <= s[0] or r >= s[-1]:          # outside the hull: nearest extreme server
            i = 0 if r <= s[0] else len(s) - 1
            cost += abs(s[i] - r); s[i] = r
        else:                                # between neighbours: both move by delta
            j = np.searchsorted(s, r)        # s[j-1] < r <= s[j]
            delta = min(r - s[j - 1], s[j] - r)
            cost += 2 * delta
            s[j - 1] += delta; s[j] -= delta
        s.sort()
    return cost

def opt_two_servers(servers, reqs):          # offline optimum for k = 2 by dynamic programming
    pts = list(servers) + list(reqs)         # after request t one server sits at reqs[t]
    a, b = servers
    V = {1: abs(a - reqs[0]), 0: abs(b - reqs[0])}   # key = index in pts of the other server
    for t in range(1, len(reqs)):
        prev, cur, W = reqs[t - 1], reqs[t], {}
        for j, c in V.items():
            W[j] = min(W.get(j, np.inf), c + abs(prev - cur))                  # move the server at prev
            W[2 + t - 1] = min(W.get(2 + t - 1, np.inf), c + abs(pts[j] - cur))  # move the other one
        V = W
    return min(V.values())

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
start = (0.0, 10.0)
trap = [4.9, 5.1] * 500 + [9.0]              # greedy oscillates, DC brings the far server in
rand = list(rng.uniform(0, 10, 300))
for name, reqs in [("trap", trap), ("random", rand)]:
    o = opt_two_servers(start, reqs)
    print(f"{name:6s}: OPT {o:7.2f}   greedy/OPT {greedy(start, reqs) / o:5.2f}"
          f"   DC/OPT {double_coverage(start, reqs) / o:4.2f}  (k = 2)")

On the trap, greedy pays 15 times the optimum, a ratio that grows with the length of the sequence, while DC is optimal. On 300 uniform requests greedy does better than DC (1.08 against 1.50): a competitive ratio guarantees the worst case, it does not predict typical inputs.

8.3 The work function algorithm

The work function algorithm (WFA) moves to the configuration C that minimizes w_t(C) + d(C_{t-1}, C), with w_t the work function of ch. 7.3 on configurations: a balance between the cheapest move and the offline optimum.

Theorem 8.4 — WFA (Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou, 1995) On every metric space, the work function algorithm is (2k - 1)-competitive.

The proof is long and is surveyed by Koutsoupias (2009). Closing the gap between k and 2k - 1 is the deterministic k-server conjecture.

8.4 Tree reductions

Many online problems have good algorithms on trees, and random trees transfer them to every metric.

Definition 8.5 — Hierarchically separated tree (Bartal, 1996) A \gamma-HST is a rooted tree whose leaves are the points, with edge lengths that decrease by a factor at least \gamma from each level to the next. The distance between two leaves is the length of the tree path between them.

Theorem 8.6 — Random tree embeddings (Bartal, 1996; Fakcharoenphol, Rao and Talwar, 2004) Every metric on n points admits a probability distribution over HST metrics d_T such that, for all points x, y, d_T(x, y) \geq d(x, y), \qquad \mathbb{E}_T\big[d_T(x, y)\big] \leq O(\log n)\, d(x, y). Bartal’s first embeddings had a polylogarithmic distortion; the bound O(\log n), which is tight, is due to Fakcharoenphol, Rao and Talwar.

Proposition 8.7 — The tree reduction If a randomized algorithm is \alpha-competitive on HSTs, running it on a random tree of Theorem 8.6 is O(\alpha \log n)-competitive on the original metric, for any problem whose cost is a sum of distances. Indeed, distances only grow in the tree, the tree optimum is at most the tree cost of the original optimal solution, and that cost is O(\log n)\,\mathrm{OPT} in expectation.

Bansal, Buchbinder, Mądry and Naor (2015) gave the first randomized k-server algorithm with a ratio polylogarithmic in k and n. Bubeck, Cohen, Lee, Lee and Mądry (2018) reached O(\log^2 k) on HSTs with online mirror descent, the bridge with online learning of ch. 1.4. Bubeck, Coester and Rabani (2023) showed that some metrics force \Omega(\log^2 k), refuting the conjectured O(\log k).

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • k-server: move k servers to cover requests in a metric; paging is the uniform case.
  • The averaging argument over k offline algorithms proves that no deterministic ratio is below k.
  • Double Coverage is k-competitive on the line, by the potential k M + S.
  • The work function algorithm is (2k-1)-competitive everywhere; the gap to k is open.
  • Random HST embeddings reduce metric problems to trees at a cost O(\log n); randomized k-server needs \Omega(\log^2 k) on some metrics.

9. Convex body chasing and online transportation

The last in-class chapter takes metrical problems to continuous spaces and to transport.

9.1 Convex body chasing

Definition 9.1 — Convex body chasing (Friedman and Linial, 1993) A point starts at x_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d. At each time t, a closed convex set K_t is revealed, and the algorithm must move to a point x_t \in K_t, paying the Euclidean distance \|x_t - x_{t-1}\|.

It is a task system with infinitely many states, where the cost of a task is 0 inside K_t and infinite outside. Friedman and Linial proved a lower bound \sqrt{d} and asked whether a ratio depending on d only is possible.

Proposition 9.2 — The lower bound \sqrt{d} Start at x_0 = 0 and let K_t be the affine subspace where the first t coordinates equal signs \varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_t \in \{-1, 1\}, for t = 1, \dots, d. Choose \varepsilon_t uniformly at random, independently of the algorithm’s coins.

  • At step t, the t-th coordinate must move from its current value a to \varepsilon_t, at an expected cost (|1 - a| + |1 + a|)/2 \geq 1; the algorithm pays at least d in expectation.
  • The offline optimum moves once, from 0 to (\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_d), at cost \sqrt{d}.

By Yao’s principle (Theorem 1.4), no algorithm, even randomized, beats \sqrt{d}.

Theorem 9.3 — Chasing convex bodies (Bubeck, Lee, Li and Sellke, 2019; Argue, Gupta, Guruganesh and Tang, 2020; Sellke, 2020) Convex body chasing in \mathbb{R}^d admits a competitive ratio that depends on d only. Argue et al. and Sellke independently reached O(\min(d, \sqrt{d \log T})), with T the number of requests; Sellke’s ratio d holds in every norm.

Sellke’s algorithm moves to the Steiner point of a suitable body, the average over random directions of the extreme points of that body. The Steiner point always lies in the body and moves little when the body shrinks, the property behind the guarantee. The right dependence on d is open (ch. 10.3).

9.2 Online metric matching and online transportation

Definition 9.4 — Online metric matching Servers s_1, \dots, s_k sit at points of a metric space. Requests r_1, \dots, r_k arrive one at a time and must each be matched at once to a distinct free server, at a cost equal to their distance. The offline optimum is the minimum-cost perfect matching, a discrete optimal transport problem.

The online transportation problem of Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs (2000) gives each server site a capacity, so that it serves several requests: optimal transport in an online fashion.

Theorem 9.5 — Deterministic online metric matching (Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs, 1993; Khuller, Mitchell and Vazirani, 1994) The greedy rule, which takes the nearest free server, can be as bad as 2^k - 1. The permutation algorithm keeps an optimal offline matching of the requests seen so far; the optimum for t requests can be chosen to use the servers of the optimum for t - 1 requests plus one new server, and r_t takes that new server. It is (2k - 1)-competitive, and no deterministic algorithm does better.

Randomization and trees help again: on HSTs, randomized algorithms reach polylogarithmic ratios, which the reduction of Proposition 8.7 transfers to every metric (Meyerson, Nanavati and Poplawski, 2006; Bansal, Buchbinder, Gupta and Naor, 2014, with O(\log^2 k)).

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

def match_cost(reqs, servers):               # offline min-cost matching on the line
    C = abs(np.subtract.outer(reqs, servers))
    r, c = linear_sum_assignment(C)
    return C[r, c].sum()

def greedy(reqs, servers):
    free, cost = list(servers), 0.0
    for r in reqs:
        j = int(np.argmin([abs(r - s) for s in free]))
        cost += abs(r - free.pop(j))
    return cost

def permutation(reqs, servers):              # Kalyanasundaram-Pruhs / Khuller-Mitchell-Vazirani
    used, cost = [], 0.0
    for t, r in enumerate(reqs):
        cand = [s for s in range(len(servers)) if s not in used]
        best = min(cand, key=lambda s: match_cost(reqs[: t + 1], servers[used + [s]]))
        used.append(best)                    # the optimum for r_1..r_t uses one new server
        cost += abs(r - servers[best])       # r_t takes that new server
    return cost

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
k = 30
ratios = {"greedy": [], "permutation": []}
for _ in range(10):
    servers = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, k))
    reqs = rng.uniform(0, 1, k)
    opt = match_cost(reqs, servers)
    ratios["greedy"].append(greedy(reqs, servers) / opt)
    ratios["permutation"].append(permutation(reqs, servers) / opt)
for name, r in ratios.items():
    print(f"{name:12s} mean ratio {np.mean(r):.2f}, worst {np.max(r):.2f}  (2k - 1 = {2 * k - 1})")

servers, reqs = np.array([0.0, 2.0]), np.array([1.01, 2.0])   # the two-server trap
print("trap: greedy", greedy(reqs, servers), " permutation", permutation(reqs, servers),
      " OPT", match_cost(reqs, servers))

On random instances both rules stay within a factor 1.8 of the optimum, far from 2k - 1 = 59. On the two-server trap, a request just right of the midpoint lures both rules to the right server; the next request sits on that server, and both pay 2.99 against 1.01, close to 2k - 1 = 3.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Convex body chasing: no algorithm beats \sqrt{d}, and ratios depending on d only, such as O(d), exist.
  • Online metric matching is online optimal transport; the permutation algorithm is (2k - 1)-competitive and optimal among deterministic rules.
  • Tree embeddings again give polylogarithmic randomized ratios.

10. Research directions and open problems

This self-study chapter collects the open questions announced along the course, with pointers to the papers where they are stated. Each section can seed an internship subject or one of the projects below.

10.1 Stopping problems: constants, combinatorics and samples

  • Open constants. The worst-case ratio of the optimal gambler is known for adversarial order (1/2) and for i.i.d. values (about 0.745, Correa et al., 2017). For the prophet secretary and the free-order model, the gap between the best known algorithms and the best known impossibility results is still open.
  • Combinatorial selection. When the gambler may keep several values subject to a matroid constraint, Kleinberg and Weinberg (2012) keep the factor 1/2. Online contention resolution schemes (Feldman, Svensson and Zenklusen, 2016) give constant factors for matchings and knapsacks, and the best constants are an active topic.
  • Matroid secretary conjecture. Babaioff, Immorlica and Kleinberg (2007) conjectured that the secretary problem on every matroid admits a constant ratio. The best known ratios are of order \log \log of the rank (Lachish, 2014; Feldman, Svensson and Zenklusen, 2015); the conjecture is open.
  • Learning the distributions. The prophet inequality assumes known distributions. Azar, Kleinberg and Weinberg (2014) study what limited information suffices, and Rubinstein, Wang and Weinberg (2020) show that a single sample from each distribution already gives the factor 1/2. How many samples suffice for the i.i.d. constants is a natural question.

10.2 Online matching and allocation

  • Weights and general bids. For edge-weighted matching with free disposal, Fahrbach, Huang, Tao and Zadimoghaddam (2020) beat the greedy factor 1/2; Huang, Zhang and Zhang (2020) do the same for AdWords without the small-bids assumption. The optimal ratios in both settings are open.
  • Both sides online. In fully online matching, every vertex arrives and must be matched before a deadline (Huang, Kang, Tang, Wu, Zhang and Zhu, 2018); the tight ratio of RANKING-like rules is only partly known.
  • Random order. The exact random-order ratio of RANKING lies between 0.696 (Mahdian and Yan, 2011) and known upper bounds; closing that interval is open.
  • Learning and robustness. Dual-price learning (ch. 6.3) is near-optimal for i.i.d. inputs and fragile for adversarial ones. Algorithms that are good in both worlds at once, and the price of that robustness, are studied in the line of Balseiro, Lu and Mirrokni (2020).

10.3 k-server, task systems and chasing

  • The deterministic k-server conjecture. The ratio k is reached on lines, trees and spaces with k + 1 points, and 2k - 1 everywhere (Theorem 8.4). Whether k is achievable on every metric is open, even for k = 3 on general metrics.
  • Randomized k-server. After Bubeck, Coester and Rabani (2023), the question is the exact randomized ratio: between \Omega(\log^2 k) on some metrics and the best known polylogarithmic upper bounds, and how it depends on the metric.
  • Convex body chasing. The best ratio in \mathbb{R}^d lies between \sqrt{d} (Proposition 9.2) and O(\min(d, \sqrt{d \log T})) (Argue et al., 2020; Sellke, 2020); the right dependence on d is open, and so is the analogous question for chasing convex functions, the smoothed online convex optimization of Chen, Goel and Wierman (2018).
  • Online transport and matching on metrics. For online metric matching, randomized ratios are polylogarithmic in k, while the best lower bounds are logarithmic; on the line, the right order is still open.

10.4 Learning-augmented online algorithms

A recent line of work gives the online algorithm a prediction of the future, possibly wrong, and asks for two guarantees at once: consistency, a ratio close to 1 when the prediction is correct, and robustness, a bounded ratio whatever the prediction.

  • Lykouris and Vassilvitskii (2018) predict, for paging, the next request time of each page, and adapt the marking algorithm.
  • Purohit, Svitkina and Kumar (2018) predict the length of a ski trip. With a trust parameter \lambda \in (0, 1], they buy on day \lceil \lambda B \rceil if the prediction exceeds B and on day \lceil B/\lambda \rceil otherwise. The rule is (1 + \lambda)-consistent and (1 + 1/\lambda)-robust (Exercise 15).

Open directions include the best consistency–robustness trade-offs for matching and k-server, the value of a limited number of queries to the prediction, and predictions learned from data with statistical guarantees.

10.5 Bridges with online learning

The disclaimer of ch. 1.4 separates two theories, yet their tools meet. Mirror descent with entropic regularizers is the engine of the recent randomized results for task systems and k-server (Bubeck, Cohen, Lee and Lee, 2019; Bubeck et al., 2018). Dual descent for budgets (ch. 6.3) is an online convex optimization algorithm whose regret bound becomes a competitive guarantee. Bandit feedback inside stopping problems, where the distributions must be learned while stopping, and regret bounds for repeated prophet or secretary problems, are active questions at the frontier between the two fields.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • The optimal constants of the prophet secretary, of free-order stopping and of the matroid secretary problem are open.
  • Online matching with weights, general bids or two-sided arrivals still has gaps between algorithms and impossibility results.
  • The deterministic k-server conjecture is open; the randomized one is false, and the exact randomized ratio is unknown.
  • Learning-augmented algorithms trade consistency against robustness, and mirror descent links online algorithms with online learning.

  10. ★★ Greedy b-matching. Prove that any rule that matches an online vertex whenever one of its neighbours has spare capacity is 1/2-competitive for b-matching, and that the factor 1/2 is tight for some of these rules.

    Solution: call an offline vertex full if greedy uses all its capacity. Take an optimal b-matching. Each of its edges uv either has v matched by greedy, or u full when v arrived, otherwise greedy would have matched v.

  11. ★★ FIFO is k-competitive. Prove that on any segment of requests involving at most k distinct pages, FIFO faults at most k times, and conclude with the phase argument of Theorem 7.2.

Friedman and Linial (1993) give a constant-competitive algorithm for chasing lines in the plane.

  17. ★★★ Prophet secretary experiments. For n = 10 independent values with different distributions (uniform, exponential, two-point) in uniformly random order, compare by simulation: the single threshold \mathbb{E}[\max]/2, the 1/n-type single threshold of Theorem 3.5, and decreasing thresholds T_i chosen by backward induction for a random order. Search for distributions that make each rule bad.

    Hints and expected result: estimate \mathbb{E}[\max] by Monte Carlo and every rule over at least 10^5 orders. The single threshold always keeps its 1/2; good decreasing thresholds stay above 1 - 1/e (Esfandiari et al., 2015). Hard instances mix one rare large value with many moderate ones, as in Proposition 3.3; the worst ratio found by search is a lower estimate of the open constant of ch. 3.4.

Projects and assessment

master-mva.com announces a written exam (“Mode de validation”), and the Université Paris-Saclay course detail describes the assessment as “Evaluation Continue non Intégrale” (non-integral continuous assessment); no weights are displayed. The written exam below is a proposal in the spirit of a blackboard course: a course question, a computation and proofs. The projects are practice beyond the official assessment, in the MVA tradition of reproducing a research paper; each is evaluated with the rubric at the end of the section.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Prophet secretary and blind strategies (paper study: Esfandiari, Hajiaghayi, Liaghat and Monemizadeh, 2015; Correa, Saona and Ziliotto, 2019). How much do time-dependent thresholds gain over a single threshold when independent values arrive in random order?
    • Implement: the single threshold of Theorem 3.2, the decreasing thresholds of Esfandiari et al., one blind strategy of Correa et al., and the optimal policy for a known order by backward induction (ch. 2, 3).
    • Data: synthetic families (uniform, exponential, two-point and mixtures), n from 2 to 50.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk.
    • Success criterion: every rule above its proven guarantee on every instance, and a table of the worst ratios found by a random search over distributions.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] The randomized primal–dual proof of RANKING (paper study: Devanur, Jain and Kleinberg, 2013). Can the dual certificate of Theorem 5.3 be checked exactly?
    • Prove: the monotonicity lemma used in step 3, in full; then the vertex-weighted extension of Aggarwal, Goel, Karande and Mehta (2011) (ch. 0, 5).
    • Implement: exact expectations of the duals on small bipartite graphs, by enumerating all priority orders and arrival orders.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report with the proofs, a 10-minute talk.
    • Success criterion: \mathbb{E}[\alpha_u + \beta_v] \geq 1 - 1/e verified for every edge of every bipartite graph with at most four vertices per side, with a discrete analogue of g(y) = e^{y - 1}.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Caching with predictions (paper study: Lykouris and Vassilvitskii, 2018). How does the predictive marking algorithm degrade when the predicted next-request times are noisy?
    • Implement: LRU, marking, Belady’s optimum and the predictive marker, with predictions corrupted by noise of increasing level (ch. 7, 10.4).
    • Data: synthetic request traces with locality (Zipf popularity, working sets that drift).
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk.
    • Success criterion: a curve of faults relative to the optimum against the prediction error, close to 1 for exact predictions and never far above the marking algorithm.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 3 · ≈ 30 h each] Budget pacing under three arrival models (paper study: Mehta, Saberi, Vazirani and Vazirani, 2007; Devanur and Hayes, 2009; Balseiro, Lu and Mirrokni, 2020). Which allocation rule should a platform use when it does not know whether queries are adversarial, randomly ordered or i.i.d.?
    • Data: synthetic queries with heterogeneous bids and budgets, every bid below 1 % of every budget (the small-bids regime of Theorem 6.3), presented in adversarial, random and i.i.d. orders, T up to 10^4.
    • Split: member A implements the rule of Mehta et al. and adversarial instances; member B the sampling-based prices of Devanur and Hayes; member C dual mirror descent and the offline LP benchmark (ch. 6).
    • Deliverables: shared code, one report of 8–10 pages, a 20-minute talk.
    • Success criterion: a table of ratios to the LP bound, three rules by three arrival models, with Mehta et al. above 1 - 1/e everywhere and the learned prices above 0.95 on i.i.d. inputs.
  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] k-server on lines, trees and random trees (paper study: Chrobak, Karloff, Payne and Vishwanathan, 1991; Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou, 1995; Fakcharoenphol, Rao and Talwar, 2004). How far from their worst-case ratios are DC and the work function algorithm, and what does a random tree cost?
    • Data: synthetic request sequences on the line, on random trees and on random point sets in the plane, including adversarial sequences from Theorem 8.2.
    • Split: member A computes the offline optimum by minimum-cost flow and implements DC on lines and trees; member B implements the work function algorithm for k \leq 3 on finite metrics; member C builds random HST embeddings and measures their distortion (ch. 8).
    • Deliverables: shared code, one report of 8–10 pages, a 20-minute talk.
    • Success criterion: on every input, the bound \mathrm{DC} \leq k\, \mathrm{OPT} + \Phi_0 of Theorem 8.3 checked with the initial potential, and ratios below 2k - 1 for the work function algorithm on long sequences. The average distortion of the trees should grow like \log n. Short inputs can exceed k because of the additive constant.
  3. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Chasing convex bodies with the Steiner point (paper study: Sellke, 2020; Argue, Gupta, Guruganesh and Tang, 2020). Does the Steiner point move much less than greedy projection on nested and general convex sequences?
    • Data: synthetic sequences of polytopes in dimensions 2 to 10, nested and non-nested, plus the lower-bound sequences of Proposition 9.2 and Exercise 16.
    • Split: member A estimates Steiner points of polytopes by random directions and linear programs; member B implements the chasing algorithms; member C builds hard instances and the offline optimum as a convex program (ch. 9).
    • Deliverables: shared code, one report of 8–10 pages, a 20-minute talk.
    • Success criterion: a plot of the cost ratio against the dimension for each rule, with greedy failing on the line-chasing instances and the Steiner rule staying bounded.

Written exam (proposal)

Format (proposal): 3 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic device, four exercises.

  1. Question (4 points). Define the competitive ratio of a randomized online algorithm against an oblivious adversary. Explain, on ski rental and on a bandit problem, how it differs from the regret of online learning. State Yao’s principle and say what it is used for.

    Expected: Definition 1.2 with the expectation over the coins and the input fixed in advance; regret compares with the best fixed action and decisions carry no state, while competitive analysis compares with the best sequence and decisions are irrevocable (ch. 1.4); Theorem 1.4, used to prove lower bounds for randomized algorithms.

  2. Computation (6 points). (a) In the secretary problem with n = 6, compute the success probability of every threshold rule and find the best one.

    1. Let X_1 be uniform on [0, 1] and X_2 uniform on [0, 2], independent, observed in this order. Compute the prophet’s gain, the optimal gambler’s gain, and the gain of the threshold T = \mathbb{E}[\max]/2.

    Expected: (a) Theorem 2.5 gives 0.381, 0.428, 0.392, 0.300 and 0.167 for r = 2, \dots, 6, and r = 1 wins with probability 1/6. Reject the first two candidates: success 0.428.

    1. The tail formula gives \mathbb{E}[\max] = 5/6 + 1/4 = 13/12. The gambler stops at the first value only if it exceeds \mathbb{E}[X_2] = 1, so he gets 1, a ratio of 12/13. With T = 13/24, the gain is \frac{1 - T^2}{2} + T\, \frac{4 - T^2}{4} \approx 0.855, a ratio of about 0.79.

  3. Short proof (5 points). Prove that stopping at the first X_i \geq \mathbb{E}[\max_j X_j]/2 secures half of the prophet’s gain for independent non-negative values, and show that no stopping rule secures more than half in general.

    Expected: Theorem 3.2: the split into threshold and excess, independence of the future (Proposition 0.4), the bound of Proposition 0.2; tightness with Proposition 3.3.

  4. Problem (5 points). (a) Prove that no deterministic paging algorithm with a cache of size k is better than k-competitive. (b) Prove that LRU faults at most k times per phase and conclude that it is k-competitive. (c) State, without proof, what randomization changes, and name an algorithm that achieves it.

    Expected: (a) k + 1 pages, the adversary requests the missing page, Belady’s rule faults at most once every k requests. (b) Theorem 7.2, both steps. (c) Theorem 7.3: the marking algorithm is 2H_k-competitive, and H_k is the optimal randomized ratio.
