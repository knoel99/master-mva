A robot is a mechanical system whose state obeys Newton’s laws, whose sensors are noisy, and whose motions must avoid obstacles and respect contact. Each chapter turns one of these constraints into a mathematical problem — a least-squares problem, a graph search, an optimal control problem, a filtering problem, a stochastic optimisation — and into an algorithm that runs on a real robot.

The course leans on Convex Optimization and Applications in Machine Learning, which the official page recommends taking at the same time.   • Solve inverse kinematics by damped least squares, exploit redundancy with null-space projections, and write whole-body control as a quadratic program.
  • Design computed-torque and PD-plus-gravity controllers and prove their stability with a Lyapunov function.

  • Generate a walking pattern with the linear inverted pendulum, the zero-moment point and the capture point.
  • Build a safety filter with a control barrier function, and argue about the responsibilities of robotics research.

0. Review: least squares, rotations, integration and Gaussian conditioning

Four M1 tools carry most of the course. Least squares solves inverse kinematics and estimation problems; quadratic programs with equality constraints encode control; rotations describe every rigid body; numerical integration turns equations of motion into simulations.

This chapter states each tool in the form used later, with short proofs. The last section recalls Gaussian conditioning, on which the Kalman filter of ch. 5 rests.

Least squares and the pseudo-inverse

Robotics constantly solves linear systems that have too many or too few equations. A task in the plane constrains two coordinates of a seven-joint arm; twenty camera measurements constrain six pose parameters. The pseudo-inverse handles both cases at once.

Definition 0.1 — Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} have the singular value decomposition A = U \Sigma V^\top, with singular values \sigma_1 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r > 0. Its pseudo-inverse is A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^\top, where \Sigma^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m} has 1/\sigma_i in position (i, i) for i \leq r and zeros elsewhere.

When A has full row rank, A^+ = A^\top (A A^\top)^{-1}; when it has full column rank, A^+ = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top.

Theorem 0.2 — Minimum-norm least squares For every b \in \mathbb{R}^m, the vector x^\star = A^+ b minimises \|A x - b\|, and among all minimisers it has the smallest norm.

Proof. Write y = V^\top x and c = U^\top b. Orthogonal matrices preserve norms, so \|A x - b\|^2 = \sum_{i \leq r} (\sigma_i y_i - c_i)^2 + \sum_{i > r} c_i^2 .

The second sum does not depend on x. The first one vanishes exactly when y_i = c_i / \sigma_i for i \leq r; the coordinates y_i with i > r are free.

Since \|x\| = \|y\|, the minimiser of smallest norm sets the free coordinates to zero. That vector is y = \Sigma^+ c, that is x = V \Sigma^+ U^\top b = A^+ b. ∎

Near a rank drop, some \sigma_i is tiny and c_i / \sigma_i explodes. For a robot this means huge joint velocities near a singular configuration (ch. 3). The cure is to penalise the norm of the solution.

Proposition 0.3 — Damped least squares For \lambda > 0, the unique minimiser of \|A x - b\|^2 + \lambda \|x\|^2 is x_\lambda = (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1} A^\top b = A^\top (A A^\top + \lambda I)^{-1} b .

In the coordinates of Theorem 0.2, y_i = \sigma_i c_i / (\sigma_i^2 + \lambda), so that every coordinate is bounded by |c_i| / (2 \sqrt{\lambda}).

Proof. The objective is strongly convex; setting its gradient to zero gives the normal equations (A^\top A + \lambda I) x = A^\top b.

The second expression follows from the identity (A^\top A + \lambda I) A^\top = A^\top (A A^\top + \lambda I), multiplied on the left and on the right by the two inverses.

The diagonal form follows by substituting the SVD. Finally \sigma^2 + \lambda \geq 2 \sigma \sqrt{\lambda}, by the inequality between arithmetic and geometric means, which gives the bound. ∎

The second form is the one used in practice: it inverts an m \times m matrix, and m (the task dimension) is usually smaller than n (the number of joints).

Quadratic programs with equality constraints

Whole-body control (ch. 3), the LQR (ch. 4) and walking pattern generation (ch. 7) all reduce to minimising a convex quadratic under linear constraints.

Proposition 0.4 — KKT system Let H \succ 0 and let A \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n} have full row rank. The problem \min_x \ \tfrac12 x^\top H x + g^\top x \quad \text{subject to} \quad A x = b has a unique solution x^\star. With a multiplier \nu^\star, it is the unique solution of the linear system \begin{pmatrix} H & A^\top \\ A & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x^\star \\ \nu^\star \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -g \\ b \end{pmatrix}.

Proof. The feasible set is an affine subspace, non-empty because A is onto, and the objective is strongly convex on it: the minimiser exists and is unique.

At the minimiser, the gradient H x^\star + g is orthogonal to \ker A, hence lies in the range of A^\top: this is the first block row. The second block row is feasibility.

The matrix is invertible. If H x + A^\top \nu = 0 and A x = 0, then x^\top H x = -x^\top A^\top \nu = 0, so x = 0; then A^\top \nu = 0 and \nu = 0 by the rank assumption. ∎

Inequality constraints (torque limits, friction cones, obstacle half-planes) require active-set or interior-point solvers; they are the subject of the Convex Optimization course and of chapter 16 of Nocedal and Wright’s textbook.

Rotations and the exponential map

Definition 0.5 — The rotation group The special orthogonal group is SO(3) = \{ R \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3} : R^\top R = I, \ \det R = 1 \}. For \omega \in \mathbb{R}^3, the hat map gives the skew-symmetric matrix [\omega]_\times such that [\omega]_\times v = \omega \times v for every v.

Differentiating R^\top R = I shows that R^\top \dot R is skew-symmetric. Hence every motion of a rotation obeys \dot R = R\, [\omega]_\times , where \omega is the angular velocity expressed in the moving (body) frame.

With \omega constant, the solution starting at I is the matrix exponential of t\, [\omega]_\times. For skew-symmetric matrices, the exponential has a closed form.

Proposition 0.6 — Rodrigues’ formula For a unit vector u and an angle \theta, \exp(\theta [u]_\times) = I + \sin\theta \, [u]_\times + (1 - \cos\theta) \, [u]_\times^2 . This is the rotation of angle \theta about the axis u.

Proof. For a unit u, one checks that [u]_\times^3 = -[u]_\times; hence [u]_\times^{2k+1} = (-1)^k [u]_\times and [u]_\times^{2k+2} = (-1)^k [u]_\times^2 for k \geq 0.

Splitting the exponential series into odd and even powers gives I + \Big(\sum_{k \geq 0} \tfrac{(-1)^k \theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\Big) [u]_\times + \Big(\sum_{k \geq 0} \tfrac{(-1)^k \theta^{2k+2}}{(2k+2)!}\Big) [u]_\times^2 , and the two series are \sin\theta and 1 - \cos\theta. ∎

Example 0.7 — A quarter turn Take u = e_z and \theta = \pi/2. Then \sin\theta = 1, 1 - \cos\theta = 1, and [e_z]_\times^2 = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -1, 0).

Rodrigues’ formula gives R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, which sends e_x to e_y, as a quarter turn about z should.

Integrating equations of motion

A simulator advances the state (x, v) of a mechanical system with a fixed step h. Two first-order schemes look almost identical:

  • explicit Euler: x_{k+1} = x_k + h v_k and v_{k+1} = v_k + h a(x_k);
  • semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler: first v_{k+1} = v_k + h a(x_k), then x_{k+1} = x_k + h v_{k+1}.

Proposition 0.8 — Energy behaviour on the oscillator Apply both schemes to \ddot x = -x, whose energy \tfrac12 (x^2 + v^2) is constant.

  • Explicit Euler multiplies the energy by exactly 1 + h^2 at every step.
  • Semi-implicit Euler conserves exactly the modified energy x^2 + v^2 - h\, x v, which differs from twice the energy by at most a factor 1 \pm h/2.

Proof. For explicit Euler, the cross terms cancel in x_{k+1}^2 + v_{k+1}^2 = (x_k + h v_k)^2 + (v_k - h x_k)^2 = (1 + h^2)(x_k^2 + v_k^2).

For the semi-implicit scheme, substitute v_{k+1} = v_k - h x_k and x_{k+1} = x_k + h v_{k+1} into the modified energy and expand; all terms in h and h^2 cancel. The comparison follows from |x v| \leq \tfrac12 (x^2 + v^2). ∎

For the semi-implicit scheme with h = 0.1 and (x_0, v_0) = (1, 0), the modified energy equals 1. The energy therefore stays, for ever, in the band 0.476 \leq \tfrac12 (x_k^2 + v_k^2) \leq 0.526 , that is within 5.3 % of its initial value 0.5.

For explicit Euler, 1,000 steps multiply the energy by 1.01^{1000} \approx 2.1 \times 10^4. This is the explosion announced in the introduction; the code below confirms both claims and checks Rodrigues’ formula against a general-purpose matrix exponential.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm

def hat(w):
    """Skew-symmetric matrix [w]x, so that hat(w) @ v == np.cross(w, v)."""
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]])

def rodrigues(w):
    th = np.linalg.norm(w)
    if th < 1e-12:
        return np.eye(3) + hat(w)
    K = hat(w / th)
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(th) * K + (1 - np.cos(th)) * K @ K

w = np.array([0.3, -0.5, 0.8])
R = rodrigues(w)
print("max |Rodrigues - expm| =", np.abs(R - expm(hat(w))).max())
print("R^T R = I:", np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(3)), "| det R =", round(np.linalg.det(R), 12))

# Explicit vs semi-implicit Euler on x'' = -x, step h = 0.1, 1000 steps
h, n = 0.1, 1000
for scheme in ["explicit", "semi-implicit"]:
    x, v = 1.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        if scheme == "explicit":
            x, v = x + h * v, v - h * x
        else:
            v = v - h * x
            x = x + h * v
    print(f"{scheme:>13} Euler: energy after {n} steps = {0.5 * (x**2 + v**2):.4f} (initial 0.5)")

The output gives an energy of about 10,480 for explicit Euler and 0.52 for semi-implicit Euler. Physics engines therefore use semi-implicit schemes, which also extend naturally to contact (ch. 1).

Gaussian conditioning

Proposition 0.9 — Conditioning a Gaussian vector Let (X, Y) be a Gaussian vector with means \mu_X, \mu_Y and covariance blocks \Sigma_{XX}, \Sigma_{XY}, \Sigma_{YY}, with \Sigma_{YY} \succ 0. Given Y = y, the vector X is Gaussian, with \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y] = \mu_X + \Sigma_{XY} \Sigma_{YY}^{-1} (y - \mu_Y), \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(X \mid Y) = \Sigma_{XX} - \Sigma_{XY} \Sigma_{YY}^{-1} \Sigma_{YX}.

Proof sketch. The vector X - \Sigma_{XY} \Sigma_{YY}^{-1} Y is uncorrelated with Y, hence independent of Y since the pair is Gaussian. Writing X as this vector plus a function of Y gives both formulas; see the Probability and Statistics refresher.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The pseudo-inverse gives the minimum-norm least-squares solution; damping bounds it near singularities at the price of a small bias.
  • A strictly convex quadratic under linear equality constraints is solved by one symmetric linear (KKT) system.
  • Rotations form the group SO(3); Rodrigues’ formula is the closed form of its exponential map.
  • For mechanics, prefer semi-implicit Euler to explicit Euler: same cost, bounded energy error.
  • Gaussian conditioning is the algebra behind every Kalman filter.

1. Modelling and simulation of robotic systems

Before planning or controlling a robot, one needs its model: which configurations it can take, where its end effector is for a given configuration, and how torques produce accelerations. This chapter builds that model, from geometry to dynamics, and ends with simulation and contact.

Configuration space and rigid motions

Definition 1.1 — Configuration space, degrees of freedom The configuration of a robot is a minimal set of parameters that fixes the position of all its points; the set \mathcal{Q} of configurations is the configuration space, and its dimension is the number of degrees of freedom.

  • A revolute joint contributes an angle (a circle), a prismatic joint a length (an interval of the line).
  • A serial arm with n revolute joints has \mathcal{Q} = (S^1)^n, a torus of dimension n.
  • A legged robot also has a floating base, whose pose lies in SE(3) (below). A quadruped with 12 joints has 6 + 12 = 18 degrees of freedom, of which only 12 are actuated.

Definition 1.2 — Rigid motions The pose of a frame attached to a rigid body is a rotation R \in SO(3) and a position p \in \mathbb{R}^3, stored in a homogeneous matrix g = \begin{pmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in SE(3), \qquad g^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} R^\top & -R^\top p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

Composition of poses is the matrix product: if g_{ab} is the pose of frame b in frame a, then g_{ac} = g_{ab}\, g_{bc}.

Like SO(3), the group SE(3) has an exponential map. A twist \xi = (v, \omega) \in \mathbb{R}^6 describes an infinitesimal screw motion. Its matrix form is \hat\xi = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega]_\times & v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}, and e^{\hat\xi \theta} \in SE(3) is the rigid motion obtained by following the twist during a time \theta.

For a revolute joint of unit axis \omega through the point r, the twist is \xi = (-\omega \times r, \omega). Then e^{\hat\xi \theta} is the rotation of angle \theta about that axis.

Forward kinematics

Theorem 1.3 — Product of exponentials Let \xi_1, \dots, \xi_n be the twists of the joints of a serial chain, expressed in the base frame at the reference configuration q = 0, and let g(0) be the pose of the end effector there. For every configuration q, g(q) = e^{\hat\xi_1 q_1}\, e^{\hat\xi_2 q_2} \cdots e^{\hat\xi_n q_n}\; g(0).

Proof. Move the joints one at a time, starting from the last. Rotating joint n by q_n, all other joints at zero, moves the end effector rigidly about the fixed axis \xi_n: the pose becomes e^{\hat\xi_n q_n} g(0).

Then rotate joint n-1. It moves rigidly everything beyond it, including the current axis of joint n, about the axis \xi_{n-1}, which is still at its reference place because the joints before it have not moved. The pose becomes e^{\hat\xi_{n-1} q_{n-1}} e^{\hat\xi_n q_n} g(0).

Induction down to joint 1 gives the formula. ∎

The formula, due to Brockett, needs no frame per link; Murray, Li and Sastry (chapter 3) build their whole treatment of manipulators on it. For planar arms, elementary trigonometry gives the same result.

Example 1.4 — The planar two-link arm Links of lengths l_1, l_2, joint angles q_1 (from the horizontal axis) and q_2 (relative). The end effector is at x = l_1 \cos q_1 + l_2 \cos(q_1 + q_2), \qquad y = l_1 \sin q_1 + l_2 \sin(q_1 + q_2).

With l_1 = 1 m, l_2 = 0.8 m and q = (0.4, 1.1) rad: (x, y) = (0.978, \ 1.187) \ \text{m}.

Jacobians, singularities and statics

Definition 1.5 — Kinematic Jacobian If p = f(q) is the position of the end effector, its velocity is \dot p = J(q)\, \dot q, \qquad J(q) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial q}(q).

A configuration where J loses rank is singular: some directions of motion of the end effector cannot be produced, and the inverse problems of ch. 3 become ill-conditioned there.

For the arm of Example 1.4, a direct computation gives \det J(q) = l_1 l_2 \sin q_2 . The arm is singular when it is fully stretched (q_2 = 0) or fully folded (q_2 = \pi). At q = (0.4, 1.1) the determinant is 0.713.

Proposition 1.6 — Statics duality To exert a force f on the environment with its end effector, in static equilibrium and without friction in the joints, the robot must apply the joint torques \tau = J(q)^\top f .

Proof. By the principle of virtual work, the work of the joint torques in a virtual displacement \delta q equals the work of the force in the induced displacement \delta p = J \delta q. Hence \tau^\top \delta q = f^\top J \delta q for every \delta q, which gives \tau = J^\top f. ∎

The same transpose appears in dynamics: contact forces enter the equations of motion through J_c^\top \lambda, where J_c is the Jacobian of the contact point.

Lagrangian dynamics

Let T(q, \dot q) = \tfrac12 \dot q^\top M(q) \dot q be the kinetic energy of the robot and V(q) its potential energy. The matrix M(q) is the mass matrix. For a robot made of point masses m_i located at p_i = f_i(q), it is M(q) = \sum_i m_i\, J_i(q)^\top J_i(q), since the kinetic energy is \sum_i \tfrac12 m_i \|J_i \dot q\|^2. Rigid links add the rotational terms J_{\omega,i}^\top I_i J_{\omega,i} in the same way.

Theorem 1.7 — Equations of motion The Euler–Lagrange equations of the robot read M(q)\, \ddot q + C(q, \dot q)\, \dot q + g(q) = \tau + J_c(q)^\top \lambda , where g = \nabla V, \tau are the joint torques, \lambda the contact forces, and C_{ij} = \sum_k \Gamma_{ijk}\, \dot q_k, \qquad \Gamma_{ijk} = \tfrac12 \Big( \frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial M_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i} \Big).

Proof sketch. Apply the Euler–Lagrange equations with L = T - V: \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = \tau + J_c^\top \lambda . The first term gives M \ddot q + \dot M \dot q. Subtracting \tfrac12 \dot q^\top (\partial_{q_i} M) \dot q and symmetrising the sum over j, k gives the Christoffel symbols \Gamma_{ijk}. The generalised forces of the contacts follow from Proposition 1.6.

Key result — the equation of every robot M(q)\, \ddot q + C(q, \dot q)\, \dot q + g(q) = \tau + J_c(q)^\top \lambda

Proposition 1.8 — Skew-symmetry and energy balance With the Christoffel choice of C, the matrix \dot M - 2C is skew-symmetric. Consequently, without contact, the total energy E = T + V obeys \dot E = \dot q^\top \tau .

Proof. Since \dot M_{ij} = \sum_k (\partial_{q_k} M_{ij})\, \dot q_k, the definition of \Gamma gives (\dot M - 2C)_{ij} = \sum_k \Big( \frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i} - \frac{\partial M_{ik}}{\partial q_j} \Big) \dot q_k , which changes sign when i and j are exchanged.

For the energy, \dot E = \dot q^\top M \ddot q + \tfrac12 \dot q^\top \dot M \dot q + \dot q^\top g . Substituting M \ddot q = \tau - C \dot q - g leaves \dot q^\top \tau + \tfrac12 \dot q^\top (\dot M - 2C) \dot q, and the last term vanishes by skew-symmetry. ∎

Proposition 1.8 says that the joints inject exactly the power \dot q^\top \tau. It is the key step of the stability proofs of ch. 3, and a sharp test for a simulator: with \tau = 0 the energy must stay constant.

Rigid-body algorithms and libraries

Forming M(q) and C(q, \dot q) symbolically is hopeless beyond a few joints. Three recursive algorithms over the kinematic tree, collected in Featherstone’s monograph, do the work instead:

  • the recursive Newton–Euler algorithm computes the inverse dynamics \tau = \mathrm{ID}(q, \dot q, \ddot q) in O(n) operations — a forward pass for velocities and accelerations, a backward pass for forces;
  • the composite rigid-body algorithm computes M(q) in O(n^2);
  • the articulated-body algorithm computes the forward dynamics \ddot q in O(n), without forming or inverting M.

The open-source library Pinocchio (Carpentier and coauthors, 2019) implements these algorithms together with their analytical derivatives, which ch. 4 needs for trajectory optimisation. The MuJoCo simulator (Todorov, Erez and Tassa, 2012) adds contact. Both have Python bindings and are the natural tools for the projects.

The official course includes practical exercises with such state-of-the-art libraries. In Pinocchio’s Python interface, pinocchio.buildModelFromUrdf loads a robot description and model.createData() creates a workspace. The three algorithms above are then the functions rnea, crba and aba, which take the model, the workspace and the joint vectors. MuJoCo loads a model with mujoco.MjModel.from_xml_path and advances it by one time step with mujoco.mj_step. The code of this page re-implements the same computations in NumPy on small examples, so that every block runs without these libraries.

Contact and simulation

Walking and grasping are made of contacts that open and close. A contact is unilateral: the bodies cannot interpenetrate, and the ground can push but not pull.

Definition 1.9 — Unilateral contact with Coulomb friction Let \phi(q) be the signed distance between two bodies and \lambda = (\lambda_n, \lambda_t) the normal and tangential contact forces. The contact is described by the complementarity condition 0 \leq \phi(q) \ \perp \ \lambda_n \geq 0 , meaning that both are non-negative and at least one is zero. Coulomb’s law adds the friction cone \|\lambda_t\| \leq \mu\, \lambda_n , and when the contact slides, the bound is reached and the tangential force opposes the sliding velocity.

The conditions are non-smooth: velocities jump at impacts. Time-stepping schemes (Stewart and Trinkle, 1996) therefore work with impulses over one step. At each step they solve a complementarity problem for the contact impulses, then update the velocities and the positions in the semi-implicit order of ch. 0. MuJoCo relaxes this problem into a convex one, which makes it fast and always solvable, at the price of small artificial penetrations.

Without contact, any good integrator works. The code below takes the two-link arm of Example 1.4 with point masses at the elbow and at the tip, and checks the Jacobian against finite differences. It builds M(q) = \sum_i m_i J_i^\top J_i, lets the arm fall freely for 5 s, and monitors the energy (Proposition 1.8).

import numpy as np

l1, l2, m1, m2, g = 1.0, 0.8, 1.0, 0.5, 9.81   # lengths (m), point masses (kg)

def fk(q):
    """Position of the end effector (angles measured from the x axis)."""
    return np.array([l1 * np.cos(q[0]) + l2 * np.cos(q[0] + q[1]),
                     l1 * np.sin(q[0]) + l2 * np.sin(q[0] + q[1])])

def jacobian(q):
    s1, c1 = np.sin(q[0]), np.cos(q[0])
    s12, c12 = np.sin(q[0] + q[1]), np.cos(q[0] + q[1])
    return np.array([[-l1 * s1 - l2 * s12, -l2 * s12],
                     [ l1 * c1 + l2 * c12,  l2 * c12]])

q = np.array([0.4, 1.1])
eps = 1e-6
J_fd = np.column_stack([(fk(q + eps * e) - fk(q - eps * e)) / (2 * eps) for e in np.eye(2)])
print("max |J - J_fd| =", f"{np.abs(jacobian(q) - J_fd).max():.1e}",
      "| det J =", round(np.linalg.det(jacobian(q)), 6), "| l1 l2 sin q2 =", round(l1 * l2 * np.sin(q[1]), 6))

def mass_matrix(q):
    J1 = np.array([[-l1 * np.sin(q[0]), 0.0], [l1 * np.cos(q[0]), 0.0]])  # elbow mass
    return m1 * J1.T @ J1 + m2 * jacobian(q).T @ jacobian(q)            # M = sum m_i J_i^T J_i

def bias(q, dq):
    """Coriolis, centrifugal and gravity terms h(q, dq)."""
    k = m2 * l1 * l2 * np.sin(q[1])
    cor = np.array([-k * (2 * dq[0] * dq[1] + dq[1] ** 2), k * dq[0] ** 2])
    grav = np.array([(m1 + m2) * g * l1 * np.cos(q[0]) + m2 * g * l2 * np.cos(q[0] + q[1]),
                     m2 * g * l2 * np.cos(q[0] + q[1])])
    return cor + grav

def energy(q, dq):
    return 0.5 * dq @ mass_matrix(q) @ dq + m1 * g * l1 * np.sin(q[0]) + m2 * g * fk(q)[1]

# Passive motion (tau = 0) during 5 s: semi-implicit Euler vs Runge-Kutta 4
def rhs(s):
    return np.concatenate([s[2:], np.linalg.solve(mass_matrix(s[:2]), -bias(s[:2], s[2:]))])

s0 = np.array([0.4, 1.1, 0.0, 0.0])
E0 = energy(s0[:2], s0[2:])
for h in [1e-3, 1e-4]:
    s, dev = s0.copy(), 0.0
    for _ in range(round(5 / h)):
        s[2:] += h * rhs(s)[2:]          # velocity first ...
        s[:2] += h * s[2:]               # ... then position with the new velocity
        dev = max(dev, abs(energy(s[:2], s[2:]) - E0) / E0)
    print(f"semi-implicit Euler, h = {h:g} s: max relative energy error {dev:.1e}")
s, h = s0.copy(), 1e-3
for _ in range(5000):
    k1 = rhs(s); k2 = rhs(s + h / 2 * k1); k3 = rhs(s + h / 2 * k2); k4 = rhs(s + h * k3)
    s = s + h / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)
print(f"Runge-Kutta 4,       h = 0.001 s: relative energy error {abs(energy(s[:2], s[2:]) - E0) / E0:.1e}")

The finite differences agree with the analytic Jacobian to about 10^{-10}. The semi-implicit scheme keeps the energy within 3.6 % at h = 1 ms and within 0.35 % at h = 0.1 ms: the error is of first order, but bounded.

Runge–Kutta 4 conserves the energy to 2 \times 10^{-10}, which confirms that the Coriolis terms are right. Yet contact simulators keep first-order schemes, because high-order schemes lose their accuracy at every impact.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • A robot lives in a configuration space; a floating base adds six unactuated degrees of freedom.
  • Forward kinematics is a product of exponentials of joint twists; its Jacobian maps joint velocities to end-effector velocities, and its transpose maps forces to torques.
  • The dynamics always has the form M \ddot q + C \dot q + g = \tau + J_c^\top \lambda, with M \succ 0 and \dot M - 2C skew-symmetric.
  • Recursive algorithms compute dynamics in linear time; Pinocchio and MuJoCo implement them.
  • Contact is a complementarity condition with a friction cone; simulators handle it with semi-implicit time stepping.

2. Motion planning

Motion planning finds a collision-free motion from a start configuration to a goal configuration. The key idea is to plan in the configuration space of ch. 1, where the robot is a point, rather than in the workspace, where it is a complicated body.

The planning problem

Definition 2.1 — Free space and path Let \mathcal{A}(q) be the set of points occupied by the robot at configuration q, and \mathcal{O} the obstacles. The obstacle region and the free space are \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{obs}} = \{ q : \mathcal{A}(q) \cap \mathcal{O} \neq \emptyset \}, \qquad \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}} = \mathcal{Q} \setminus \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{obs}} .

A path is a continuous map \sigma : [0, 1] \to \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}} with \sigma(0) = q_s and \sigma(1) = q_g. A planner is complete if it returns a path whenever one exists and reports failure otherwise.

Complete planning is hard. Reif (1979) proved that the generalised “mover’s problem” is PSPACE-hard, and the exact algorithms that exist are exponential in the number of degrees of freedom. Practical planners give up exact completeness: they discretise the space (resolution completeness) or sample it (probabilistic completeness).

Search on a graph: A*

On a grid or a roadmap, planning is a shortest-path problem on a graph with non-negative edge costs c(u, v). Dijkstra’s algorithm expands vertices by increasing cost-to-come g(v); A* (Hart, Nilsson and Raphael, 1968) expands them by g(v) + h(v), where the heuristic h estimates the cost-to-go.

Theorem 2.2 — Optimality of A* Suppose the heuristic is consistent: h(v_{\mathrm{goal}}) = 0 and h(u) \leq c(u, v) + h(v) \quad \text{for every edge } (u, v). Then A* expands every vertex at most once and returns a shortest path.

Proof. Define the reduced costs c'(u, v) = c(u, v) - h(u) + h(v), which are non-negative by consistency. Along any path from v_s to v, the reduced costs telescope: \sum c' = \sum c + h(v) - h(v_s).

So A* with costs c is exactly Dijkstra’s algorithm with costs c': the priorities differ by the constant h(v_s). Dijkstra with non-negative costs settles each vertex once, at its shortest reduced distance.

All paths from v_s to the goal have their cost shifted by the same constant -h(v_s), because h(v_{\mathrm{goal}}) = 0. Shortest paths for c' are therefore shortest paths for c. ∎

The Euclidean distance to the goal is a consistent heuristic on any geometric graph, by the triangle inequality. Grids, however, scale as (1/\delta)^d with the resolution \delta and the dimension d: a seven-joint arm needs another idea.

Sampling-based planning: PRM and RRT

The probabilistic roadmap (PRM; Kavraki, Švestka, Latombe and Overmars, 1996) samples N configurations uniformly in \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}}. It connects every pair at distance at most r whose straight segment is collision-free, and answers queries by graph search. Only two primitives are needed: a collision test for a configuration and one for a segment.

Theorem 2.3 — Failure probability of the PRM In \mathcal{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}^d, suppose a path of length L from q_s to q_g has clearance r: the ball of radius r around each of its points is free.

Let \mu denote volume and set m = \lceil 2L / r \rceil.

A PRM with N uniform samples and connection radius r, with q_s and q_g added to the roadmap, connects them except with probability at most m \Big( 1 - \frac{\mu(B_{r/4})}{\mu(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}})} \Big)^N \ \leq \ m \exp\Big( - N \frac{\mu(B_{r/4})}{\mu(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}})} \Big).

Proof. Place points y_0 = q_s, y_1, \dots, y_m = q_g along the path, at arc-length spacing at most L/m \leq r/2.

Suppose each ball B(y_i, r/4) with 0 < i < m contains a sample x_i, and set x_0 = q_s, x_m = q_g.

  • Consecutive samples are close: \|x_i - x_{i+1}\| \leq r/4 + r/2 + r/4 = r, so the PRM tries to connect them.
  • Both endpoints lie in the ball B(y_i, 3r/4), which is convex, so the whole segment does too. That ball lies in B(y_i, r) \subset \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}}: the segment is collision-free.

So failure requires that one of the m - 1 balls receives no sample. A given ball is missed by all N independent samples with probability \big(1 - \mu(B_{r/4}) / \mu(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}})\big)^N , and the union bound concludes. ∎

Example 2.4 — How many samples? In the unit square with \mu(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}}) = 0.8, take a path of length L = 1.7 and clearance r = 0.1. Then m = 34, and each ball of radius 0.025 has relative volume p = \pi (0.025)^2 / 0.8 \approx 0.00245.

The bound is 34\, (1 - p)^N:

  • about 0.25 for N = 2{,}000;
  • about 1.6 \times 10^{-4} for N = 5{,}000.

The failure probability decays exponentially in N, but the rate depends on the clearance: narrow passages are the enemy of sampling.

The rapidly-exploring random tree (RRT; LaValle, 1998; LaValle and Kuffner, 2001) grows a single tree from q_s. At each iteration it samples q_{\mathrm{rand}} and finds the nearest tree vertex q_{\mathrm{near}}. It moves from q_{\mathrm{near}} towards q_{\mathrm{rand}} by at most a step \eta, and adds the new vertex if the segment is free.

Large Voronoi cells attract samples, so the tree rushes into unexplored regions. RRT is probabilistically complete but not optimal. Karaman and Frazzoli (2011) proved that, with probability one, its solution does not converge to an optimal path. Their RRT* rewires the tree within a radius shrinking like (\log n / n)^{1/d}, and is asymptotically optimal.

From paths to trajectories

A sampled path zigzags. Shortcutting replaces a sub-path by a straight segment whenever that segment is free. Then a time scaling s(t) turns the geometric path \sigma(s) into a trajectory \sigma(s(t)) that respects velocity and torque limits.

For a rest-to-rest motion, the cubic scaling s(t) = 3 (t/T)^2 - 2 (t/T)^3 has peak speed 1.5 / T at mid-course. On a path of length L with a speed limit v_{\max}, it needs T \geq 1.5\, L / v_{\max}.

Time-optimal scaling under torque limits is a classical problem (Bobrow, Dubowsky and Gibson, 1985). Planning directly with dynamics — kinodynamic planning — merges with the optimal control of ch. 4. Artificial potential fields (Khatib, 1986) are a reactive alternative, fast but trapped by local minima.

The code below runs an RRT in a square with three discs. The straight line is blocked, and the gaps between the discs are narrower than the step, so the tree must go around. Shortcutting then shortens the path.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
obstacles = [((0.5, 0.5), 0.2), ((0.25, 0.75), 0.15), ((0.75, 0.25), 0.15)]  # (centre, radius)
start, goal = np.array([0.05, 0.05]), np.array([0.95, 0.95])

def free(p):
    return all(np.linalg.norm(p - np.array(c)) > r for c, r in obstacles)

def segment_free(a, b, step=0.005):
    n = max(2, int(np.linalg.norm(b - a) / step) + 1)
    return all(free(a + t * (b - a)) for t in np.linspace(0, 1, n))

def rrt(eta=0.05, goal_bias=0.05, max_iter=5000):
    nodes, parent = [start], [-1]
    for it in range(max_iter):
        x_rand = goal if rng.random() < goal_bias else rng.random(2)
        i_near = int(np.argmin([np.linalg.norm(x_rand - v) for v in nodes]))
        d = x_rand - nodes[i_near]
        x_new = nodes[i_near] + d * min(1.0, eta / (np.linalg.norm(d) + 1e-12))
        if not segment_free(nodes[i_near], x_new):
            continue
        nodes.append(x_new); parent.append(i_near)
        if np.linalg.norm(x_new - goal) < eta and segment_free(x_new, goal):
            nodes.append(goal); parent.append(len(nodes) - 2)
            path, i = [], len(nodes) - 1
            while i >= 0:
                path.append(nodes[i]); i = parent[i]
            return path[::-1], it + 1, len(nodes)
    return None, max_iter, len(nodes)

def shortcut(path):
    """Greedy shortcutting: jump to the farthest vertex visible from the current one."""
    out, i = [path[0]], 0
    while i < len(path) - 1:
        j = len(path) - 1
        while j > i + 1 and not segment_free(path[i], path[j]):
            j -= 1
        out.append(path[j]); i = j
    return out

length = lambda p: sum(np.linalg.norm(b - a) for a, b in zip(p, p[1:]))
path, iters, n_nodes = rrt()
short = shortcut(path)
print(f"RRT: {iters} iterations, {n_nodes} nodes, path of {len(path)} vertices, length {length(path):.3f}")
print(f"after shortcutting: {len(short)} vertices, length {length(short):.3f}")
print(f"straight line {np.linalg.norm(goal - start):.3f} (blocked by the central disc: {not segment_free(start, goal)})")

With this seed, the tree reaches the goal after 500 iterations with 206 vertices. Its path has length 2.22; shortcutting reduces it to 1.71 with a single intermediate vertex, against 1.27 for the blocked straight line.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Plan in configuration space, where the robot is a point and obstacles are the region \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{obs}}.
  • A* with a consistent heuristic is Dijkstra on reduced costs, hence optimal.
  • Sampling-based planners need only collision tests; the PRM fails with a probability that decays exponentially in the number of samples, at a rate set by the clearance.
  • RRT explores fast but is not optimal; RRT* is asymptotically optimal.
  • Shortcutting and time scaling turn a path into an executable trajectory.

3. Inverse problems for robot control

A task is specified for the end effector — reach this point, follow this line, push with this force — but the robot is commanded through its joints. Each level of control is therefore an inverse problem: positions (inverse geometry), velocities (differential kinematics), accelerations and torques (inverse dynamics).

Inverse geometry

Example 3.1 — Closed-form inverse kinematics of the two-link arm For a target (x, y) of Example 1.4, the law of cosines gives \cos q_2 = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2}{2 l_1 l_2}, \qquad q_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \operatorname{atan2}(l_2 \sin q_2, \ l_1 + l_2 \cos q_2).

  • For the target (0.978, 1.187), \cos q_2 \approx 0.453, so q_2 \approx \pm 1.10: two solutions, elbow up and elbow down.
  • When |\cos q_2| > 1 the target is out of reach, and when |\cos q_2| = 1 the two solutions merge at a singularity.

Closed forms exist only for special geometries. In general, inverse geometry is posed as nonlinear least squares, with e(q) = x^\star - f(q): \min_q \ \tfrac12 \| x^\star - f(q) \|^2 .

Linearising f at the current iterate and solving the damped problem of Proposition 0.3 gives the Levenberg–Marquardt step \Delta q = J^\top (J J^\top + \lambda I)^{-1} e . The damping is decreased after a successful step and increased after a failed one.

Proposition 3.2 — The damped step is a descent direction If J^\top e \neq 0 and \lambda > 0, the Levenberg–Marquardt step decreases the objective for a small enough step length.

Proof. By Proposition 0.3, \Delta q = (J^\top J + \lambda I)^{-1} J^\top e. The gradient of the objective is -J^\top e, and \langle -J^\top e, \Delta q \rangle = -(J^\top e)^\top (J^\top J + \lambda I)^{-1} (J^\top e) < 0, because the matrix in the middle is positive definite. ∎

A large \lambda gives a short gradient step; a small one gives the fast Gauss–Newton step. Damped least squares in inverse kinematics was introduced by Wampler (1986) and by Nakamura and Hanafusa (1986).

Differential kinematics and redundancy

When the robot has more joints than the task has dimensions (n > m), the task does not determine the joint velocities.

Proposition 3.3 — All solutions and the null-space projector Let J \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} have full row rank. The solutions of J \dot q = v are exactly \dot q = J^+ v + (I - J^+ J)\, z, \qquad z \in \mathbb{R}^n . J^+ v is the solution of smallest norm, and P = I - J^+ J is the orthogonal projector onto \ker J.

Proof. With J^+ = J^\top (J J^\top)^{-1}, one has J J^+ = I, so J^+ v is a solution and J P = J - J = 0.

P is symmetric, and P^2 = P because J^+ J J^+ = J^+.

Its range is \ker J: it lies in \ker J since J P = 0, and P z = z whenever J z = 0.

Any solution differs from J^+ v by an element of \ker J, that is by some P z.

Finally J^+ v lies in the range of J^\top, orthogonal to \ker J, so adding P z can only increase the norm. ∎

The free vector z serves a secondary objective: avoid joint limits, stay away from singularities, keep a posture. The task-priority scheme (Nakamura, Hanafusa and Yoshikawa, 1987; Siciliano and Slotine, 1991) projects a second task J_2 \dot q = v_2 into the null space of the first: \dot q = J_1^+ v_1 + (J_2 P_1)^+ \big( v_2 - J_2 J_1^+ v_1 \big).

Integrating velocities lets the error drift. Closed-loop inverse kinematics adds a feedback term on the task error.

Proposition 3.4 — Closed-loop inverse kinematics Let x^\star(t) be a desired trajectory, e = x^\star - f(q), and k > 0. If J(q) keeps full row rank along the motion, the control \dot q = J^+ (\dot x^\star + k\, e) gives \dot e = -k e, hence e(t) = e^{-kt} e(0).

Proof. Since J J^+ = I, \dot e = \dot x^\star - J \dot q = \dot x^\star - J J^+ (\dot x^\star + k e) = -k e . ∎

The code below solves inverse geometry for a three-link planar arm, first for a reachable target, then for an unreachable one. It then solves the reachable task again with a null-space term z = -q that pulls the joints towards zero.

import numpy as np

L = np.array([1.0, 0.8, 0.5])            # three-link planar arm, reach 2.3 m

def fk(q):
    a = np.cumsum(q)                      # absolute link angles
    return np.array([L @ np.cos(a), L @ np.sin(a)])

def jac(q):
    a = np.cumsum(q)
    return np.array([[-L[j:] @ np.sin(a[j:]) for j in range(3)],
                     [ L[j:] @ np.cos(a[j:]) for j in range(3)]])

def ik_lm(x_star, q0, lam=1e-2, tol=1e-12, max_iter=200):
    """Levenberg-Marquardt on 1/2 |x* - fk(q)|^2, damping adapted to progress."""
    q = q0.copy()
    for k in range(max_iter):
        e = x_star - fk(q)
        if e @ e < tol:
            break
        J = jac(q)
        q_try = q + J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + lam * np.eye(2), e)
        if np.linalg.norm(x_star - fk(q_try)) < np.linalg.norm(e):
            q, lam = q_try, lam / 2          # success: trust the Gauss-Newton model more
        else:
            lam = lam * 10                   # failure: shorter, gradient-like step
    return q, k

q0, x_star = np.array([0.3, 0.3, 0.3]), np.array([1.2, 1.0])
q, k = ik_lm(x_star, q0)
print(f"reachable target: {k} iterations, q = {np.round(q, 4)}, error {np.linalg.norm(x_star - fk(q)):.1e}")
q_lm = q
far = np.array([3.0, 0.0])
q, k = ik_lm(far, q0)
print(f"unreachable target: q = {np.round(q, 4) + 0.0}, residual {np.linalg.norm(far - fk(q)):.4f} (= 3.0 - 2.3)")

# Redundancy: same target, secondary task "stay close to q = 0" projected in the null space
q = q0.copy()
for _ in range(400):
    J = jac(q); Jp = np.linalg.pinv(J)
    dq = Jp @ (2.0 * (x_star - fk(q))) + (np.eye(3) - Jp @ J) @ (-q)
    q = q + 0.05 * dq
print(f"with a null-space task: q = {np.round(q, 4)}, |q| = {np.linalg.norm(q):.4f} "
      f"(Levenberg-Marquardt: {np.linalg.norm(q_lm):.4f}), error {np.linalg.norm(x_star - fk(q)):.1e}")

The reachable target is met in 6 iterations. For the unreachable one, the method converges to the stretched arm pointing at the target, at the best possible distance 0.7 m, instead of diverging. The null-space term reaches the same target with a smaller joint norm (1.513 against 1.530): the secondary task acts without disturbing the primary one.

Inverse dynamics and torque control

Kinematic control assumes that the joints follow their commands. Fast or heavy robots need torque control, which inverts the dynamics of Theorem 1.7. Write h(q, \dot q) = C(q, \dot q) \dot q + g(q).

Proposition 3.5 — Computed torque Let q_d(t) be a twice differentiable reference, e = q_d - q, and K_p, K_d diagonal positive definite. The control \tau = M(q) \big( \ddot q_d + K_d \dot e + K_p e \big) + h(q, \dot q) gives the linear error dynamics \ddot e + K_d \dot e + K_p e = 0, which converge exponentially to zero.

Proof. Substituting \tau into M \ddot q + h = \tau and cancelling h gives M (\ddot q_d - \ddot q + K_d \dot e + K_p e) = 0. Since M \succ 0, the bracket vanishes; it is the stated equation, whose characteristic roots have negative real parts. ∎

Computed torque needs an exact model. A much simpler law needs only gravity.

Theorem 3.6 — PD plus gravity compensation (Takegaki and Arimoto, 1981) For a constant target q_d and K_p, K_d \succ 0, the control \tau = K_p (q_d - q) - K_d \dot q + g(q) makes (q, \dot q) = (q_d, 0) globally asymptotically stable.

Proof. Take V = \tfrac12 \dot q^\top M \dot q + \tfrac12 e^\top K_p e with e = q_d - q, so that \dot e = -\dot q. Then \dot V = \dot q^\top M \ddot q + \tfrac12 \dot q^\top \dot M \dot q - \dot q^\top K_p e .

Substitute M \ddot q = \tau - C \dot q - g = K_p e - K_d \dot q - C \dot q. By the skew-symmetry of Proposition 1.8, \dot V = -\dot q^\top K_d \dot q \leq 0 .

LaSalle’s invariance principle applies, V being radially unbounded. On the set where \dot V = 0 we have \dot q \equiv 0, hence \ddot q = 0 and 0 = K_p e, so e = 0. The largest invariant set is the target. ∎

Task-space inverse dynamics as a quadratic program

A humanoid must track several tasks while respecting its dynamics, its contacts and its torque limits. Modern controllers solve, at every control period (typically 1 ms), a quadratic program in (\ddot q, \tau, \lambda): \begin{aligned} \min_{\ddot q, \tau, \lambda} \ & \| J_t \ddot q + \dot J_t \dot q - \ddot x_t^\star \|^2 + \epsilon \|\ddot q\|^2 \\ \text{s.t.} \ & M \ddot q + h = S^\top \tau + J_c^\top \lambda, \quad J_c \ddot q + \dot J_c \dot q = 0, \\ & \lambda \in \text{linearised friction cones}, \quad \tau_{\min} \leq \tau \leq \tau_{\max} . \end{aligned}

Here S selects the actuated joints (the floating base has no motor), and the second equality keeps the contact points fixed. The reference \ddot x_t^\star comes from a PD law on the task, as in Proposition 3.5.

With equality constraints only, Proposition 0.4 solves the problem with one KKT system. With strict priorities between tasks, a hierarchy of such programs is solved, efficiently, by the hierarchical quadratic programming of Escande, Mansard and Wieber (2014).

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Inverse geometry is nonlinear least squares; Levenberg–Marquardt steps are descent directions and stay bounded near singularities.
  • Redundancy is handled by the null-space projector I - J^+ J; priorities stack tasks without disturbing the first.
  • Closed-loop inverse kinematics makes the task error decay exponentially.
  • Computed torque linearises the dynamics exactly; PD plus gravity is globally stable thanks to the skew-symmetry of \dot M - 2C.
  • Whole-body control is a quadratic program over accelerations, torques and contact forces, solved at every control period.

4. Optimal control and trajectory optimisation

Inverse kinematics and computed torque track a motion that someone has designed. Optimal control designs the motion itself: among all control signals that respect the dynamics, it picks the one that minimises a cost — effort, time, distance to a goal.

Dynamic programming

Definition 4.1 — Discrete-time optimal control problem Given dynamics x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t), a running cost \ell and a terminal cost \ell_N, find controls u_0, \dots, u_{N-1} that minimise J = \sum_{t=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_t, u_t) + \ell_N(x_N) from a given initial state x_0.

Theorem 4.2 — Bellman’s principle Define the value functions backwards by V_N = \ell_N and V_t(x) = \min_u \ \big[ \ell(x, u) + V_{t+1}(f(x, u)) \big]. Then V_0(x_0) is the optimal cost, and choosing at each time a minimiser u_t = \pi_t(x_t) is optimal.

Proof sketch. By backward induction, V_t(x) is the optimal cost-to-go from state x at time t. The tail of an optimal trajectory must be optimal for the tail problem; otherwise replacing it would lower the total cost.

The theorem returns a feedback law, not just a trajectory, but it requires a minimisation over u for every state: tabulating V_t is impossible beyond a few dimensions. One structure escapes this curse exactly.

The linear-quadratic regulator

Theorem 4.3 — Discrete-time LQR Let f(x, u) = A x + B u, \ell(x, u) = x^\top Q x + u^\top R u and \ell_N(x) = x^\top Q_N x, with Q, Q_N \succeq 0 and R \succ 0.

Then V_t(x) = x^\top P_t x, the optimal control is the linear feedback u_t = -K_t x_t, and with P_N = Q_N: K_t = (R + B^\top P_{t+1} B)^{-1} B^\top P_{t+1} A, \qquad P_t = Q + A^\top P_{t+1} (A - B K_t).

Proof. By induction, assume V_{t+1}(x) = x^\top P_{t+1} x with P_{t+1} \succeq 0. The Bellman minimand x^\top Q x + u^\top R u + (A x + B u)^\top P_{t+1} (A x + B u) is strictly convex in u, since R + B^\top P_{t+1} B \succ 0.

Setting its gradient in u to zero gives u = -K_t x. Substituting back, the value is x^\top P_t x with P_t as stated, and P_t \succeq 0 as a minimum of non-negative quantities. ∎

Over a long horizon, P_t converges to the solution of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation when (A, B) is stabilisable and (A, Q^{1/2}) detectable. The stationary gain then stabilises the system.

Example 4.4 — A scalar LQR Take a = 1, b = 0.5, q = 1, r = 0.1. The stationary Riccati equation p = q + a^2 p - (a b p)^2 / (r + b^2 p) reduces to p^2 - p - 0.4 = 0, \qquad p = \frac{1 + \sqrt{2.6}}{2} \approx 1.306 .

  • The gain is K = a b p / (r + b^2 p) \approx 1.531.
  • The closed loop x_{t+1} = (a - b K) x_t has pole 0.234: the error shrinks by a factor of about four at each step.

Chapter 6 will rediscover this gain by trial and error.

Pontryagin’s maximum principle

In continuous time, with \dot x = f(x, u) and cost \int_0^T \ell(x, u)\, dt + \ell_T(x(T)), the necessary conditions take the form of a two-point boundary value problem. Define the Hamiltonian H(x, u, p) = \ell(x, u) + p^\top f(x, u).

Theorem 4.5 — Maximum principle (minimum form) Assume f and \ell are continuously differentiable and the final time T is fixed. If u^\star is optimal, with trajectory x^\star, there is a costate p(t) such that, in the normal case, \dot x^\star = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}, and for almost every t, u^\star(t) minimises H(x^\star(t), \cdot, p(t)) over the admissible controls. Boundary conditions complete the system: a fixed final state, or the transversality condition p(T) = \nabla \ell_T(x^\star(T)) when it is free.

In general the Hamiltonian is p_0 \ell + p^\top f with a constant p_0 \geq 0, and (p_0, p) never vanishes. The normal case is p_0 = 1; it always holds when the final state is free. With a fixed final state, an abnormal extremal (p_0 = 0) can occur, and the cost then plays no role in the conditions.

The proof, by needle variations, is out of scope; see chapter 4 of Liberzon (2011).

Example 4.6 — Minimum-energy transfer of a double integrator Move a unit mass from rest at 0 to rest at 1 in time 1, with \ddot x = u and cost \int_0^1 u^2 dt.

  1. With state (x, v), the Hamiltonian is u^2 + p_1 v + p_2 u.
  2. The problem is normal: with p_0 = 0, minimising p_2 u over all real u would force p_2 \equiv 0, then p_1 = 0.
  3. The costate equations are \dot p_1 = 0 and \dot p_2 = -p_1, so p_2 is affine in t.
  4. Minimising H gives u = -p_2 / 2, so u(t) = a + b t.
  5. The four boundary conditions give a = 6 and b = -12.

The optimal control and its cost are u(t) = 6 - 12 t, \qquad \int_0^1 u^2\, dt = 12 .

Direct methods and differential dynamic programming

Robots have nonlinear dynamics, constraints and dozens of states. Practical solvers discretise first and optimise afterwards:

  • single shooting optimises the controls only, the states being obtained by simulation; simple, but badly conditioned for unstable systems;
  • multiple shooting and collocation also treat the states as variables, linked by the dynamics as equality constraints. The resulting large, sparse nonlinear program is solved by sequential quadratic programming or interior points.

Differential dynamic programming (DDP; Mayne, 1966) applies Bellman’s principle locally around a nominal trajectory (\bar x_t, \bar u_t). Its iterative-LQR variant (iLQR; Li and Todorov, 2004) linearises the dynamics, \delta x_{t+1} \approx A_t \delta x_t + B_t \delta u_t, and expands the cost to second order. It then solves the resulting LQ problem by the backward recursion Q_u = \ell_u + B_t^\top V'_x, \quad Q_{uu} = \ell_{uu} + B_t^\top V'_{xx} B_t, \quad Q_{ux} = \ell_{ux} + B_t^\top V'_{xx} A_t, where V' is the quadratic model of the value at time t + 1. The step is \delta u_t = k_t + K_t \delta x_t, with k_t = -Q_{uu}^{-1} Q_u and K_t = -Q_{uu}^{-1} Q_{ux}.

A forward simulation with a line search on k_t produces the next nominal trajectory. DDP also keeps the second derivatives of f, which iLQR drops. The Crocoddyl library (Mastalli and coauthors, 2020) implements DDP for multi-contact robots on top of Pinocchio.

The code computes the LQR gain of a double integrator by the Riccati recursion and checks it against SciPy. It then swings up a damped pendulum with iLQR, from rest hanging down to the upright position in 5 s.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_are

# (a) LQR for the double integrator, state (position, velocity), period dt = 0.1 s
dt = 0.1
A = np.array([[1.0, dt], [0.0, 1.0]]); B = np.array([[dt**2 / 2], [dt]])
Q, R = np.diag([1.0, 0.1]), np.array([[0.01]])
P = Q.copy()
for _ in range(500):                                   # Riccati recursion, backwards in time
    K = np.linalg.solve(R + B.T @ P @ B, B.T @ P @ A)
    P = Q + A.T @ P @ (A - B @ K)
P_are = solve_discrete_are(A, B, Q, R)
print("K from the recursion:", np.round(K, 4), "| max |P - P_are| =", f"{np.abs(P - P_are).max():.1e}")
print("closed-loop eigenvalue moduli:", np.round(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(A - B @ K)), 4))

# (b) iLQR swing-up of a damped pendulum (theta = 0 hanging down, target theta = pi)
m, l, g, b, h, N = 1.0, 1.0, 9.81, 0.1, 0.05, 100      # 100 steps of 50 ms
x_goal, r, w_T = np.array([np.pi, 0.0]), 1e-2, 1e3

def f(x, u):                                           # explicit Euler discretisation
    return np.array([x[0] + h * x[1], x[1] + h * (-g / l * np.sin(x[0]) - b * x[1] + u / (m * l**2))])

def f_x(x):
    return np.array([[1.0, h], [-h * g / l * np.cos(x[0]), 1.0 - h * b]])

f_u = np.array([0.0, h / (m * l**2)])

def cost(X, U):
    e = X[-1] - x_goal
    return r * U @ U + w_T * e @ e

U = np.zeros(N)
X = np.zeros((N + 1, 2))
for t in range(N):
    X[t + 1] = f(X[t], U[t])
J = cost(X, U)
for it in range(200):
    Vx, Vxx = 2 * w_T * (X[-1] - x_goal), 2 * w_T * np.eye(2)    # backward pass
    k_ff, K_fb = np.zeros(N), np.zeros((N, 2))
    for t in reversed(range(N)):
        At = f_x(X[t])
        Qx, Qu = At.T @ Vx, 2 * r * U[t] + f_u @ Vx
        Qxx, Quu, Qux = At.T @ Vxx @ At, 2 * r + f_u @ Vxx @ f_u, f_u @ Vxx @ At
        k_ff[t], K_fb[t] = -Qu / Quu, -Qux / Quu
        Vx = Qx + Qux * k_ff[t]                                   # value of the optimal step
        Vxx = Qxx - np.outer(Qux, Qux) / Quu
    for alpha in [1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.1, 0.03, 0.01]:               # forward pass + line search
        Xn, Un = np.zeros_like(X), np.zeros(N)
        Xn[0] = X[0]
        for t in range(N):
            Un[t] = U[t] + alpha * k_ff[t] + K_fb[t] @ (Xn[t] - X[t])
            Xn[t + 1] = f(Xn[t], Un[t])
        if cost(Xn, Un) < J:
            break
    if J - cost(Xn, Un) < 1e-9 * J:
        break
    X, U, J = Xn, Un, cost(Xn, Un)
print(f"iLQR: {it} iterations, cost {J:.3f}, final state {np.round(X[-1], 4)}, max |u| = {np.abs(U).max():.2f} N m")

The recursion matches SciPy to 10^{-13} and both closed-loop poles have modulus 0.76. iLQR converges in 46 iterations to a final angle of 3.1405 rad with a peak torque of 2.66 N m. That is about a quarter of the torque m g l = 9.81 N m needed to hold the pendulum horizontal, so the optimiser has discovered that it must pump energy by swinging.

Model predictive control

An open-loop trajectory fails at the first disturbance. Model predictive control (MPC) closes the loop by re-optimising.

Definition 4.7 — Receding horizon At each control period, measure the state x, solve the optimal control problem of horizon N from x, apply only the first control u_0^\star(x), and repeat at the next period.

Proposition 4.8 — Stability with a terminal cost (sketch) Suppose the terminal cost V_f admits a local feedback \kappa_f such that, on a terminal set \mathbb{X}_f that \kappa_f keeps invariant, V_f\big(f(x, \kappa_f(x))\big) - V_f(x) \leq -\ell\big(x, \kappa_f(x)\big), and suppose the MPC problem includes the constraint x_N \in \mathbb{X}_f. Then the optimal value V_N^\star decreases along the closed loop by at least \ell(x, u_0^\star), and is a Lyapunov function.

Proof. At the next state x^+, the shifted sequence u_1^\star, \dots, u_{N-1}^\star followed by \kappa_f(x_N^\star) is feasible. Its cost is V_N^\star(x) - \ell(x, u_0^\star), plus the terminal difference of the assumption, which is non-positive. The optimum at x^+ is no larger. ∎

This argument, surveyed by Mayne, Rawlings, Rao and Scokaert (2000), underlies the walking controllers of ch. 7, which re-solve a small MPC problem at every control period.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Bellman’s principle gives optimal feedback by backward recursion; it is tractable exactly in the linear-quadratic case.
  • The LQR gain comes from a Riccati recursion; the stationary gain stabilises the system.
  • Pontryagin’s principle gives necessary conditions as a boundary-value problem with a costate.
  • Direct methods (shooting, collocation) and DDP/iLQR solve nonlinear trajectory optimisation; iLQR is a sequence of local LQR problems.
  • MPC re-optimises at every period; a terminal cost and set make its value a Lyapunov function.

5. Perception and estimation

A controller needs the state of the robot — its pose, its velocity, the position of the objects around it — but sensors give only noisy, partial measurements. Estimation fuses a model of the motion with these measurements; perception extracts geometric information from cameras and range sensors.

Sensors and the filtering problem

Robots measure themselves with joint encoders, force sensors and inertial measurement units (gyroscopes and accelerometers). They measure the world with cameras, depth cameras and LiDAR. Both kinds of measurement are summarised by a state-space model: x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k) + w_k, \qquad y_k = h(x_k) + v_k , with process noise w_k and measurement noise v_k.

The Bayes filter propagates the distribution of x_k given y_{0:k} in two steps. The prediction integrates the motion model against the previous posterior; the update multiplies by the likelihood p(y_{k+1} \mid x_{k+1}) and renormalises. Two cases are tractable: linear-Gaussian models (Kalman filter) and low dimensions, or many particles (particle filters, see Computational Statistics).

The Kalman filter

Theorem 5.1 — Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960) Let f(x, u) = F x + G u and h(x) = H x, with independent Gaussian noises of covariances Q and R and a Gaussian prior.

The posterior of x_k given y_{0:k} is Gaussian, \mathcal{N}(m_k, P_k), computed by the following recursion.

  • Predict: m^- = F m_{k-1} + G u_{k-1} and P^- = F P_{k-1} F^\top + Q.
  • Update: with the innovation covariance S = H P^- H^\top + R and the gain K = P^- H^\top S^{-1}, m_k = m^- + K (y_k - H m^-), \qquad P_k = (I - K H) P^- .

Proof. By induction, suppose the posterior at time k - 1 is \mathcal{N}(m_{k-1}, P_{k-1}). The state x_k is an affine function of x_{k-1} plus independent Gaussian noise: given y_{0:k-1}, it is Gaussian with the predicted moments.

The pair (x_k, y_k) is then jointly Gaussian given y_{0:k-1}, with \mathrm{Cov}(x_k, y_k) = P^- H^\top and \mathrm{Cov}(y_k) = S. Gaussian conditioning (Proposition 0.9) gives exactly the update formulas. ∎

Example 5.2 — One update by hand A scalar position has prior \mathcal{N}(0, 4), and a sensor of noise variance 1 returns y = 2.

  • The gain is K = 4 / (4 + 1) = 0.8.
  • The posterior mean is 0 + 0.8 \times 2 = 1.6, and the posterior variance (1 - 0.8) \times 4 = 0.8.

The estimate moves most of the way towards the precise sensor, and its variance is smaller than both the prior and the sensor variances.

The Riccati recursion of the covariance is the time-reversed twin of the LQR recursion of ch. 4: estimation and control are dual problems, as Kalman already noted.

Nonlinear models: the EKF and observability

The extended Kalman filter (EKF) applies the same recursion to the linearisations F_k = \partial f / \partial x and H_k = \partial h / \partial x at the current estimate. It is the workhorse of robot navigation, but it has no optimality guarantee. Linearising at a wrong estimate can make it inconsistent, that is, overconfident.

Before filtering, one should ask whether the measurements determine the state at all.

Proposition 5.3 — Observability rank condition The linear system (F, H) with state dimension n is observable — the initial state is determined by noise-free outputs — if and only if the matrix stacking H, HF, \dots, HF^{n-1} has rank n.

Example. For the constant-velocity model with position measurements, H = (1, 0) and HF = (1, \Delta t): rank 2, so the velocity is observable although never measured. With velocity measurements only, the rank is 1 and the position is not observable.

Estimation on Lie groups: invariant errors

Orientation lives in SO(3), not in a vector space, and the EKF’s additive error R - \hat R is not a rotation. Bonnabel, Martin and Rouchon (2008) proposed instead errors defined with the group operation. Their behaviour can be remarkable.

Proposition 5.4 — An autonomous error for attitude Let the true attitude obey \dot R = R [\omega]_\times, where \omega is measured by a perfect gyroscope, and propagate the estimate with the same equation, \dot{\hat R} = \hat R [\omega]_\times. Then the invariant error \eta = R \hat R^\top is constant, whatever the motion \omega(t).

Proof. Since [\omega]_\times^\top = -[\omega]_\times, \dot \eta = R [\omega]_\times \hat R^\top + R [\omega]_\times^\top \hat R^\top = 0 . ∎

The additive error, by contrast, rotates with \omega(t), and a linearisation of it depends on the trajectory. Barrau and Bonnabel (2017) generalised the observation to group-affine systems, a class that includes inertial navigation. For these systems, the invariant error obeys an equation that does not depend on the estimate, and its logarithm obeys an exactly linear equation.

The resulting invariant EKF inherits convergence properties of the linear Kalman filter that the standard EKF lacks. Hartley and coauthors (2020) built a contact-aided invariant EKF for legged robots on this basis.

Batch estimation, SLAM and registration

Filtering processes measurements once. Batch estimation computes the maximum a posteriori trajectory given all measurements, as a nonlinear least-squares problem: \min_{x_{0:K}} \ \| x_0 - m_0 \|^2_{P_0^{-1}} + \sum_k \| x_{k+1} - f(x_k, u_k) \|^2_{Q^{-1}} + \sum_k \| y_k - h(x_k) \|^2_{R^{-1}} , where \mathcal{N}(m_0, P_0) is the prior on the initial state.

Gauss–Newton solves it with sparse linear algebra, because each term involves one or two states. In the linear-Gaussian case, the solution coincides with the Kalman smoother. When the landmarks of a map join the unknowns, the problem becomes simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM). Factor graphs (Dellaert and Kaess, 2017) represent the sparsity; Barfoot’s textbook develops the whole approach.

Cameras enter through the pinhole model. A point (X, Y, Z) in the camera frame projects to the pixel (f X / Z + c_x, \ f Y / Z + c_y), and minimising reprojection errors is bundle adjustment; see 3D Computer Vision. Depth sensors give 3D points directly, and the basic problem becomes aligning two point clouds.

Theorem 5.5 — Rigid registration (Kabsch–Umeyama) Given pairs (p_i, q_i), i = 1, \dots, n, with centroids \bar p, \bar q, let W = \sum_i (q_i - \bar q)(p_i - \bar p)^\top = U S V^\top , an SVD with singular values s_1 \geq s_2 \geq s_3 \geq 0. The minimiser of \sum_i \|q_i - R p_i - t\|^2 over R \in SO(3) and t \in \mathbb{R}^3 is R^\star = U\, \mathrm{diag}\big(1, 1, \det(U V^\top)\big)\, V^\top, \qquad t^\star = \bar q - R^\star \bar p .

Proof. For fixed R, the optimal t is the least-squares fit of the residual means, t = \bar q - R \bar p. With centred points \tilde p_i, \tilde q_i, the cost becomes a constant minus 2 \sum_i \tilde q_i^\top R \tilde p_i, and this sum equals \mathrm{tr}(R^\top W).

Write Z = U^\top R V, which is orthogonal. Since |Z_{ii}| \leq 1, \mathrm{tr}(R^\top W) = \mathrm{tr}(Z^\top S) = \sum_i s_i Z_{ii} \leq \sum_i s_i . Equality holds at Z = I, that is R = U V^\top, which is a rotation when \det(U V^\top) = 1.

Otherwise the constraint \det R = 1 forces \det Z = -1. The best choice flips the sign of the smallest singular direction, Z = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1); see Umeyama (1991) for the details. ∎

When correspondences are unknown, the iterative closest point algorithm (Besl and McKay, 1992) alternates nearest-neighbour matching and Theorem 5.5. Each step decreases the cost, so ICP converges, but only to a local minimum: it needs a good initial guess.

The code runs a Kalman filter on a constant-velocity target observed in position only, then registers two noisy point clouds.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial.transform import Rotation

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
# (a) Kalman filter: constant-velocity target, only the position is measured
dt, n, sa, sr = 0.1, 200, 0.5, 1.0          # period, steps, acceleration std, measurement std

Qn = sa**2 * np.array([[dt**4 / 4, dt**3 / 2], [dt**3 / 2, dt**2]])
Rn = np.array([[sr**2]])
x, xs, ys = np.array([0.0, 1.0]), [], []
for _ in range(n):
    x = F @ x + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(2), Qn)
    xs.append(x); ys.append(H @ x + sr * rng.standard_normal(1))
xs, ys = np.array(xs), np.array(ys)

m, P, est = np.zeros(2), 10.0 * np.eye(2), []
for y in ys:
    m, P = F @ m, F @ P @ F.T + Qn                        # predict
    S = H @ P @ H.T + Rn
    K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)                        # Kalman gain
    m, P = m + K @ (y - H @ m), (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ P   # update
    est.append(m)
est = np.array(est)
rmse = lambda u, v: np.sqrt(np.mean((u - v) ** 2))
print(f"position RMSE: raw measurements {rmse(ys[:, 0], xs[:, 0]):.3f}, Kalman {rmse(est[:, 0], xs[:, 0]):.3f}")
print(f"velocity RMSE (never measured): {rmse(est[:, 1], xs[:, 1]):.3f}; final gain K = {np.round(K.ravel(), 3)}")

# (b) Registration of two point sets with known correspondences (Theorem 5.5)
R_true = Rotation.from_rotvec([0.2, -0.4, 0.9]).as_matrix()
t_true = np.array([0.5, -1.0, 2.0])
p = rng.standard_normal((100, 3))
q = p @ R_true.T + t_true + 0.01 * rng.standard_normal((100, 3))
pc, qc = p.mean(0), q.mean(0)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd((q - qc).T @ (p - pc))           # W = sum (q_i - qc)(p_i - pc)^T
D = np.diag([1.0, 1.0, np.sign(np.linalg.det(U @ Vt))])
R_hat = U @ D @ Vt
t_hat = qc - R_hat @ pc
angle = np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R_hat.T @ R_true) - 1) / 2, -1, 1)))
print(f"rotation error {angle:.3f} deg, translation error {np.linalg.norm(t_hat - t_true):.4f}")

The filter divides the position error by almost three (0.35 against 0.98 for the raw measurements). It also estimates the velocity, which is never measured, with an error of 0.27, as Proposition 5.3 promised. The registration recovers the rotation to 0.05 degree and the translation to 2 mm.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Estimation fuses a motion model and measurements; the Bayes filter alternates prediction and update.
  • For linear-Gaussian models, the Kalman filter is exact: it is Gaussian conditioning, step after step, and it is dual to the LQR.
  • The EKF linearises; check observability before trusting any filter.
  • Errors defined with the group operation can be independent of the trajectory: the basis of the invariant EKF.
  • Batch estimation and SLAM are sparse nonlinear least squares; rigid registration has a closed form by SVD, iterated in ICP.

6. Reinforcement learning for robotics

Optimal control needs a model of the dynamics and of the cost. Reinforcement learning (RL) optimises a policy from trials instead — in simulation, most of the time. It has produced the most robust legged controllers of recent years; this chapter gives its mathematical core and the conditions under which it transfers to real robots.

Markov decision processes and optimal control

Definition 6.1 — Markov decision process A Markov decision process consists of states x, actions u, a transition kernel p(x' \mid x, u), a cost c(x, u) and a horizon (finite, or infinite with a discount factor \gamma \in (0, 1)).

A stochastic policy \pi_\theta(u \mid x) induces a distribution over trajectories \tau = (x_0, u_0, x_1, \dots), and the objective is J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} \Big[ \sum_t \gamma^t c(x_t, u_t) \Big].

With a known model, this is the optimal control problem of ch. 4, and Bellman’s recursion applies with an expectation over x'. RL methods differ in what they learn: value functions (Q-learning), policies (policy gradient), or both (actor–critic). The Reinforcement Learning course of semester 2 develops them in depth. Here, the policy-gradient family matters most, because robot actions are continuous.

The policy-gradient theorem

Theorem 6.2 — Likelihood-ratio policy gradient (Williams, 1992) For a finite horizon T and G_t = \sum_{s \geq t} c(x_s, u_s) the cost-to-go from time t, \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(u_t \mid x_t) \, \big( G_t - b(x_t) \big) \Big] for any baseline b that depends on the state only.

Proof. The trajectory density is p_\theta(\tau) = p(x_0) \prod_t \pi_\theta(u_t \mid x_t)\, p(x_{t+1} \mid x_t, u_t). Only the policy factors depend on \theta, so \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(u_t \mid x_t), and the unknown dynamics disappear. Differentiating J = \int p_\theta(\tau) C(\tau)\, d\tau under the integral and using \nabla p = p \nabla \log p gives the formula with C(\tau) in place of G_t - b.

Causality: a cost c(x_s, u_s) with s < t is a function of the history up to x_t. Given that history, u_t has density \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_t), so \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla \log \pi_\theta(u_t \mid x_t) \mid x_t\big] = \nabla \int \pi_\theta(u \mid x_t)\, du = 0 . So past costs can be dropped, leaving G_t; the same identity shows that b(x_t) adds zero in expectation. ∎

The estimator only needs to sample trajectories and differentiate the log-policy: no model, no derivative of the dynamics. Its variance, however, is large; the baseline reduces it without bias.

Example 6.3 — A linear Gaussian policy on the scalar system of Example 4.4 Take u = -\theta x + \sigma \varepsilon with \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). Then \log \pi_\theta(u \mid x) = -\frac{(u + \theta x)^2}{2 \sigma^2} + \text{const}, \qquad \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta = -\frac{\varepsilon\, x}{\sigma} .

The code below runs this REINFORCE estimator, with the time-wise average of G_t as baseline, on x_{t+1} = x_t + 0.5 u_t with cost x^2 + 0.1 u^2 over 30 steps. It compares the learned gain with the LQR gain of Example 4.4.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a, b, qc, rc, T = 1.0, 0.5, 1.0, 0.1, 30       # x' = a x + b u, cost q x^2 + r u^2, horizon T

p = qc                                          # stationary scalar Riccati equation (ch. 4)
for _ in range(1000):
    p = qc + a * a * p - (a * b * p) ** 2 / (rc + b * b * p)
K_lqr = a * b * p / (rc + b * b * p)

def rollouts(theta, sigma, n):
    """n episodes of the Gaussian policy u = -theta x + sigma eps; returns states, noises, costs."""
    x = rng.standard_normal(n)
    X, E, C = np.zeros((T, n)), np.zeros((T, n)), np.zeros((T, n))
    for t in range(T):
        eps = rng.standard_normal(n)
        u = -theta * x + sigma * eps
        X[t], E[t], C[t] = x, eps, qc * x**2 + rc * u**2
        x = a * x + b * u
    return X, E, C

theta, sigma, lr = 0.0, 0.5, 0.003
for it in range(501):
    X, E, C = rollouts(theta, sigma, 500)
    G = np.cumsum(C[::-1], axis=0)[::-1]              # cost-to-go from each time step
    G = G - G.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)             # baseline: average cost-to-go at time t
    score = -E * X / sigma                            # d/dtheta log pi(u_t | x_t)
    grad = np.mean(np.sum(score * G, axis=0))         # REINFORCE estimate of dJ/dtheta
    theta -= lr * grad
    if it % 100 == 0:
        print(f"iteration {it:3d}: theta = {theta:.3f}, average cost {C.sum(0).mean():.2f}")
print(f"LQR gain K = {K_lqr:.3f}")

After 501 iterations of 500 episodes each, the learned gain is 1.533, against 1.531 for the LQR. RL has rediscovered the optimal controller, but it used about 250,000 episodes where the Riccati equation needed one line. That ratio is the price of ignoring the model, and the reason why robot RL is done in simulation.

Actor–critic methods: PPO and SAC

Practical algorithms replace G_t - b(x_t) by an estimate of the advantage A(x_t, u_t) = Q(x_t, u_t) - V(x_t), given by a learned critic. Proximal policy optimisation (PPO; Schulman and coauthors, 2017) maximises a clipped surrogate objective over a batch collected with the previous policy: \mathbb{E}\Big[ \min\big( \rho_t A_t, \ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) A_t \big) \Big], \qquad \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(u_t \mid x_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(u_t \mid x_t)}, written for rewards (negative costs). The clipping keeps the new policy close to the one that collected the data. Soft actor–critic (SAC; Haarnoja and coauthors, 2018) adds an entropy bonus to the reward and learns off-policy from a replay buffer, which saves samples.

From simulation to real robots

A policy trained in simulation meets a different robot: other masses, friction, motor dynamics, delays and sensor noise. How much does a model error cost?

Proposition 6.4 — Simulation lemma Let a fixed policy induce transition matrices P (real robot) and \hat P (simulator) on a finite state space, with costs in [0, c_{\max}] and discount \gamma. Suppose that for every state x \|P(\cdot \mid x) - \hat P(\cdot \mid x)\|_1 \leq \varepsilon . Then the values satisfy \| V - \hat V \|_\infty \leq \frac{\gamma\, \varepsilon\, c_{\max}}{(1 - \gamma)^2} .

Proof. The values solve V = c + \gamma P V and \hat V = c + \gamma \hat P \hat V. Subtracting, (I - \gamma P)(V - \hat V) = \gamma (P - \hat P) \hat V .

Since \|\hat V\|_\infty \leq c_{\max} / (1 - \gamma), the right-hand side has sup norm at most \gamma \varepsilon c_{\max} / (1 - \gamma). Since P is stochastic, (I - \gamma P)^{-1} = \sum_k \gamma^k P^k has sup-norm at most 1 / (1 - \gamma). ∎

The error is amplified by the square of the effective horizon 1 / (1 - \gamma): small per-step errors add up along long trajectories. Three remedies dominate practice.

  • Domain randomisation (Tobin and coauthors, 2017) trains on a distribution of simulators — random masses, frictions, delays — so that the real robot looks like one more sample.
  • Better simulators, such as a learned model of the actuators (Hwangbo and coauthors, 2019).
  • Privileged learning: a teacher policy trained with simulator-only information is distilled into a student that sees only onboard sensors (Lee and coauthors, 2020). Miki and coauthors (2022) extended this approach to perceptive locomotion, and their quadruped completed a hike in the mountains.

Learning from demonstrations

When a human can demonstrate the task, behaviour cloning fits the policy by supervised learning on the demonstrated state–action pairs. Its weakness is distribution shift: a small error leads to states never demonstrated, where errors grow.

Ross and Bagnell (2010) showed that the cost of behaviour cloning can grow quadratically with the horizon. The DAgger algorithm (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011) queries the expert on the states visited by the learned policy, and recovers a linear growth.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • RL solves the optimal control problem from samples, without a model of the dynamics.
  • The policy gradient is an expectation of score times cost-to-go; baselines reduce its variance without bias.
  • On a linear-quadratic problem, policy gradient recovers the LQR gain — with orders of magnitude more data.
  • PPO and SAC are the standard actor–critic algorithms for continuous control.
  • Model errors are amplified by the square of the horizon (simulation lemma); domain randomisation, better simulators and privileged teachers close the gap.

7. Legged locomotion

A legged robot is not attached to the ground. It can move its body only by pushing on the ground through its feet, and the ground can only push. This chapter turns that observation into the zero-moment point, the linear inverted pendulum and the capture point. It then generates walking motions with the MPC of ch. 4.

Floating base and centroidal dynamics

Write the configuration as q = (q_b, q_j), with the floating-base pose q_b \in SE(3) and the joint angles q_j. In the dynamics of Theorem 1.7, the torque vector is S^\top \tau, where S selects the actuated joints: the first six equations, those of the base, contain no motor torque at all.

Proposition 7.1 — Centroidal dynamics Let m be the total mass, c the centre of mass (CoM) and L the angular momentum about c.

Let f_i be the contact forces, applied at the points p_i. With gravity -g e_z, m \ddot c = \sum_i f_i - m g\, e_z, \qquad \dot L = \sum_i (p_i - c) \times f_i .

Proof. Sum Newton’s and Euler’s laws over all bodies. Internal forces, including those produced by the motors, come in action–reaction pairs with the same line of action. They cancel in the total force and in the total moment, and only the external forces remain: gravity, whose moment about c vanishes, and the contact forces. ∎

Without contact, the CoM follows a parabola, whatever the joints do. Walking is therefore the art of choosing contact forces — and the ground constrains them.

The zero-moment point and the support polygon

Definition 7.2 — Centre of pressure (zero-moment point) On flat ground, with contact points p_i and normal forces f_{i,z} \geq 0, not all zero, the centre of pressure is the point of the ground z = \frac{\sum_i f_{i,z}\, p_i}{\sum_i f_{i,z}} . At this point, the contact forces produce no moment about horizontal axes; this is why it is also called the zero-moment point (ZMP), after Vukobratović.

Proposition 7.3 — The ZMP stays in the support polygon The ZMP lies in the convex hull of the contact points, called the support polygon.

Proof. The weights f_{i,z} / \sum_j f_{j,z} are non-negative and sum to one, so z is a convex combination of the p_i. ∎

The moment balance of Proposition 7.1, taken about z instead of c, relates the ZMP to the motion of the CoM. With L constant and the CoM at constant height h, it gives, for the horizontal coordinates, z = c - \frac{h}{g}\, \ddot c . Balance is then a constraint on the CoM acceleration: c - (h/g) \ddot c must stay inside the support polygon, or the foot starts to tip over its edge.

The linear inverted pendulum and the capture point

Key result — the linear inverted pendulum (Kajita and coauthors, 2001) \ddot c = \omega^2 (c - z), \qquad \omega = \sqrt{g / h}

This is the previous relation solved for \ddot c. The CoM is repelled by the ZMP: if z stays fixed, the solution c(t) - z = (c_0 - z) \cosh \omega t + \frac{\dot c_0}{\omega} \sinh \omega t diverges exponentially unless the initial conditions are just right.

Definition 7.4 — Divergent component of motion, capture point The divergent component of motion is \xi = c + \frac{\dot c}{\omega} . In the ground plane, the point \xi is called the capture point (Pratt, Carff, Drakunov and Goswami, 2006).

Proposition 7.5 — Stability splits into a stable and an unstable part Along the linear inverted pendulum, \dot \xi = \omega (\xi - z), \qquad \dot c = -\omega (c - \xi).

With z held constant, c comes to rest above z if and only if z = \xi(0). To stop after a push, the robot must therefore place its ZMP — in practice, its next foot — on the capture point.

Proof. Differentiate, using the pendulum equation: \dot \xi = \dot c + \ddot c / \omega = \dot c + \omega (c - z) = \omega (\xi - z). The second equation is the definition of \xi rearranged.

With z constant, \xi(t) - z = e^{\omega t} (\xi(0) - z), which stays bounded only if \xi(0) = z.

In that case \xi \equiv z, and the stable equation \dot c = -\omega (c - z) brings c to z. ∎

Example 7.6 — How far to step after a push With h = 0.8 m, \omega = \sqrt{9.81 / 0.8} \approx 3.50 s^{-1}. A robot standing still is pushed to \dot c = 0.5 m/s.

  • Its capture point is \xi - c = 0.5 / 3.50 \approx 0.143 m ahead of the CoM.
  • If the foot extends 0.1 m ahead of the ankle, the ZMP cannot reach \xi: the robot must step.
  • The divergence time constant is 1/\omega \approx 0.29 s: the gap \xi - z grows by a factor e every 0.29 s, which sets the urgency of the step.

Proposition 7.5 also explains why walking needs preview. The only bounded solution of the unstable equation is \xi(t) = \omega \int_t^{\infty} e^{-\omega (s - t)} z(s)\, ds , so the current state must anticipate the future ZMP, that is the future footsteps.

Walking pattern generation by MPC

Kajita and coauthors (2003) generated walking by preview control of the ZMP; Wieber (2006) recast it as linear MPC. Discretise the CoM with period T, state \hat x = (c, \dot c, \ddot c) and the jerk as control. Over a horizon of N periods, the future ZMPs are affine in the jerks U: Z = P_x \hat x + P_u U .

The pattern generator minimises a weighted sum of \|Z - Z_{\mathrm{ref}}\|^2, where Z_{\mathrm{ref}} follows the planned footsteps, and of \|U\|^2. Without inequality constraints, the minimiser is explicit; with the support polygons as constraints, it is a small QP.

Herdt and coauthors (2010) made the footstep positions decision variables of the same QP. Only the first jerk is applied, and the problem is re-solved at the next period (Definition 4.7). The code below generates a lateral sway for steps alternating between y = \pm 0.1 m every 0.8 s.

import numpy as np

g, hc, T, N = 9.81, 0.8, 0.1, 16          # gravity, CoM height (m), period (s), horizon (1.6 s)
omega = np.sqrt(g / hc)
# Lateral CoM state (y, y', y''), input = jerk; ZMP z = y - (hc/g) y''
A = np.array([[1, T, T**2 / 2], [0, 1, T], [0, 0, 1]])
B = np.array([T**3 / 6, T**2 / 2, T])
C = np.array([1.0, 0.0, -hc / g])
Px = np.array([C @ np.linalg.matrix_power(A, i + 1) for i in range(N)])
Pu = np.zeros((N, N))
for i in range(N):
    for j in range(i + 1):
        Pu[i, j] = C @ np.linalg.matrix_power(A, i - j) @ B

# Footsteps alternate left/right every 0.8 s (y = +-0.1 m); foot half-width 0.05 m
steps = 120
z_ref = np.where((np.arange(steps + N) // 8) % 2 == 0, 0.1, -0.1)   # 8 periods = 0.8 s
z_ref[:8] = 0.0                            # start in double support, between the feet
R_jerk = 1e-6
Hq = Pu.T @ Pu + R_jerk * np.eye(N)        # least squares in the jerks (Wieber, 2006)

x, zs, ys = np.zeros(3), [], []
for k in range(steps):
    jerks = np.linalg.solve(Hq, Pu.T @ (z_ref[k + 1:k + 1 + N] - Px @ x))
    x = A @ x + B * jerks[0]               # receding horizon: apply the first jerk only
    zs.append(C @ x); ys.append(x[0])
zs, ys = np.array(zs), np.array(ys)
dev = np.abs(zs[8:] - z_ref[1:steps + 1][8:])
print(f"omega = {omega:.3f} 1/s; max |ZMP - foot centre| = {dev.max():.3f} m (foot half-width 0.05 m)")
print(f"CoM lateral sway: {ys[40:].min():+.3f} m to {ys[40:].max():+.3f} m, while the feet are at +-0.100 m")
xi = 0.0 + 0.5 / omega                     # capture point after a 0.5 m/s push from rest
print(f"capture point after a 0.5 m/s push: {xi:.3f} m ahead of the CoM")

The ZMP stays within 7 mm of the foot centres, well inside the 5 cm half-width of the feet. The CoM sways between -0.053 m and +0.053 m: it never goes above the feet, and it starts moving towards the next foot before the step, as the preview formula requires.

Whole-body control and learned locomotion

On a real humanoid, the layers of this course stack up:

  • a footstep planner, often a graph search or an optimisation (ch. 2);
  • a CoM pattern generator by MPC, as above;
  • a whole-body QP that tracks the CoM and foot tasks under the full dynamics, contact and torque limits (ch. 3), at about 1 kHz;
  • a state estimator that fuses joint encoders, inertial measurements and contact information (ch. 5).

The control of the Atlas humanoid described by Kuindersma and coauthors (2016) follows this optimisation-based scheme; Wensing and coauthors (2024) survey it. The alternative of ch. 6 trains a single neural policy by RL in simulation, from proprioception and sometimes perception to joint targets. Combining the guarantees of the first approach with the robustness of the second is an open question (ch. 9).

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Only contact forces move the CoM; the centroidal equations make this precise.
  • The ZMP is a convex combination of the contact points, so it stays in the support polygon.
  • Under constant height and angular momentum, the CoM follows the linear inverted pendulum; its divergent component must be steered by the ZMP.
  • To stop, step on the capture point c + \dot c / \omega; to walk, preview the future ZMP — by MPC.
  • Full controllers stack planning, MPC, whole-body QP and estimation; RL policies are the main alternative.

8. Responsible robotics

The last lecture of the official programme steps back from algorithms. It asks what responsibilities researchers and engineers take on when they build machines that act in the physical world. This chapter gives one mathematical tool, one statistical tool, and a framework for the questions that have no formula.

What is specific to robots

Many questions of AI ethics apply to robots, but several take a sharper form:

  • physical harm: a wrong output is a collision, not a wrong label, and failures can injure;
  • diluted responsibility: when a learned controller fails, responsibility is spread between the designer, the data, the integrator and the operator;
  • sensing: cameras and microphones in homes, hospitals and streets record people who never consented;
  • dual use: the controller that lets a quadruped climb rubble after an earthquake works just as well on a military platform; lethal autonomous weapons are debated within the United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons;
  • work: automation transforms jobs, and who benefits from the productivity gains is a political question;
  • environment: robots contain batteries, motors and electronics, with their mining and recycling problems, and training large policies consumes energy.

Safety by design and control barrier functions

Industrial practice relies on standards: ISO 10218 for industrial robots, ISO/TS 15066 for collaborative robots that share space with humans, and ISO 13482 for personal care robots. In the European Union, the Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 and the AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, add legal obligations; the Regulating AI course studies them.

Standards ask for risk analysis and protective measures. Control theory offers one precise kind of protective measure: a filter that keeps the state in a safe set, whatever the nominal controller — hand-designed, optimised or learned — asks for.

Definition 8.1 — Control barrier function Consider \dot x = f(x) + g(x) u and a safe set \mathcal{S} = \{ x : h(x) \geq 0 \}, with h continuously differentiable.

The function h is a control barrier function with rate \alpha > 0 if, for every x, some admissible u satisfies \nabla h(x)^\top \big( f(x) + g(x) u \big) \geq -\alpha\, h(x).

Theorem 8.2 — Forward invariance (Ames and coauthors, 2017) If a locally Lipschitz feedback u(x) satisfies the inequality of Definition 8.1 at every state, then every trajectory that starts in \mathcal{S} stays in \mathcal{S}.

Proof. Along a trajectory, \dot h \geq -\alpha h, so \frac{d}{dt} \big( e^{\alpha t} h(x(t)) \big) = e^{\alpha t} (\dot h + \alpha h) \geq 0 . Hence h(x(t)) \geq e^{-\alpha t}\, h(x(0)) \geq 0 . ∎

The safety filter chooses the admissible input closest to the nominal one: u^\star(x) = \arg\min_u \ \|u - u_{\mathrm{nom}}(x)\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \nabla h^\top (f + g u) \geq -\alpha h . With one constraint, this QP is the projection of u_{\mathrm{nom}} onto a half-space, which has a closed form (Exercise 12).

The code applies the filter to a robot \dot x = u in the plane, driven to a goal by a proportional controller, past a disc of radius 0.5 that lies across the straight path.

import numpy as np

# Single integrator x' = u in the plane; the nominal controller drives the robot to the goal
c_obs, r_obs = np.array([0.0, 0.1]), 0.5          # disc to avoid, slightly off the straight line
goal, k_nom, dt = np.array([2.0, 0.0]), 1.0, 0.01

def h(x):                                          # barrier: h >= 0 outside the disc
    return (x - c_obs) @ (x - c_obs) - r_obs**2

def safety_filter(x, u_nom, alpha):
    """argmin |u - u_nom|^2 s.t. grad h(x) . u >= -alpha h(x): one half-plane, closed form."""
    a = 2 * (x - c_obs)
    slack = a @ u_nom + alpha * h(x)
    return u_nom if slack >= 0 else u_nom - slack * a / (a @ a)

for alpha in [None, 1.0, 5.0]:                     # None = no filter
    x, h_min, active = np.array([-2.0, 0.0]), np.inf, 0
    for _ in range(1500):                          # 15 s, explicit Euler
        u_nom = -k_nom * (x - goal)
        u = u_nom if alpha is None else safety_filter(x, u_nom, alpha)
        active += int(np.any(u != u_nom))
        x = x + dt * u
        h_min = min(h_min, h(x))
    print(f"alpha = {alpha}: min h = {h_min:+.3f}, final distance to goal "
          f"{np.linalg.norm(x - goal):.1e}, filter active during {active * dt:.2f} s")

Without the filter, the robot crosses the disc (h reaches -0.24). With the filter, h stays positive and the goal is still reached.

A small rate \alpha = 1 keeps a wide margin (h \geq 0.48); a larger rate \alpha = 5 lets the robot graze the obstacle (h \geq 0.016).

The guarantee is only as good as the model. Unmodelled dynamics, delays and estimation errors (ch. 5) erode it, and a robot can also get stuck in front of an obstacle. Robust and learned variants of barrier functions are an active research topic (ch. 9).

Research integrity: claims, videos and trials

Robotics papers are often judged on videos and success rates, which invites selective reporting. A few habits make claims verifiable:

  • report the number of trials, the failures and the conditions, not only the best run;
  • release code, simulator configurations and hardware details, in line with the reproducible research course;
  • give confidence intervals: a handful of real-robot trials says less than it seems.

Example 8.3 — What 18 successes out of 20 trials tell Suppose a grasping policy succeeds in 18 of 20 trials, a “90 % success rate”. The exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95 % confidence interval for the success probability is [0.683, \ 0.988]. The data are compatible with a policy that fails almost one time in three. Comparing two methods at this sample size rarely supports a ranking.

A framework for responsible innovation

Stilgoe, Owen and Macnaghten (2013) propose four dimensions of responsible innovation, which translate into questions for a robotics project:

  • anticipation — who will use this robot, for what, and what could go wrong, including uses the authors do not intend;
  • reflexivity — which assumptions are built into the objective, the data and the benchmark;
  • inclusion — have the people who will work next to the robot, or be watched by it, been consulted;
  • responsiveness — can the project change course when the answers are uncomfortable, up to not publishing a component.

These questions have no algorithmic answer. They belong in the report of every project of this course (see the rubric in Projects and assessment), and they link this page to Responsible Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence and the Environment.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Robots raise sharper questions than software: physical harm, diluted responsibility, sensing, dual use, work and environment.
  • Standards and regulations frame industrial safety; control barrier functions give a provable, minimally invasive safety filter for a given model.
  • Success rates need trial counts and confidence intervals; 18 of 20 does not mean 90 %.
  • Anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion and responsiveness turn responsibility into concrete questions for each project.

9. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It points from each chapter of the course to active research questions, citing only well-established work, and ends with open problems that make good project or internship topics.

Differentiable and contact-rich simulation

Trajectory optimisation and learning both need derivatives of the dynamics. Carpentier and Mansard (2018) derived the analytical derivatives of the recursive rigid-body algorithms of ch. 1, which made second-order solvers such as the DDP of ch. 4 fast enough for humanoids.

Contacts remain the hard part. The complementarity conditions of Definition 1.9 make the dynamics non-smooth, and every simulator chooses a compromise between physical accuracy, convexity and differentiability. Todorov (2014) described the convex formulation behind MuJoCo; how to obtain informative gradients through contact switches is an open question.

Optimisation-based whole-body control

Whole-body MPC — the full dynamics of ch. 1, the contacts and the tasks in one receding-horizon problem — is now possible in real time on some robots. The survey of Wensing and coauthors (2024) maps the field: centroidal versus whole-body models, contact sequences fixed or optimised, and the solvers that make them run.

Strict task priorities (ch. 3) solved by hierarchical quadratic programming (Escande, Mansard and Wieber, 2014) raise their own questions. How should priorities be combined with the horizon of MPC, and with inequality constraints that switch on and off?

Learned locomotion and manipulation

Policies trained by RL in simulation now walk on rough terrain (Lee and coauthors, 2020; Miki and coauthors, 2022). In manipulation, imitation learning has progressed with expressive policy classes: Chi and coauthors (2023) represent the policy as a diffusion model over action sequences.

Brohan and coauthors (2023) fine-tune vision-language models to output robot actions. How such large models should be evaluated, and how their failures can be bounded, is largely open.

Estimation on Lie groups

The invariant EKF of ch. 5 (Barrau and Bonnabel, 2017) has spread from inertial navigation to legged robots (Hartley and coauthors, 2020). Solà, Deray and Atchuthan (2018) give a compact introduction to the Lie-group calculus that these filters use. Questions remain for systems that are not group-affine, and for the fusion of learned perception with filters that come with guarantees.

Safety of learned controllers

Control barrier functions (ch. 8; Ames and coauthors, 2017, 2019) certify safety for a known model. Making them robust to model errors and to estimation errors, learning them from data, and combining them with RL policies without destroying performance are active topics.

Open problems to think about

  1. Can a simulator be differentiable, physically consistent at impacts and fast, all at once? Which of the three can be relaxed for which task?
  2. The simulation lemma (Proposition 6.4) is pessimistic. Which structural properties of a robot make policies transfer much better than the bound predicts?
  3. How many real-robot trials are needed to rank two controllers with confidence (Example 8.3)? Can simulation reduce that number without biasing the comparison?
  4. Can an invariant filter be built for a legged robot whose feet slip, with a consistent uncertainty?
  5. What would a responsible-innovation assessment (ch. 8) of a general-purpose humanoid look like, and who should carry it out?

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Derivatives of rigid-body dynamics are solved; derivatives through contact are not.
  • Whole-body MPC and hierarchical QPs bring optimisation to the full robot in real time.
  • Learning-based policies dominate rough-terrain locomotion and progress fast in manipulation; their evaluation and certification lag behind.
  • Lie-group estimation and barrier-function safety are mature for known models and open for learned ones.

  1. ★ Kinematics of the two-link arm. For the arm of Example 1.4 (l_1 = 1, l_2 = 0.8) at q = (0, \pi/2), compute the position of the end effector, the Jacobian and its determinant. Which joint velocities move the end effector straight up at 0.1 m/s? List all singular configurations.

    Solution: the end effector is at (1, 0.8), and J = \begin{pmatrix} -0.8 & -0.8 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J = 0.8 = l_1 l_2 \sin q_2 .

    Solving J \dot q = (0, 0.1) gives \dot q_1 = 0.1 and \dot q_2 = -0.1 rad/s: the shoulder lifts and the elbow compensates to keep x fixed.

    The singular configurations are q_2 = 0 and q_2 = \pi, for any q_1.

  2. ★ A rotation that permutes the axes. With Rodrigues’ formula (Proposition 0.6), show that the rotation of angle 2\pi/3 about u = (1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3} sends e_x to e_y. Without computation, where does it send e_y?

    Solution: [u]_\times e_x = u \times e_x = (0, 1, -1)/\sqrt{3} and [u]_\times^2 e_x = u (u^\top e_x) - e_x = (-2/3, 1/3, 1/3). With \sin(2\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}/2 and 1 - \cos(2\pi/3) = 3/2: R e_x = (1, 0, 0) + \tfrac12 (0, 1, -1) + (-1, \tfrac12, \tfrac12) = (0, 1, 0). The rotation commutes with the cyclic permutation of the axes, which fixes u, so it also sends e_y to e_z and e_z to e_x.

  3. ★ One Kalman step by hand. A constant-velocity model with \Delta t = 1 and no process noise has prior mean (0, 1) and covariance I. The position is measured as y = 1.5 with noise variance 1. Compute the predicted and updated means and covariances.

    Solution: prediction, m^- = (1, 1) and P^- = F F^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

    Update: S = 3, K = (2/3, 1/3), innovation 0.5, hence m = (1.333, 1.167) and P = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 2/3 \end{pmatrix}. The velocity estimate moved, although only the position was measured: the prediction correlated the two. Repeated over time, this is how the velocity becomes observable (Proposition 5.3).

  4. ★ How many samples for a roadmap? In a free space of area 0.9 in the plane, a path of length L = 2 has clearance r = 0.05. With Theorem 2.3, how many uniform samples guarantee a failure probability of at most 1 %? What if the clearance is halved?

    Solution: m = 80 and p = \pi (0.0125)^2 / 0.9 \approx 5.45 \times 10^{-4}. The condition 80\, e^{-N p} \leq 0.01 gives N \geq \ln(8000) / p \approx 16{,}500.

    With r = 0.025, m doubles and p is divided by four, so N \approx 71{,}000: more than four times as many samples. Narrow passages dominate the cost of sampling-based planning.

  5. ★ Push recovery. A humanoid has its CoM at h = 1 m. It stands still with its ZMP able to move at most 0.12 m ahead of the CoM. Can it stop without stepping after a push to 0.3 m/s? To 0.5 m/s? How long does it have before the divergent component has grown by a factor e?

    Solution: \omega = \sqrt{9.81} \approx 3.13 s^{-1}. After 0.3 m/s the capture point is 0.3 / 3.13 \approx 0.096 m ahead, inside the reach of the ZMP: the ankles suffice (Proposition 7.5).

    After 0.5 m/s it is 0.16 m ahead, beyond 0.12 m: the robot must step. The time constant is 1/\omega \approx 0.32 s.

  6. ★★ Holding a load. The arm of Example 1.4, with massless links, holds a 1 kg mass at the tip in the configuration q = (0.4, 1.1). Compute the joint torques with Proposition 1.6, and interpret each one as a weight times a lever arm. Which configurations of the same tip height minimise the shoulder torque?

    Solution: the arm must push up with f = (0, 9.81) N, so \tau = J^\top f = 9.81 \begin{pmatrix} l_1 \cos q_1 + l_2 \cos(q_1 + q_2) \\ l_2 \cos(q_1 + q_2) \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 9.59 \\ 0.555 \end{pmatrix} \text{N m}.

    Each torque is the weight times the horizontal distance from the joint to the load: \tau_1 = 9.81\, x. The shoulder torque is therefore minimal, and zero, when the load is directly above the shoulder (x = 0), if the tip height is reachable there.

  7. ★★ Two tasks without interference. Let P_1 = I - J_1^+ J_1 and \dot q = J_1^+ v_1 + (J_2 P_1)^+ (v_2 - J_2 J_1^+ v_1), with J_1 of full row rank. Prove that the first task is always met exactly, and that the second one is met too when J_2 P_1 has full row rank.

    Solution: the range of (J_2 P_1)^+ is the range of (J_2 P_1)^\top = P_1 J_2^\top, contained in the range of P_1, which is \ker J_1 (Proposition 3.3). Hence J_1 (J_2 P_1)^+ = 0 and J_1 \dot q = J_1 J_1^+ v_1 = v_1.

    For the second task, P_1 (J_2 P_1)^+ = (J_2 P_1)^+ because P_1 is a projector onto a space containing that range. So J_2 (J_2 P_1)^+ = J_2 P_1 (J_2 P_1)^+ = I under the rank assumption, and J_2 \dot q = J_2 J_1^+ v_1 + v_2 - J_2 J_1^+ v_1 = v_2.

  8. ★★ The scalar Riccati equation. For x_{t+1} = a x_t + b u_t with a = 1.2, b = 1, q = r = 1, solve the stationary Riccati equation and give the gain and the closed-loop pole.

    Then show that when r \to \infty (expensive control) and |a| > 1, the closed-loop pole tends to 1/a.

    Solution: multiplying the equation by 1 + p gives p^2 - 1.44 p - 1 = 0, so p \approx 1.952, K = 1.2 p / (1 + p) \approx 0.794, and the pole is a - b K \approx 0.406.

    For large r, write p = r s and divide by r. In the limit, s = a^2 s - a^2 b^2 s^2 / (1 + b^2 s). Since s \neq 0 for an unstable system, 1 + b^2 s = a^2, hence K \to \frac{a^2 - 1}{a b}, \qquad a - b K \to \frac{1}{a} . The cheapest stabilising controller mirrors the unstable pole inside the unit circle; here 1/1.2 \approx 0.833.

  9. ★★ Minimum-energy transfer, general case. Redo Example 4.6 for a transfer of length D in time T. How does the optimal cost scale with T? What does that say about fast motions?

    Solution: the same steps give u(t) = a + b t, and the four boundary conditions yield u(t) = \frac{6 D}{T^2} \Big( 1 - \frac{2 t}{T} \Big), \qquad \int_0^T u^2\, dt = \frac{12 D^2}{T^3} . Halving the duration multiplies the energy by eight, and the peak acceleration 6 D / T^2 by four: fast motions quickly hit torque limits.

  10. ★★ The best constant baseline. For the estimator \hat g = s\, (G - b), where s = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta is a scalar score and b a constant, show that \hat g is unbiased for every b.

    Find the b that minimises its variance, and check that \mathbb{E}[s] = 0 for the policy of Example 6.3.

    Solution: \mathbb{E}[s\, b] = b\, \mathbb{E}[s] = 0 by the identity in the proof of Theorem 6.2, so the mean does not depend on b. The second moment \mathbb{E}[s^2 (G - b)^2] is a quadratic in b, minimal at b^\star = \frac{\mathbb{E}[s^2 G]}{\mathbb{E}[s^2]} , a score-weighted average of the costs. For Example 6.3, \mathbb{E}[-\varepsilon x / \sigma] = 0 because \varepsilon is centred and independent of x.

  11. ★★ Why walking needs preview. Prove that the only solution of \dot \xi = \omega (\xi - z) that stays bounded for a bounded ZMP z is \xi(t) = \omega \int_t^\infty e^{-\omega (s - t)} z(s)\, ds. Apply it to a ZMP that jumps from 0 to d at time t_1 (a step), and interpret.

    Solution: the difference of two solutions is C e^{\omega t}, bounded only if C = 0. The given formula is bounded by \sup |z|, and differentiating it gives \dot \xi = -\omega z(t) + \omega \xi(t).

    For the step, \xi(t) = d\, e^{-\omega (t_1 - t)} for t < t_1. The divergent component, hence the CoM, starts moving towards the new foot before the step, with a lead time of order 1/\omega — what the code of ch. 7 shows.

  12. ★★ The safety filter in closed form. Show that the solution of \min_u \|u - u_{\mathrm{nom}}\|^2 subject to a^\top u \geq \beta, with a \neq 0, is u^\star = u_{\mathrm{nom}} + \max\big(0, \ \beta - a^\top u_{\mathrm{nom}}\big) \frac{a}{\|a\|^2} . Identify a and \beta in the code of ch. 8.

    Solution: if u_{\mathrm{nom}} is feasible, it is optimal. Otherwise the constraint is active at the optimum (a convex problem whose unconstrained minimiser is infeasible). Proposition 0.4 with the single equality a^\top u = \beta gives u = u_{\mathrm{nom}} + \nu a with \nu = (\beta - a^\top u_{\mathrm{nom}}) / \|a\|^2 > 0.

    In the code, a = \nabla h(x) = 2 (x - c) and \beta = -\alpha h(x), and slack is a^\top u_{\mathrm{nom}} - \beta.

  13. ★★ Damped least squares near a singularity (Python). Put the two-link arm almost stretched, q = (0, 0.01), and ask the tip to move towards the base at 0.1 m/s. Compare the joint velocities and the task error given by the pseudo-inverse and by damped least squares with \lambda = 10^{-4} and 10^{-2}. Explain the results with Proposition 0.3.

    Hints and expected result: the smallest singular value of J is about 0.004. The pseudo-inverse asks for about 25 rad/s, physically impossible, to track the task exactly.

    With \lambda = 10^{-4}, the joint speed drops to about 3.5 rad/s and the task is tracked only partially. With \lambda = 10^{-2}, the arm barely moves. The factor \sigma / (\sigma^2 + \lambda) replaces 1/\sigma: damping trades accuracy for bounded speeds.

    import numpy as np

l1, l2 = 1.0, 0.8

def jac(q):
    s1, c1, s12, c12 = np.sin(q[0]), np.cos(q[0]), np.sin(q.sum()), np.cos(q.sum())
    return np.array([[-l1 * s1 - l2 * s12, -l2 * s12], [l1 * c1 + l2 * c12, l2 * c12]])

q = np.array([0.0, 0.01])            # arm almost stretched along the x axis
v = np.array([-0.1, 0.0])            # move the tip towards the base: the nearly singular direction
J = jac(q)
print("singular values of J:", np.round(np.linalg.svd(J, compute_uv=False), 5))
for lam in [0.0, 1e-4, 1e-2]:
    dq = J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + lam * np.eye(2), v)
    print(f"lambda = {lam:g}: |dq| = {np.linalg.norm(dq):7.3f} rad/s, "
          f"task velocity error {np.linalg.norm(J @ dq - v):.4f} m/s")

  14. ★★ Is my Kalman filter consistent? (Python). For a consistent filter, the normalised estimation error squared e^\top P^{-1} e, with e = x - m, has mean equal to the state dimension. Check it by Monte Carlo on the constant-velocity model of ch. 5. Then give the filter a process-noise covariance one hundred times too small.

    Hints and expected result: with the right noise model, the average is 2.00. With the underestimated noise, it is about 72: the filter is overconfident, and in a real robot it would reject valid measurements as outliers. This test is the standard way to tune Q and R.

    import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
dt, n, runs, sa, sr = 0.1, 100, 500, 0.5, 1.0
F, H = np.array([[1.0, dt], [0.0, 1.0]]), np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])
G = np.array([dt**2 / 2, dt])                    # a random acceleration enters through G
Q = sa**2 * np.outer(G, G)

def average_nees(Q_filter):
    """Average over runs of e^T P^-1 e at the last step (should be about 2 if consistent)."""
    x = rng.standard_normal((runs, 2))           # true initial states, prior N(0, I)
    m, P = np.zeros((runs, 2)), np.eye(2)
    for _ in range(n):
        x = x @ F.T + sa * np.outer(rng.standard_normal(runs), G)
        y = x[:, 0] + sr * rng.standard_normal(runs)
        m, P = m @ F.T, F @ P @ F.T + Q_filter
        S = P[0, 0] + sr**2
        K = P[:, 0] / S
        m = m + np.outer(y - m[:, 0], K)
        P = P - np.outer(K, P[0, :])
    e = x - m
    return np.mean(np.einsum("ri,ij,rj->r", e, np.linalg.inv(P), e))

print(f"correct Q:        NEES = {average_nees(Q):.2f}  (dimension 2)")
print(f"Q divided by 100: NEES = {average_nees(Q / 100):.2f}  (overconfident filter)")

Apply a lateral push (a jump of the CoM velocity) at t = 4 s and find the largest push the generator can absorb without violating the constraints. Then let the next footstep position be a decision variable, as in Herdt and coauthors (2010).

    Hints and expected result: with fixed footsteps, the QP becomes infeasible beyond a push for which the capture point leaves the reachable ZMP region (Proposition 7.5). Report this threshold as a function of the foot half-width. With a free footstep, the admissible push grows markedly. A clean figure of the CoM, ZMP and footsteps with and without the push is the expected deliverable.

  16. ★★★ Invariant versus standard EKF (self-study, ch. 5 and ch. 9). Simulate a unicycle in the plane, \dot \theta = \omega and \dot p = R(\theta) (v, 0), measured by noisy odometry (v, \omega) and noisy GPS positions.

    Implement the standard EKF on (\theta, p) and an invariant EKF on SE(2), choosing between the errors \hat g\, g^{-1} and g^{-1} \hat g according to the form of the GPS measurement, as Barrau and Bonnabel (2017) explain. Compare the two filters from an initial heading error of 90 degrees.

    Hints and expected result: the dynamics are group-affine, so the invariant error propagates independently of the estimate. Expect the invariant EKF to converge from large heading errors where the standard EKF can become inconsistent — its covariance shrinks while its error does not. Plot the error and the normalised error of ex. 14 for both filters over 100 runs.

  17. ★★★ A responsible-robotics case study (ch. 8). A team plans to deploy a sidewalk delivery robot in a city. Write a two-page analysis organised by the four dimensions of Stilgoe and coauthors (2013). Add one quantitative element: how many independent trials without a single failure are needed to claim, with 95 % confidence, a failure probability below 1 %?

    Hints and expected result: with zero failures in n trials, the one-sided bound requires 0.99^n \leq 0.05, that is n \geq 299 — the “rule of three”, 3/n.   2. Question (6 points). A planar two-link arm has l_1 = l_2 = 1 m.   3. Question (5 points). State and prove the global asymptotic stability of PD control with gravity compensation for a fully actuated robot. Say exactly where the skew-symmetry of \dot M - 2C is used, and what happens without gravity compensation.
