0. Review: Euclidean structure and projections

Later chapters minimise a convex function over a convex set in Euclidean space. The arguments use an inner product, the spectral theorem, and a projection. This chapter records those three tools. A quadratic is convex when every eigenvalue of its symmetric matrix is nonnegative, which is the test in Exercise 2 and again in ch. 6.

Inner product and symmetric matrices

Definition 0.1 — Euclidean structure

The Euclidean inner product of two vectors in \mathbb{R}^n is the sum of the products of their coordinates. The Euclidean norm is the square root of the inner product of a vector with itself. Cauchy-Schwarz says that the absolute value of an inner product is at most the product of the two norms. Equality holds when the vectors are parallel.

Proposition 0.2 — Spectral theorem

Every symmetric real matrix factors as Q D Q^\top with Q orthogonal and D diagonal. The diagonal entries are the eigenvalues, and they are real. The quadratic form x^\top A x is nonnegative for every vector if and only if every eigenvalue is nonnegative. The matrix is then positive semidefinite.

Proof. Symmetry gives an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, which is the spectral theorem in finite dimension. If x = Q z, then x^\top A x = z^\top D z, a sum of eigenvalue times squared coordinate. The sum is nonnegative for every z exactly when every eigenvalue is nonnegative. ∎

Projection onto a closed convex set

Definition 0.3 — Closed convex set

A set C \subset \mathbb{R}^n is convex when the segment between any two of its points lies in C. It is closed when it contains its limit points. Halfspaces, affine subspaces, boxes and Euclidean balls are closed and convex. An open halfspace is convex and not closed.

Theorem 0.4 — Projection

Let C be a nonempty closed convex set, and let y be any point. There is a unique nearest point of C to y. It is characterised by the variational inequality

\langle y - x,\, z - x \rangle \leq 0 \qquad \text{for every } z \in C.

Proof. Existence follows from a minimising sequence of the distance. The sequence is bounded, so it has a convergent subsequence, and the limit lies in C because C is closed.

Uniqueness uses the parallelogram identity. If x_1 and x_2 were both closest, their midpoint would lie in C and would be strictly closer unless x_1 = x_2.

For the inequality, fix a feasible point and a step in (0, 1). Moving from the projection toward that point by this fraction stays in the set, so the new point is no closer to y. Expand the squared distance, divide by the step, and let the step decrease to 0. The inequality remains. ∎

Example 0.5 — Clipping onto the unit box

Project y = (1.2,\, -0.4,\, 0.3) onto the box [0, 1]^3. Clipping each coordinate to [0, 1] gives x = (1,\, 0,\, 0.3).

The residual is (0.2, -0.4, 0). The first coordinate sits on the upper face and the second on the lower face, so the variational inequality holds.

Key formula — variational inequality

\langle y - x,\, z - x \rangle \leq 0 \qquad \text{for every } z \in C.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A symmetric matrix is positive semidefinite exactly when every eigenvalue is nonnegative. Gram matrices G^\top G are positive semidefinite.
  • A nonempty closed convex set has a unique nearest point. The residual makes a nonpositive inner product with every feasible direction.
  • On a box the projection is coordinate-wise clipping. On a subspace it is the orthogonal projection.
  • These two facts are the linear algebra behind convex programs, Newton systems and semidefinite constraints.

1. Convex sets, functions and programs

A model is convex when the feasible set is convex and the objective lies above its tangents. This chapter gives the tests, and it records that a local minimum is then global.

Convex sets

Intersection preserves convexity, so the probability simplex is convex. Affine images do too, so ellipsoids are convex.

The positive semidefinite cone is convex. If A and B are positive semidefinite and \theta \in [0, 1], then for every vector u

u^\top (\theta A + (1 - \theta) B)\, u = \theta\, u^\top A u + (1 - \theta)\, u^\top B u \geq 0.

That one-line check is why a semidefinite constraint is a convex constraint in ch. 6.

Definition 1.1 — Convex function

A function f with convex domain is convex when, for every x, y in the domain and every \theta \in [0, 1],

f(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y) \leq \theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y).

It is strictly convex when the inequality is strict for \theta \in (0, 1) and x \neq y. Norms are convex. A sum of convex functions is convex. The pointwise maximum of convex functions is convex.

Proposition 1.2 — First-order and second-order tests

Suppose f is differentiable on an open convex domain. Then f is convex if and only if every point x satisfies the tangent inequality

f(y) \geq f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x),\, y - x \rangle \qquad \text{for every } y.

If f is twice continuously differentiable, convexity is also equivalent to a positive semidefinite Hessian at every point.

Proof. Assume convexity. The definition rearranges into a difference quotient bounded by f(y) - f(x). Let the step size decrease to 0. The quotient becomes the directional derivative, which is the inner product of the gradient with y - x.

Conversely, write a point of the segment as a convex combination and apply the tangent inequality at that point to both endpoints. Weight the two inequalities and add them. The gradient terms cancel and the definition of convexity remains.

If the Hessian is positive semidefinite, the integral form of the remainder is nonnegative, so the tangent inequality holds. The converse follows by applying that inequality to a short step along an eigenvector. ∎

Key formula — tangent lower bound

f(y) \geq f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x),\, y - x \rangle.

Theorem 1.3 — Local minima are global

Let f be convex. Every local minimum is a global minimum. If f is strictly convex, there is at most one global minimum.

Proof. Suppose x^\star is a local minimum and some feasible point has a strictly smaller value. Points on the open segment lie in the domain. Convexity puts their values strictly below f(x^\star). Points of the segment close to x^\star contradict the local minimum.

If two distinct points had the same minimal value, strict convexity would put a strictly smaller value at their midpoint. ∎

Convex programs

Definition 1.4 — Convex program

A convex program minimises a convex objective over a convex set. In standard form one minimises f_0(x) subject to f_i(x) \leq 0 and Ax = b, where f_0 and each f_i are convex. Affine equalities define a convex set. Sublevel sets of convex functions are convex, so the inequalities do too.

Example 1.5 — Three convex models

Least squares minimises \|Xw - y\|^2. The Hessian in w is 2 X^\top X, which is positive semidefinite, so the objective is convex and there is no constraint.

Logistic regression, with labels in \{-1, +1\}, minimises the sum of \log(1 + e^{-y_i w^\top x_i}). The second derivative of t \mapsto \log(1 + e^{-t}) is positive, and an affine score preserves convexity.

A convex quadratic program minimises \tfrac{1}{2} x^\top Q x + c^\top x with Q positive semidefinite, subject to linear equalities and inequalities. Portfolio problems in ch. 5 are of this form.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Intersection and affine images preserve convex sets. The positive semidefinite cone is convex.
  • A differentiable convex function lies above its tangents, and a positive semidefinite Hessian is the second-order test.
  • A local minimum of a convex function is global.
  • Least squares, logistic regression and convex quadratic programs are convex programs.

2. Lagrangian duality

A feasible point upper-bounds the optimal value. Duality builds matching lower bounds from the constraints. This chapter proves weak duality and shows how a zero gap forces complementary slackness.

Definition 2.1 — Lagrangian and dual function

For the standard program of Definition 1.4, with inequality multipliers \lambda \geq 0 and equality multipliers \nu, the Lagrangian is

L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f_0(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x) + \nu^\top (Ax - b).

The dual function is the infimum of L over x in the domain. The dual problem maximises the dual function over \lambda \geq 0 and over \nu free.

Proposition 2.2 — Weak duality

Every dual-feasible value is at most every primal-feasible value. In particular the dual optimal value is at most the primal optimal value.

Proof. Take a nonnegative multiplier vector and a primal-feasible point. Each penalty term in the Lagrangian is nonpositive, and the equality residual vanishes, so the Lagrangian is at most the objective. The dual function is an infimum of the Lagrangian, hence at most the objective. ∎

Key formula — weak duality

q(\lambda, \nu) \leq f_0(x)

for every primal-feasible x and every \lambda \geq 0.

A zero gap proves optimality of both points. Exercise 6 computes one such pair on a random affine constraint.

Theorem 2.3 — Strong duality under Slater’s condition

Suppose the program is convex and there is a strictly feasible point, meaning f_i(x) < 0 for every inequality and Ax = b. Then the dual optimal value equals the primal optimal value, and the dual problem attains its maximum whenever the primal value is finite.

Proof. Sketch. Consider the set of constraint-and-objective values that the primal can reach, and the open set of values that would beat the primal optimum while keeping the inequalities strict. Both sets are convex, and they do not meet. A separating hyperplane produces multipliers. Strict feasibility stops the multiplier of the objective from vanishing, which turns the weak-duality inequality into equality. Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004), sections 5.3 and 5.4, write the separation in full. ∎

Proposition 2.4 — KKT conditions

Assume convex differentiable functions, a zero duality gap, and multipliers (\lambda, \nu) dual optimal. A feasible x is primal optimal if and only if the Lagrangian is minimised at x and complementary slackness holds,

\lambda_i f_i(x) = 0 \qquad \text{for every } i.

If the domain is open, minimising the Lagrangian is the stationarity condition that its gradient in x vanishes.

Proof. Zero gap and weak duality give the chain

f_0(x) = q(\lambda, \nu) \leq L(x, \lambda, \nu) \leq f_0(x).

The two inequalities are therefore equalities. The second one forces every product of a multiplier with its inequality to vanish. Each product is nonpositive, so each product is zero.

The first equality says that the infimum defining q is attained at this x, so x minimises the Lagrangian. Differentiability on an open domain turns that into a vanishing gradient. ∎

Example 2.5 — A bound on the line

Minimise \tfrac{1}{2} x^2 subject to x \geq 1. The Lagrangian adds \lambda(1 - x) with \lambda \geq 0. The infimum over the scalar is attained at x = \lambda and equals \lambda - \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^2.

The dual maximum is at \lambda = 1, with value 1/2, matching the primal minimum at x = 1. Complementary slackness holds because the constraint is active.

The same pattern gives the minimum-norm point on an affine subspace Ax = b. Stationarity reads x = A^\top \nu. Exercise 5 derives the dual, and Exercise 6 checks the value.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Every dual value lower-bounds every primal value.
  • A strictly feasible point gives zero duality gap for a convex program.
  • Zero gap forces complementary slackness, and stationarity minimises the Lagrangian.
  • A dual feasible point is a lower bound that does not require the whole feasible set.

3. Interior-point methods

Newton’s method rescales the gradient by the inverse Hessian. Interior-point methods apply that step to a barrier whose minimiser traces the central path. Self-concordance makes the number of steps depend on the barrier parameter.

Newton steps

Definition 3.1 — Newton step and Newton decrement

For a twice differentiable function with positive definite Hessian, the Newton step solves Hv = -g. The Newton decrement is the local norm of that step. It is also the decrease predicted by the quadratic model.

Proposition 3.2 — One Newton step on a barrier

Let f(x) = (x - 3)^2 - \log x on (0, \infty). Starting at x = 1, one Newton step lands at 8/3.

Proof. The first derivative is 2(x-3)-1/x, hence -5 at the start x=1. The second derivative is 2+1/x^2, hence 3 there. The Newton update adds 5/3 and lands at 8/3. ∎

A second Newton step from 8/3 lands near 3.153, against a minimiser near 3.158. Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004), section 9.6, place this quadratic phase at decrement at most 1/4.

Barriers and the central path

Definition 3.3 — Logarithmic barrier and central path

For inequalities f_i(x) \leq 0, the logarithmic barrier is \phi(x) = -\sum_i \log(-f_i(x)), defined only in the strict interior. For a parameter t > 0 the central-path point minimises t f_0(x) + \phi(x). As t grows, the barrier becomes a smaller fraction of the objective and the central point approaches a primal minimiser.

Example 3.4 — An explicit central point

Minimise (x - 3)^2 subject to x > 0. At parameter t = 1 the central objective is the function of Proposition 3.2. Its derivative vanishes when 2(x - 3) = 1/x, so 2x^2 - 6x - 1 = 0.

The positive root is (3 + \sqrt{11})/2, about 3.158. For a general parameter the stationarity condition is 2t(x-3)=1/x. As the parameter grows, the root decreases toward 3. As it decreases, the root tends to infinity.

Theorem 3.5 — Self-concordance of the logarithm

The function \varphi(u) = -\log u on (0, \infty) is self-concordant. In Nesterov’s normalisation, the third derivative along any direction h satisfies

|\varphi'''(u)[h, h, h]| \leq 2\, \big(\varphi''(u)[h, h]\big)^{3/2}.

Proof. The first three derivatives are -1/u, 1/u^2 and -2/u^3. Both sides of the inequality equal 2|h|^3/u^3. The constant 2 is sharp. ∎

The orthant barrier -\sum_i \log x_i is self-concordant with parameter \nu = m. On positive definite matrices, -\log\det has parameter n. That parameter is what the complexity bound uses.

Proposition 3.6 — Complexity of short-step path following

Let the feasible set carry a self-concordant barrier of parameter \nu. A short-step path-following method, which increases t by a fixed fraction of order 1/\sqrt{\nu} and recenters by one Newton step, returns an \varepsilon-accurate point after

O\big(\sqrt{\nu}\, \log(1/\varepsilon)\big)

Newton steps. For m linear inequalities the logarithm gives \nu = m, hence order \sqrt{m} Newton steps up to the logarithm of the accuracy.

Proof. Sketch. Self-concordance makes the Newton decrement a faithful measure of the distance to the central point, with constants that do not depend on the level set. While the decrement is large, a damped Newton step decreases the barrier-augmented objective by a fixed amount. Once the decrement is at most 1/4, full steps converge quadratically, as in the numerical example above.

Increasing t by a factor 1 + \Theta(1/\sqrt{\nu}) keeps the next point inside the quadratic region. Reaching a target of order \nu/\varepsilon therefore takes order \sqrt{\nu}\, \log(1/\varepsilon) increases, and each increase costs a constant number of Newton steps. Nesterov and Nemirovskii (1994) and Nesterov (2004), chapter 4, give the constants. ∎

Interior-point methods fit when the Newton system is affordable and a certificate is required. A huge weight vector with a modest gap is a better match for ch. 4.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • One Newton step on (x-3)^2 - \log x, started at 1, lands at 8/3.
  • The logarithmic barrier keeps iterates interior. Its minimiser is the central-path point.
  • -\log u is self-concordant with constant 2. The orthant barrier has parameter m.
  • Short-step path following uses order \sqrt{\nu}\,\log(1/\varepsilon) Newton steps.

4. First-order methods and acceleration

Learning models are often smooth, or smooth plus an \ell_1 penalty, with too many variables for a Hessian. This chapter proves the descent lemma and the 1/k rate, then states acceleration and soft-thresholding.

Definition 4.1 — Smoothness and strong convexity

A differentiable function is L-smooth when its gradient is Lipschitz with constant L. If it is also convex and twice differentiable, the eigenvalues of the Hessian lie in [0, L].

It is \mu-strongly convex, for \mu > 0, when the tangent lower bound of ch. 1 can be strengthened by \tfrac{\mu}{2}\|y - x\|^2. The condition number is \kappa = L/\mu when both constants exist.

Theorem 4.2 — Descent lemma

If f is L-smooth, then for every x and y

f(y) \leq f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x),\, y - x \rangle + \frac{L}{2}\|y - x\|^2.

Proof. Write the increment of f as an integral of the derivative along the segment. Subtract the integral of the gradient at the start. Lipschitz continuity bounds the gradient increment by t L times the displacement. Integrating the factor t from 0 to 1 produces 1/2. ∎

The step y = x - \nabla f(x)/L decreases the value by at least \|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2L).

Theorem 4.3 — Gradient descent on a smooth convex function

Let f be convex and L-smooth, with the iteration x_{k+1} = x_k - \nabla f(x_k)/L. The gap after k steps is at most 2 L R^2 / k, where R is the distance from the start to a minimiser.

Proof. Co-coercivity of a convex L-smooth gradient, Nesterov (2004), Theorem 2.1.5, gives

\langle \nabla f(x_k), x_k - x^\star \rangle \geq \|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 / L.

Expanding the squared distance to the minimiser, the cross term and this inequality show that the distance never increases.

Convexity bounds the gap by \|\nabla f(x_k)\| R. Together with the descent inequality, this yields \delta_k - \delta_{k+1} \geq \delta_k^2 / (2 L R^2).

Taking reciprocals, 1/\delta_{k+1} - 1/\delta_k \geq 1/(2 L R^2). After k steps the reciprocal is at least k/(2 L R^2). ∎

On a quadratic whose Hessian eigenvalues lie between \mu and L, the same iteration multiplies errors by at most 1 - \mu/L.

Example 4.4 — Condition number 100

Let f(x,y)=\tfrac12 x^2+50 y^2. The Hessian eigenvalues are 1 and 100, so L=100 and \mu=1. The gradient step 1/L multiplies the slow coordinate by 0.99.

Starting from that coordinate equal to 1, it falls to 0.01 only after 459 steps, because 0.99^{458}>0.01 and 0.99^{459}<0.01. The accelerated factor 0.9 falls below 0.01 by step 44, because 0.9^{43}>0.01 and 0.9^{44}<0.01.

Theorem 4.5 — Nesterov acceleration

Let f be convex and L-smooth. Nesterov’s accelerated method, in the form used by Beck and Teboulle (2009) when the nonsmooth term is zero, satisfies

f(x_k) - f(x^\star) \leq \frac{2 L \|x_0 - x^\star\|^2}{(k + 1)^2}.

Set y_1 = x_0 and t_1 = 1. For k \geq 1, take a gradient step from y_k with step 1/L to obtain x_k, then set

t_{k+1} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 t_k^2}}{2}, \qquad y_{k+1} = x_k + \frac{t_k - 1}{t_{k+1}}(x_k - x_{k-1}),

with the convention x_0 = x_1 so that the first momentum coefficient vanishes.

Proof. Sketch. The weights t_k grow like k/2. Beck and Teboulle (2009), Theorem 4.4, prove the rate with a Lyapunov function. Nesterov (1983) gave the first O(1/k^2) method, and Nesterov (2004), section 2.2, gives the estimate-sequence proof. ∎

Strong convexity improves the contraction to 1 - 1/\sqrt{\kappa}.

Proximal maps

Definition 4.6 — Proximal map of the \ell_1 norm

For a convex g and a step \alpha > 0, the proximal map minimises g(u) + \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\|u-v\|^2. For a scaled \ell_1 norm this map is coordinate-wise soft-thresholding.

Proposition 4.7 — Soft-thresholding

The proximal map of \lambda |\cdot| on the real line, with \alpha = 1, sends v to \mathrm{sign}(v)\, \max(|v| - \lambda,\, 0).

Proof. The objective \lambda|u| + \tfrac{1}{2}(u-v)^2 is strictly convex. A positive critical point sits at u = v - \lambda, and it is positive only when v > \lambda.

A negative critical point sits at v + \lambda, and it is negative only when v < -\lambda. Otherwise the subdifferential at zero contains zero. ∎

Proximal gradient steps on an \ell_1 penalty are iterative soft-thresholding. Beck and Teboulle (2009) accelerate them with the 1/k^2 guarantee of Theorem 4.5. The lasso in ch. 5 is solved that way.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • At step 1/L, the descent lemma removes at least \|\nabla f\|^2/(2L) from the value.
  • Smooth convex gradient descent has gap at most 2LR^2/k. Condition number 100 needs 459 steps on the slow coordinate.
  • Nesterov’s method improves the guarantee to order 1/k^2, and the same quadratic reaches the tolerance in 44 steps.
  • Soft-thresholding is the proximal map of the \ell_1 norm.

5. Statistics, finance and signal processing

The course description names statistics and learning, finance, and signal processing. This chapter writes a lasso, a Markowitz portfolio and a basis-pursuit program, and it solves the portfolio by the KKT system.

Empirical risk

Definition 5.1 — Empirical risk and the lasso

Given pairs (x_i, y_i), an empirical risk is the average of a loss \ell(w^\top x_i,\, y_i). Least squares and logistic loss are convex in w, by Example 1.5. The lasso adds an \ell_1 penalty,

\frac{1}{2n}\|Xw - y\|^2 + \lambda \|w\|_1.

The quadratic term is smooth and convex. The penalty is convex and nonsmooth. The sum is the composite model of ch. 4.

Ordinary least squares needs a tall matrix, or a ridge term, before the normal equation has a unique solution. When there are more columns than rows the quadratic \tfrac{1}{2}\|Xw - y\|^2 is constant along a subspace of the kernel, so minimisers form an affine space. The \ell_1 penalty selects among them. Tibshirani (1996) introduced that selection as a convex program. The proximal step is soft-thresholding of a gradient step on the quadratic, with threshold proportional to \lambda.

Proposition 5.2 — Logistic loss is convex

For a label y \in \{-1, +1\} and a score z \in \mathbb{R}, the loss \log(1 + e^{-yz}) is convex in z. Therefore the empirical logistic risk is convex in the weight vector.

Proof. Let \psi(z) = \log(1 + e^{-z}). Its second derivative equals e^{z}/(e^{z}+1)^2, which is positive, so \psi is convex. The loss applies \psi to the product of the label and the score. That product is affine in the score, and an affine precomposition preserves convexity. ∎

A two-asset portfolio

Example 5.3 — Minimum-variance portfolio

Let the covariance be

\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

Minimise \tfrac{1}{2} w^\top \Sigma w subject to the weights summing to 1. Stationarity says that \Sigma w is a multiple of the all-ones vector. The solution is proportional to (1, 3), and normalising produces the weights 1/4 and 3/4.

The minimal variance equals 1 divided by (1, 1) \Sigma^{-1} (1, 1)^\top, which is 7/8. Both weights are positive, so the same point remains optimal if one adds the constraints w \geq 0. Markowitz (1952) is this quadratic program, with a target return added as a linear inequality when a target is required.

Sparse recovery

Example 5.4 — Basis pursuit on three variables

Minimise the sum of three absolute values subject to x+y+z=1 and x+2y=0. Substituting the two equalities leaves 3|y|+|1+y|, with minimum 1 at y=0. The recovered vector has a single nonzero entry.

The feasible point y = -1/2, x = 1, z = 1/2 has \ell_1 norm 2, twice the optimum. The convex program has preferred the sparse feasible point. Candès, Romberg and Tao (2006) give conditions under which this preference recovers a planted sparse vector from underdetermined linear measurements. The proof uses a dual certificate and is not reproduced here.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Least squares and logistic regression are convex empirical risks. The lasso adds an \ell_1 term and is solved by proximal gradient.
  • When there are more features than samples, least squares is not unique. The \ell_1 penalty selects a sparse minimiser.
  • The minimum-variance portfolio with covariance \Sigma above has weights 1/4 and 3/4 and variance 7/8.
  • Basis pursuit on the two equalities above returns (0, 0, 1), with \ell_1 norm 1, against a feasible dense point of norm 2.

6. Combinatorial problems and convex relaxations

Max-cut partitions vertices to cross as many edges as possible. The Boolean cube is not convex. A convex relaxation replaces xx^\top by a positive semidefinite matrix with diagonal 1, and a random hyperplane turns the matrix back into a cut.

Definition 6.1 — Max-cut and its semidefinite relaxation

Let G be an undirected graph with edge set E. For a signing x \in \{-1, +1\}^n, the cut value is

\frac{1}{2} \sum_{ij \in E} (1 - x_i x_j).

It equals the number of edges whose endpoints have opposite signs. The semidefinite relaxation maximises the same expression with x_i x_j replaced by X_{ij}, subject to X_{ii} = 1 and X positive semidefinite.

The matrix xx^\top is feasible, so the semidefinite value is at least the integral value. The constraint X \succeq 0 is convex by ch. 1, and -\log\det is a barrier with parameter equal to the number of vertices.

Theorem 6.2 — The triangle relaxation equals 9/4

On the complete graph with three vertices, the semidefinite relaxation has optimal value 9/4 = 2.25. The matrix with ones on the diagonal and -1/2 off the diagonal is optimal. The integral maximum is 2.

Proof. For any feasible X, the sum of all entries is 1^\top X 1 \geq 0. The diagonal contributes 3, so the three off-diagonal entries sum to at least -3/2. The objective equals 3/2 - (1/2) times that off-diagonal sum, hence at most 3/2 + 3/4 = 9/4.

The stated matrix has off-diagonal sum -3/2, so it meets the bound. Its eigenvalues are 0, with eigenvector (1, 1, 1), and 3/2 on the plane orthogonal to that vector. It is positive semidefinite and feasible.

Every signing leaves at least one edge uncut, so the integral maximum is 2. ∎

Example 6.3 — Random hyperplane on the triangle

Factor the optimal matrix as a Gram matrix of three unit vectors in a plane, at angles of 120 degrees. Draw a standard Gaussian direction and assign each vertex the sign of the inner product of its vector with that direction.

For two unit vectors with inner product -1/2, the probability that a random hyperplane separates them equals \arccos(-1/2)/\pi = 2/3. Three edges therefore contribute an expected cut of 3 \times 2/3 = 2, which matches the integral optimum. The relaxation gap is real, and this rounding closes it in expectation on this graph.

Theorem 6.4 — Goemans-Williamson guarantee

For a graph with nonnegative edge weights, the random-hyperplane rounding of an optimal semidefinite solution returns a cut whose expected weight is at least \alpha times the semidefinite value, with \alpha > 0.878. The same factor therefore lower-bounds the expected weight relative to the integral optimum.

Proof. Sketch. An edge with Gram entry \rho is cut with probability \arccos(\rho)/\pi. Goemans and Williamson (1995) prove that this probability is at least \alpha(1-\rho)/2 for every correlation in [-1,1], where \alpha exceeds 0.878. Summing over edges gives the expectation bound. The triangle ratio 8/9 is about 0.889, above that factor. The constant is a worst-case guarantee, not the ratio on every graph. ∎

The nuclear norm is a convex envelope of rank. Candès and Recht (2009) use it for matrix completion, and d’Aspremont, El Ghaoui, Jordan and Lanckriet (2007) use a semidefinite lift for sparse principal components. Both admit dual certificates from ch. 2. The triangle is the small case where the gap is exact.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Max-cut over signings is nonconvex. Replacing xx^\top by a positive semidefinite matrix with diagonal 1 gives a convex program.
  • On the triangle the relaxation equals 2.25 and the integral optimum equals 2. The matrix with off-diagonal entries -1/2 is optimal and positive semidefinite.
  • Random hyperplane rounding cuts each triangle edge with probability 2/3, so the expected cut equals the integral optimum.
  • On a general nonnegative graph the same rounding has expectation at least 0.878 times the semidefinite value.

7. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is self-study, outside the 21 h. It points at questions that start from ch. 3 to ch. 6 and are not settled by the theorems above. The voice is that of a reading list. Each item names a paper with authors and a year. None of these notes claims to solve the question.

Lower bounds for first-order methods

Nemirovsky and Yudin (1983) built the black-box model in which a method sees only values and gradients. In that model the O(1/k^2) rate of Nesterov (1983) is optimal for smooth convex minimisation, up to the constant. Nesterov (2004), chapter 2, gives a matching lower bound. The open direction is not a faster method for the same class. It is a sharper reading of which extra structure, strong convexity, finite-sum form, or a constraint geometry, improves the bound.

Su, Boyd and Candès (2016) read the accelerated method as a differential equation. The equation makes the Lyapunov function of Theorem 4.5 visible, and it has produced many variants. Which of those variants remain stable when the gradient is noisy, or when the step size is only an estimate of 1/L, is still a design question rather than a closed theorem.

Constraints, barriers and projection-free steps

Short-step interior-point theory gives a precise Newton complexity. Combining that theory with the accelerated rates of ch. 4 is delicate. The barrier parameter and the smoothness constant do not live in the same norm. Practical solvers use long steps and heuristics for the step length. A full complexity theory for those long steps, with the same sharpness as Proposition 3.6, is not in the textbook statements above.

Frank and Wolfe’s conditional-gradient method replaces a projection by a linear minimisation over the feasible set. Jaggi (2013) revisited the method for sparse convex problems and gave rates under curvature assumptions. The method is attractive on the spectrahedron and on the simplex, where the linear minimisation is a top eigenvector or a coordinate search. Matching Nesterov’s rate with a projection-free method on a general spectrahedron remains open.

Large-scale and non-Euclidean first-order methods

Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal (2018) survey stochastic gradient methods for finite-sum learning objectives. The basic stochastic step is cheap and noisy. Variance reduction restores a linear rate on strongly convex finite sums, at the price of storing or recomputing gradients. How small the batch can be, once the loss is only convex and not strongly convex, is still sensitive to the accuracy one actually needs.

Mirror descent replaces the Euclidean proximal map by a Bregman divergence. The course description’s orthant, simplex and positive semidefinite cone all have natural barriers, and those barriers are also mirror maps. A unified account that specialises to soft-thresholding, to exponentiated gradient, and to a matrix entropy is available in the books of Nesterov and of Ben-Tal and Nemirovski. The open algorithmic question is acceleration of mirror descent under the same assumptions, with a rate that is optimal rather than only better than the unaccelerated one.

Relaxations that are tight enough

The triangle shows a relaxation gap one can compute by hand. On large graphs the Goemans-Williamson factor is the worst-case guarantee, and many instances are much tighter. Predicting the gap from the graph, without solving both the integral problem and the relaxation, is open. The same remark applies to the sparse principal-component semidefinite program of d’Aspremont, El Ghaoui, Jordan and Lanckriet (2007) and to the nuclear-norm model of Candès and Recht (2009).

Dual certificates explain tightness after the fact. They do not yet give a cheap a priori test for a new combinatorial problem. Building that test for a concrete family, for example cardinality-constrained portfolios or sparse principal components with a nonnegativity constraint, is a research project of the size of ch. 7’s time budget, not of a single exercise.

Questions to read toward

  1. Under what extra assumption does a constrained smooth convex problem admit a first-order method with the unconstrained accelerated rate?
  2. Can a projection-free method match that rate on the spectrahedron?
  3. How should the continuation parameter t of a long-step interior-point method be scheduled when the Newton system is approximate?
  4. Which dual certificate for basis pursuit degrades gracefully when the linear measurements are noisy, and what does the convex program return then?

Where to start

Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004), Nesterov (2004) and Ben-Tal and Nemirovski (2001) are the three books on the course page. Bubeck (2015) is a short map of complexity results. The alternating-direction method is developed at length by Boyd, Parikh, Chu, Peleato and Eckstein (2011) and in the related MVA course on distributed optimization. It is not proved in these notes.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • The accelerated 1/k^2 rate is optimal in the black-box model of Nemirovsky and Yudin. Extra structure is the way to go faster.
  • Long-step interior-point schedules and accelerated mirror descent are less settled than the short-step theorem of ch. 3.
  • Random hyperplane rounding has a worst-case factor near 0.878. Predicting the gap on one instance, before solving it, is open.
  • The reading list is the three official books plus the papers named in this chapter.

Exercises

Levels: ★ applies one result, ★★ combines two results or writes a short proof, ★★★ is open-ended or research-flavoured. Exercises 2 and 6 are NumPy checks. A solution or a hint follows each statement.

  1. ★ Convex sets from halfspaces. Show that an arbitrary intersection of convex sets is convex. Deduce that a polyhedron, cut out by finitely many affine equalities and inequalities, is convex. Conclude that the probability simplex is convex.

    Solution. A convex combination of points of the intersection lies in every set of the family, hence in the intersection. Each equality is an affine subspace and each inequality is a halfspace. Both are convex, so their intersection is convex. The simplex is the set of nonnegative vectors whose coordinates sum to 1.

  2. ★ A random positive definite quadratic. Draw a 4 \times 3 matrix with numpy.random.default_rng(0), form the Gram matrix, and evaluate \tfrac{1}{2} x^\top Q x at x = (1, -1, 1/2). Use the printed eigenvalue to decide convexity.

import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
G = rng.normal(size=(4, 3))
Q = G.T @ G
x = numpy.array([1.0, -1.0, 0.5])
value = 0.5 * float(x @ Q @ x)
lam_min = float(numpy.linalg.eigvalsh(Q).min())
print(round(value, 6))
print(round(lam_min, 6))
*Solution.* The fence prints `0.697525` and `0.112108`. The smallest eigenvalue is positive, so $Q$ is positive definite and the quadratic is strictly convex. Proposition 0.2 gives the eigenvalue test. The value $0.697525$ is the objective at the chosen $x$, not the minimum.

  1. ★ The exponential and its tangent. Show that f(t) = e^{t} is strictly convex by the second-derivative test. Write the tangent lower bound at t = 0 and check it numerically at t = 1.

    Solution. The second derivative equals the function, which is positive, so Proposition 1.2 gives strict convexity. The tangent at 0 reads e^{t} \geq 1 + t. At t = 1 this is e \geq 2, and e \approx 2.718 satisfies it. Equality in the tangent bound holds only at the expansion point.

  2. ★ Three convex programs. For least squares, logistic regression with labels in \{-1,+1\}, and a portfolio with a positive semidefinite covariance, name the variable, the objective and the constraints. Say which result of ch. 1 makes each objective convex.

    Solution. Least squares has variable w and objective \|Xw-y\|^2, with no constraint. The Hessian 2X^\top X is positive semidefinite, which is the second-order test of Proposition 1.2.

    Logistic regression uses that same variable and the loss of Example 1.5. The portfolio minimises a convex quadratic in the weight vector, subject to the weights summing to one. Nonnegativity is an extra convex constraint when short sales are forbidden.

  3. ★★ Dual of the minimum-norm point. Minimise \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^2 subject to Ax = b, with A of full row rank. Derive the dual function, the optimal multiplier, and the common optimal value.

    Solution. The Lagrangian is \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|^2 + \nu^\top(b - Ax). Stationarity gives x = A^\top \nu. The dual function is b^\top \nu - \tfrac{1}{2}\|A^\top \nu\|^2. Maximising it gives A A^\top \nu = b. The optimal value on both sides equals \tfrac{1}{2} b^\top (AA^\top)^{-1} b. Slater’s condition holds because the constraint is affine and feasible, so Theorem 2.3 gives a zero gap.

  4. ★★ A numerical zero gap. Build a random 2 \times 4 matrix and a feasible right-hand side with numpy.random.default_rng(0). Compute the primal and dual optimal values of Exercise 5 and compare them.

import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
A = rng.normal(size=(2, 4))
b = A @ rng.normal(size=4)
gram = A @ A.T
nu = numpy.linalg.solve(gram, b)
x = A.T @ nu
primal = 0.5 * float(x @ x)
dual = float(b @ nu) - 0.5 * float(nu @ gram @ nu)
print(round(primal, 6))
print(round(dual, 6))
*Solution.* The fence prints `0.13623` and `0.13623`. The two values agree to the printed precision, which is the zero gap predicted by Exercise 5. The vector $b$ is built as $A$ times a Gaussian vector, so the affine equation is feasible and the hypothesis of full row rank holds for this draw.

  1. ★★ The logarithm is self-concordant. Prove Theorem 3.5. Then minimise (x - 3)^2 on (0, \infty) and compute the central point at t = 1.

    Solution. The first three derivatives of -\log u are -1/u, 1/u^2 and -2/u^3. Both sides of the inequality equal 2|h|^3/u^3.

    The derivative of (x-3)^2-\log x vanishes when 2x^2-6x-1=0. The positive root is (3+\sqrt{11})/2, about 3.158, as in Example 3.4.

  2. ★★ One gradient step on a stretched quadratic. Prove that x^+ = x - \nabla f(x)/L decreases an L-smooth function by at least \|\nabla f(x)\|^2/(2L). Then take f(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 + 50 y^2 and step 1/100 from (1, 1).

    Solution. Substitute the step into Theorem 4.2. The linear term contributes -\|\nabla f\|^2/L, and the quadratic remainder puts half of that back, so the decrease is at least \|\nabla f\|^2/(2L).

    Here L=100 and the gradient at (1,1) is (1,100). The next point is (0.99, 0). The stiff coordinate vanishes in one step, and the soft coordinate moves by 0.01, as in Example 4.4.

  3. ★★★ Soft-thresholding, then a lasso trial. Apply the proximal map of Proposition 4.7, with \lambda = 1/2, to (1.2,\, -0.4,\, 0.3,\, -1.5). Then design a synthetic lasso trial and say what a success looks like.

    Hints and expected result. The image is (0.7,\, 0,\, 0,\, -1). Coordinates inside the threshold vanish. The others move toward zero by 1/2.

    For the trial, draw n = 40 rows and p = 100 columns with a fixed seed, plant a vector with 5 nonzeros, and add Gaussian noise. Run proximal gradient on the lasso of Definition 5.1 for several \lambda. A success at high signal-to-noise ratio is exact recovery of the planted support for an intermediate \lambda, and a failure is a support that grows to the whole dictionary when \lambda is tiny. Least squares is not a competitor with a unique answer, because p > n.

  4. ★★★ The triangle, from the matrix to the expectation. Prove Theorem 6.2. Then explain why a random hyperplane cuts 2 edges in expectation, and why the factor 0.878 is not the ratio on this graph.

    Solution. The proof in ch. 6 uses 1^\top X 1 \geq 0 to cap the objective by 9/4, then checks the matrix with off-diagonal -1/2. Separation probability \arccos(-1/2)/\pi = 2/3, so three edges give expectation 2, equal to the integral optimum.

    The ratio of integral value to semidefinite value is 2/2.25 = 8/9 \approx 0.889. Theorem 6.4 only claims a worst-case factor above 0.878. This graph is easier than the worst case. A research-flavoured extension is to compute the same two numbers on a cycle of length 5 and compare them with the triangle.

Written exam (proposal)

Proposal only. Two hours, one handwritten sheet, no electronic device. Three questions, 20 points. The catalogue does not store a duration or a percentage.

  1. Conceptual question (6 points). Say which programs are convex, and why.

    • Minimise the squared residual \|Xw - y\|^2 over the weight vector.
    • Minimise the number of nonzeros of w subject to Xw = y.
    • Minimise w^\top \Sigma w subject to weights summing to 1 and to w \geq 0, with \Sigma positive semidefinite.

    Expected: (a) is convex because the Hessian 2X^\top X is positive semidefinite. (b) is not convex. The vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1) each have one nonzero, and their midpoint has two. (c) is a convex quadratic program. The objective has positive semidefinite Hessian and the constraints are linear.

  2. Computation (8 points). Minimise \tfrac{1}{2} x^2 subject to x \geq 1. Write the Lagrangian, the dual function, the dual maximiser, the primal minimiser, and the KKT pair.

    Expected: The Lagrangian is \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 + \lambda(1-x) for \lambda \geq 0. The dual function is \lambda - \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^2, maximised at \lambda = 1 with value 1/2.

    The primal minimiser is x = 1 with the same value. Stationarity reads x = \lambda, and complementary slackness holds because the constraint is active. This is Example 2.5.

  3. Short proof (6 points). Prove weak duality for a convex program in the standard form of Definition 2.1.

    Expected: Take \lambda \geq 0 and a feasible x. Then each \lambda_i f_i(x) is nonpositive and Ax = b, so the Lagrangian is at most f_0(x). The dual function is an infimum of the Lagrangian, hence at most f_0(x). Therefore every dual value is at most every primal value. The argument is Proposition 2.2.
