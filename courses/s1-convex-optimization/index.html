Audience: a student with a good M1 in mathematics, computer science or physics who has already run gradient descent or called an optimization solver, and now wants to recognise convex problems, write their duals and know what the algorithms guarantee.

Empirical first: on a least-squares problem in dimension 500 with condition number 10^4, a thousand steps of gradient descent bring the gap f - f^* from 23.9 down to 0.012. Nesterov’s accelerated method uses the same thousand gradients, plus one line of code, and reaches 3.9 \times 10^{-6} (code in ch. 5).

An interior-point method solves a linear program with 100 constraints to eight digits in 54 Newton steps (ch. 6). Why the first method is slow, why the second is optimal, and why the third barely depends on the data are the questions of this course.

The course teaches how to recognise a convex problem, how to rewrite it in one of a few standard forms (linear, quadratic, second-order cone and semidefinite programs), how to read its dual, and how the two families of algorithms that solve it work: first-order methods, which scale to huge problems, and interior-point methods, which reach high accuracy in few iterations. It ends with applications in statistics, learning, finance and signal processing, and with convex relaxations of combinatorial problems.

It shares tools with Introduction to Statistical Learning and Foundations of Distributed and Large-Scale Computing Optimization, and prepares Kernel Methods, Inverse Problems and Imaging and Sequential Learning.

What you will be able to do

  • Recognise convex sets and functions, and prove convexity with the operations that preserve it.
  • Compute projections, conjugate functions, subgradients and proximal operators of the functions used in machine learning.
  • Rewrite a modelling problem as a linear, quadratic, second-order cone or semidefinite program.
  • Derive the Lagrange dual of a convex program, check Slater’s condition, and solve the KKT conditions of small problems by hand.
  • Prove the convergence rates of gradient, subgradient, proximal and accelerated methods, and explain why Nesterov’s rate cannot be improved.
  • Implement Newton’s method and a barrier interior-point method, and bound their number of iterations with self-concordance.
  • Formulate the LASSO, logistic regression, portfolio selection, robust constraints, total-variation denoising and sparse recovery as convex programs, and solve them.
  • Build the semidefinite relaxation of a combinatorial problem and round it, with the Goemans–Williamson guarantee for MAXCUT.
  • Read dual variables as certificates of optimality and as sensitivities of the optimal value.

0. Review: norms, symmetric matrices and smooth functions

Convex optimization uses three M1 tools constantly: norms and their duals, symmetric matrices and their eigenvalues, and Taylor expansions of smooth functions. This chapter states them in the form used later.

The Linear Algebra, Analysis and Optimization refresher covers the same ground at a slower pace.

Norms and dual norms

Definition 0.1 — Dual norm For a norm \|\cdot\| on \mathbb{R}^n, the dual norm is \|y\|_* = \sup_{\|x\| \le 1} y^\top x . By construction y^\top x \le \|x\| \, \|y\|_* for all x, y (Hölder’s inequality).

The Euclidean norm is its own dual. The dual of \|\cdot\|_1 is \|\cdot\|_\infty and conversely, and the dual of \|\cdot\|_p is \|\cdot\|_q with 1/p + 1/q = 1.

Symmetric matrices

Proposition 0.2 — Spectral theorem and positive semidefinite matrices Every symmetric matrix A \in \mathbb{S}^n writes A = U \Lambda U^\top with U orthogonal and \Lambda real diagonal. Consequently, for all x, \lambda_{\min}(A) \, \|x\|_2^2 \le x^\top A x \le \lambda_{\max}(A) \, \|x\|_2^2 . A is positive semidefinite, A \succeq 0, when x^\top A x \ge 0 for all x, that is when all its eigenvalues are nonnegative; A \succ 0 when they are positive.

The inner product on \mathbb{S}^n is \langle A, B \rangle = \mathrm{tr}(AB).

Proposition 0.3 — Schur complement Let C \succ 0. Then \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ B^\top & C \end{pmatrix} \succeq 0 \iff A - B C^{-1} B^\top \succeq 0 .

Proof. The block matrix equals P^\top \, \mathrm{diag}(A - BC^{-1}B^\top, C) \, P with the invertible matrix P = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ C^{-1} B^\top & I \end{pmatrix} . A congruence by an invertible matrix preserves positive semidefiniteness, and a block-diagonal matrix is positive semidefinite exactly when its blocks are. ∎

Gradients, Hessians and smoothness

Definition 0.4 — Taylor’s formula with integral remainder For f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R} twice continuously differentiable, f(y) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \int_0^1 (1 - s) \, (y - x)^\top \nabla^2 f(x + s(y - x)) \, (y - x) \, ds , \nabla f(y) - \nabla f(x) = \int_0^1 \nabla^2 f(x + s(y - x)) \, (y - x) \, ds . Two gradients are used throughout: \nabla \tfrac12 \|Ax - b\|_2^2 = A^\top (Ax - b), and \nabla \log\det X = X^{-1} on positive definite matrices.

Proposition 0.5 — Descent lemma If \nabla f is L-Lipschitz, that is \|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\|_2 \le L \|x - y\|_2, then for all x, y f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \frac{L}{2} \|y - x\|_2^2 . Such an f is called L-smooth.

Proof. By the fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment, f(y) - f(x) - \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) = \int_0^1 \big(\nabla f(x + s(y - x)) - \nabla f(x)\big)^\top (y - x) \, ds . By Cauchy–Schwarz and the Lipschitz bound, the integrand is at most L s \|y - x\|_2^2, whose integral is \frac{L}{2}\|y - x\|_2^2. ∎

Optimality without inequality constraints

Proposition 0.6 — Fermat’s rule and Lagrange multipliers If x^* is a local minimum of a differentiable f on \mathbb{R}^n, then \nabla f(x^*) = 0, and \nabla^2 f(x^*) \succeq 0 if f is twice differentiable.

Now add the constraint h(x) = 0, with h : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^p continuously differentiable.

If x^* is a local minimum of f under this constraint, and the Jacobian of h at x^* has rank p, there exists \nu \in \mathbb{R}^p with \nabla f(x^*) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j \nabla h_j(x^*) = 0 . These conditions are only necessary. For convex problems they become sufficient (ch. 2 and 4), which is the first reason convexity matters.

Example 0.7 — A badly conditioned quadratic Let f(x) = \frac12 (x_1^2 + 100 x_2^2). Its gradient is (x_1, 100 x_2) and its Hessian is \mathrm{diag}(1, 100).

So f is L-smooth with L = 100, and the minimizer is 0.

The gradient step x - \nabla f(x)/L maps (x_1, x_2) to (0.99 \, x_1, \ 0) . The steep coordinate is solved in one step, but the flat one shrinks by only 1 % per step: about 460 steps to divide it by 100. The ratio 100 of the extreme curvatures, the condition number, sets the speed of gradient methods (ch. 5).

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The dual norm turns y^\top x into a product of norms; \ell_1 and \ell_\infty are dual, \ell_2 is self-dual.
  • Positive semidefiniteness is read on eigenvalues, and block matrices are tested by Schur complements.
  • Taylor’s formula with integral remainder gives the descent lemma: an L-smooth function lies below a quadratic.
  • Stationarity is necessary for a minimum; convexity will make it sufficient.

1. Convex sets

Every convex program optimizes over a convex set, and every duality argument rests on separating convex sets by hyperplanes. This chapter lists the convex sets used in the course, the operations that preserve convexity, and the two geometric tools: projection and separation.

Definition 1.1 — Convex set, cone, convex hull A set C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n is convex if \theta x + (1 - \theta) y \in C for all x, y \in C and \theta \in [0, 1].

It is a cone if \theta x \in C for all x \in C and \theta \ge 0, and a convex cone if it is both.

The convex hull \mathrm{conv}\, S is the set of convex combinations \sum_i \theta_i x_i of points of S, with \theta_i \ge 0 and \sum_i \theta_i = 1. It is the smallest convex set containing S.

Example 1.2 — The catalogue

  • Hyperplanes \{x : a^\top x = b\} and halfspaces \{x : a^\top x \le b\}, with a \neq 0.
  • Norm balls \{x : \|x - x_c\| \le r\} and ellipsoids \{x_c + P u : \|u\|_2 \le 1\}.
  • Polyhedra \{x : Ax \preceq b, \ Cx = d\}, where \preceq is componentwise; the probability simplex is one.
  • The second-order cone \{(u, t) : \|u\|_2 \le t\}.
  • The positive semidefinite cone \mathbb{S}^n_+. It is convex because it is the intersection, over all z, of the halfspaces \{X : z^\top X z \ge 0\}.

Proposition 1.3 — Operations that preserve convexity

  • Intersection of any family of convex sets.
  • Image and inverse image under an affine map x \mapsto Ax + b.
  • Image and inverse image under the perspective map (z, t) \mapsto z/t on t > 0, and under linear-fractional maps.

For example, the solution set of a linear matrix inequality, \{x : x_1 A_1 + \dots + x_n A_n \preceq B\} with symmetric A_i and B, is convex. It is the inverse image of the cone \mathbb{S}^m_+ under the affine map x \mapsto B - \sum_i x_i A_i.

Proof. For affine images: if y_1 = Ax_1 + b and y_2 = Ax_2 + b with x_1, x_2 \in C, then \theta y_1 + (1 - \theta) y_2 = A\big(\theta x_1 + (1 - \theta) x_2\big) + b is the image of a point of C. Inverse images and intersections are checked the same way; for perspectives, see Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004, §2.3). ∎

Projection and separation

Theorem 1.4 — Projection onto a closed convex set Let C be nonempty, closed and convex, and x \in \mathbb{R}^n. There is a unique point P_C(x) \in C closest to x in Euclidean norm. It is characterised by \big(x - P_C(x)\big)^\top \big(z - P_C(x)\big) \le 0 \quad \text{for all } z \in C . Moreover P_C is non-expansive: \|P_C(x) - P_C(y)\|_2 \le \|x - y\|_2 .

Proof.

  • Existence: for any z_0 \in C, minimize the continuous function z \mapsto \|x - z\|_2 on the compact set of points of C at distance at most \|x - z_0\|_2 from x.
  • Characterization: let p = P_C(x), z \in C and \theta \in (0, 1]. The point p + \theta (z - p) is in C, so expanding \|x - p - \theta(z - p)\|_2^2 \ge \|x - p\|_2^2 gives -2\theta \, (x - p)^\top (z - p) + \theta^2 \|z - p\|_2^2 \ge 0 . Divide by \theta and let \theta \to 0. Conversely, if the inequality holds, then for every z \in C \|x - z\|^2 = \|x - p\|^2 - 2(x - p)^\top (z - p) + \|z - p\|^2 \ge \|x - p\|^2 .
  • Uniqueness and non-expansiveness: with p = P_C(x) and q = P_C(y), add the characterizations (x - p)^\top (q - p) \le 0 and (y - q)^\top (p - q) \le 0. This gives \|p - q\|_2^2 \le (x - y)^\top (p - q) \le \|x - y\|_2 \, \|p - q\|_2 . With x = y it forces p = q. ∎

Theorem 1.5 — Separating and supporting hyperplanes

  1. If C is closed and convex and x_0 \notin C, there are a \neq 0 and b with a^\top z \le b < a^\top x_0 for all z \in C (strict separation).
  2. If C and D are disjoint nonempty convex sets, there are a \ne 0 and b such that a^\top x \le b \ \text{ on } C, \qquad a^\top x \ge b \ \text{ on } D .
  3. At every boundary point x_0 of a convex set C there is a supporting hyperplane: some a \neq 0 with a^\top z \le a^\top x_0 for all z \in C.

Consequently, a closed convex set is the intersection of the halfspaces that contain it.

Proof. Part 1: let p = P_C(x_0) and a = x_0 - p \neq 0. Theorem 1.4 gives a^\top z \le a^\top p for all z \in C, and a^\top x_0 - a^\top p = \|a\|_2^2 > 0. Take b = a^\top p.

Part 3: take points x_k outside the closure of C converging to x_0, separate them by Part 1 with unit normals a_k, and extract a convergent subsequence of the a_k.

Part 2: apply the same limiting argument to the convex set C - D, which does not contain 0 (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004, §2.5). ∎

Definition 1.6 — Dual cone and generalized inequality The dual cone of a cone K is K^* = \{ y : y^\top x \ge 0 \text{ for all } x \in K \} . A closed convex cone K with nonempty interior that contains no line is called proper. It defines the generalized inequality x \preceq_K y \iff y - x \in K.

Proposition 1.7 — Self-dual cones The nonnegative orthant, the second-order cone and \mathbb{S}^n_+ are self-dual: K^* = K. The dual of the norm cone \{(x, t) : \|x\| \le t\} is the cone \{(y, s) : \|y\|_* \le s\} of the dual norm.

Proof. For \mathbb{S}^n_+: if X, Y \succeq 0, then \mathrm{tr}(XY) = \mathrm{tr}(X^{1/2} Y X^{1/2}) \ge 0, so \mathbb{S}^n_+ \subseteq (\mathbb{S}^n_+)^*.

Conversely, if \mathrm{tr}(XY) \ge 0 for all X \succeq 0, take X = z z^\top: then z^\top Y z \ge 0 for all z, so Y \succeq 0. ∎

Example 1.8 — Projection onto the simplex The projection onto the probability simplex \Delta = \{x \succeq 0, \ \mathbf{1}^\top x = 1\} has the form P_\Delta(v) = \max(v - \tau \mathbf{1}, \ 0) , where the threshold \tau makes the result sum to 1. Sorting v finds \tau in O(n \log n) operations.

The code checks the characterization of Theorem 1.4 on 10 000 random points of the simplex: the largest value of (v - P_\Delta(v))^\top (z - P_\Delta(v)) is -6.2 \times 10^{-4}, negative as it must be.

import numpy as np

def proj_simplex(v):
    """Euclidean projection of v onto {x >= 0, sum(x) = 1} (sort-based)."""
    u = np.sort(v)[::-1]
    css = np.cumsum(u) - 1.0
    k = np.arange(1, v.size + 1)
    rho = np.nonzero(u - css / k > 0)[0][-1]   # last index with a positive entry
    tau = css[rho] / (rho + 1)
    return np.maximum(v - tau, 0.0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
v = rng.normal(size=6)
p = proj_simplex(v)
print("v      =", np.round(v, 3))
print("P(v)   =", np.round(p, 3), " sum =", p.sum().round(12))

# Check the obtuse-angle characterization (Theorem 1.4) on random simplex points
Z = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(6), size=10_000)
print("max_z (v - P(v))^T (z - P(v)) =", ((Z - p) @ (v - p)).max().round(12))

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Halfspaces, balls, ellipsoids, polyhedra, the second-order cone and the semidefinite cone are the building blocks; intersections and affine maps preserve convexity.
  • The projection onto a closed convex set exists, is unique, is characterised by an obtuse-angle inequality, and is non-expansive.
  • Separation and supporting hyperplanes follow from the projection; they are the engine of duality in ch. 4.
  • The orthant, the second-order cone and the semidefinite cone are self-dual, which is why their duals look like the primals.

2. Convex functions and convex analysis

Convex functions are the objectives and constraints of convex programs. This chapter gives the tests that recognise them, the operations that build new ones, and the three objects of convex analysis that every algorithm uses: the conjugate, the subdifferential, and the smoothness and strong-convexity constants.

Definition 2.1 — Convex, strictly convex and strongly convex functions A function f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\} with convex domain \mathrm{dom}\, f = \{x : f(x) < \infty\} is convex if, for x, y \in \mathrm{dom}\, f and \theta \in [0, 1], f(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y) \le \theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y) . Equivalently, its epigraph \{(x, t) : f(x) \le t\} is a convex set.

It is strictly convex if the inequality is strict for x \neq y and \theta \in (0, 1), and \mu-strongly convex (\mu > 0) if f - \frac{\mu}{2}\|\cdot\|_2^2 is convex. It is concave if -f is convex.

Theorem 2.2 — First- and second-order conditions Let f be differentiable on an open convex domain. Then f is convex if and only if f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) \quad \text{for all } x, y . If f is twice differentiable, f is convex if and only if \nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0 for all x.

The tangent plane is a global under-estimator: local information gives a global bound. In particular, \nabla f(x^*) = 0 makes x^* a global minimizer.

Proof.

  • (\Rightarrow) For \theta \in (0, 1], convexity gives f(x + \theta(y - x)) \le f(x) + \theta (f(y) - f(x)). Subtract f(x), divide by \theta and let \theta \to 0.
  • (\Leftarrow) Let z = \theta x + (1 - \theta) y. Write the inequality at z towards both points, multiply by \theta and 1 - \theta, and add. The gradient terms cancel, leaving \theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y) \ge f(z) .
  • Second order: if \nabla^2 f \succeq 0, the remainder in Taylor’s formula (Definition 0.4) is nonnegative, which is the first-order condition.
  • Conversely, g(s) = f(x + s v) is convex. Adding the first-order inequality at s towards 0 and at 0 towards s gives (g'(s) - g'(0)) s \ge 0. So g' is nondecreasing, and g''(0) = v^\top \nabla^2 f(x) v \ge 0 . ∎

Example 2.3 — A catalogue of convex functions

  • On \mathbb{R}: the exponential e^{ax}, the negative logarithm -\log x and the negative entropy x \log x.
  • The powers x^p on x > 0, for p \ge 1 or p \le 0.
  • Every norm, by the triangle inequality and homogeneity.
  • \max_i x_i, and its smooth version log-sum-exp f(x) = \log \sum_i e^{x_i}, which satisfies \max_i x_i \le f(x) \le \max_i x_i + \log n .
  • The quadratic-over-linear function x^2 / y on y > 0, and -\log\det X on positive definite matrices.

Proposition 2.4 — Two Hessian computations Log-sum-exp is convex, and \log\det is concave on positive definite matrices.

Proof.

  • With z_i = e^{x_i}, the Hessian of log-sum-exp is \nabla^2 f(x) = \frac{\mathrm{diag}(z)}{\mathbf{1}^\top z} - \frac{z z^\top}{(\mathbf{1}^\top z)^2} , and v^\top \nabla^2 f(x) v \ge 0 is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (\sum_i z_i v_i)^2 \le (\sum_i z_i)(\sum_i z_i v_i^2).
  • For \log\det, restrict to a line X = Z + tV with Z \succ 0. With \lambda_i the eigenvalues of Z^{-1/2} V Z^{-1/2}, \log\det(Z + tV) = \log\det Z + \sum_i \log(1 + t \lambda_i) , a sum of concave functions of t. ∎

Proposition 2.5 — Operations that preserve convexity

  • Nonnegative weighted sums, and composition with an affine map, x \mapsto f(Ax + b).
  • Pointwise supremum f(x) = \sup_{y \in \mathcal{A}} g(x, y) of functions convex in x: its epigraph is an intersection of epigraphs. Example: \lambda_{\max}(X) = \sup_{\|y\|_2 = 1} y^\top X y is convex.
  • Composition h(g(x)) with h convex and nondecreasing and g convex (and the other sign rules of the composition calculus).
  • Partial minimization g(x) = \inf_{y \in C} f(x, y) of a jointly convex f over a convex set C, when g > -\infty. Example: the distance to a convex set.
  • Perspective (x, t) \mapsto t f(x / t) on t > 0.

Jensen’s inequality f(\mathbb{E} X) \le \mathbb{E} f(X) extends Definition 2.1 to all probability distributions.

Proof. For partial minimization: given x_1, x_2 and \varepsilon > 0, pick y_i \in C with f(x_i, y_i) \le g(x_i) + \varepsilon. Joint convexity gives g(\theta x_1 + (1 - \theta) x_2) \le f\big(\theta x_1 + (1 - \theta) x_2, \ \theta y_1 + (1 - \theta) y_2\big) \le \theta g(x_1) + (1 - \theta) g(x_2) + \varepsilon . Let \varepsilon \to 0. ∎

Conjugate functions

Definition 2.6 — Convex conjugate The conjugate of f is f^*(y) = \sup_{x \in \mathrm{dom} f} \big( y^\top x - f(x) \big) . It is convex, as a supremum of affine functions of y, even when f is not. It satisfies the Fenchel–Young inequality f(x) + f^*(y) \ge x^\top y.

If f is convex and closed (lower semicontinuous), then f^{**} = f: this is the Fenchel–Moreau theorem (Rockafellar 1970, §12).

Subgradients

Definition 2.8 — Subgradient and subdifferential A vector g is a subgradient of a convex f at x if f(y) \ge f(x) + g^\top (y - x) \quad \text{for all } y . The set \partial f(x) of subgradients is the subdifferential. It is closed and convex, it equals \{\nabla f(x)\} where f is differentiable, and it is nonempty at every interior point of the domain: apply the supporting hyperplane theorem (Theorem 1.5) to the epigraph at (x, f(x)).

Proposition 2.9 — Fermat’s rule and subdifferential calculus

  • x^* minimizes a convex f if and only if 0 \in \partial f(x^*), by the definition.
  • The subdifferential of a sum contains the sum of the subdifferentials, with equality when the relative interiors of the domains intersect (Rockafellar 1970, §23): \partial (f_1 + f_2)(x) \supseteq \partial f_1(x) + \partial f_2(x) .
  • \partial |x| = \{\mathrm{sign}(x)\} for x \neq 0 and [-1, 1] at 0; \partial \|x\|_1 is the product of these sets.
  • For any norm, \partial \|x\| = \{ g : \|g\|_* \le 1, \ g^\top x = \|x\| \}.
  • For f = \max_i f_i with differentiable convex f_i, \partial f(x) is the convex hull of the gradients of the f_i that attain the maximum.

Smoothness and strong convexity

Proposition 2.10 — Two-sided quadratic bounds If f is L-smooth (Proposition 0.5) and \mu-strongly convex, then for all x, y \frac{\mu}{2} \|y - x\|_2^2 \le f(y) - f(x) - \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) \le \frac{L}{2} \|y - x\|_2^2 . For twice differentiable f this reads \mu I \preceq \nabla^2 f \preceq L I. The ratio \kappa = L / \mu is the condition number.

Minimizing both bounds over y gives, with f^* = \min f, \frac{1}{2L} \|\nabla f(x)\|_2^2 \le f(x) - f^* \le \frac{1}{2\mu} \|\nabla f(x)\|_2^2 . The left inequality needs only smoothness; it says that a large gradient means a large gap.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Convexity is tested by the tangent inequality or by a positive semidefinite Hessian; restricting to lines reduces matrix functions to one variable.
  • Sums, affine compositions, suprema, partial minimization and perspectives preserve convexity: this calculus is how convexity is proved in practice.
  • The conjugate is always convex; its table turns into a table of duals in ch. 4.
  • Subgradients extend gradients to nonsmooth functions, and 0 \in \partial f(x^*) characterizes minimizers.
  • The constants L and \mu sandwich f between two quadratics; their ratio \kappa governs first-order methods.

3. Convex optimization problems

A convex program minimizes a convex function over a set described by convex inequalities and affine equalities. This chapter gives the standard form, the property that makes these problems tractable (every local minimum is global), and the hierarchy of conic programs into which modelling translates applied problems.

Definition 3.1 — Convex program in standard form \begin{array}{ll} \text{minimize} & f_0(x) \\ \text{subject to} & f_i(x) \le 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, m, \\ & Ax = b , \end{array} with f_0, \dots, f_m convex. The optimal value p^* is the infimum of f_0 over the feasible set: +\infty if it is empty, -\infty if f_0 is unbounded below on it.

A feasible x is \varepsilon-suboptimal if f_0(x) \le p^* + \varepsilon.

Theorem 3.2 — Local minima are global; optimality condition For a convex program, every locally optimal point is globally optimal, and the optimal set is convex. If f_0 is differentiable, a feasible x is optimal if and only if \nabla f_0(x)^\top (y - x) \ge 0 \quad \text{for every feasible } y .

Proof.

  • Let x be locally optimal and suppose a feasible y has f_0(y) < f_0(x). The points x + \theta (y - x) are feasible, and by convexity, for every \theta \in (0, 1], f_0\big(x + \theta (y - x)\big) \le (1 - \theta) f_0(x) + \theta f_0(y) < f_0(x) . Arbitrarily small \theta gives points arbitrarily close to x with a smaller value, which contradicts local optimality.
  • The optimal set is the intersection of the feasible set with the sublevel set \{f_0 \le p^*\}, two convex sets.
  • Sufficiency of the condition is Theorem 2.2: f_0(y) \ge f_0(x) + \nabla f_0(x)^\top (y - x) \ge f_0(x).
  • Necessity: if \nabla f_0(x)^\top (y - x) < 0 for a feasible y, small steps from x towards y stay feasible and decrease f_0. ∎

Equivalent problems

Modelling means rewriting a problem until a standard form appears. Four transformations are used constantly.

  • Epigraph form: minimize t subject to f_0(x) \le t and the constraints, which makes the objective linear.
  • Slack variables: an affine inequality a^\top x \le b becomes a^\top x + s = b with s \ge 0.
  • Splitting: x = x^+ - x^- with x^\pm \succeq 0 linearizes absolute values.
  • New variables: \min_x \sum_i |a_i^\top x - b_i| becomes the linear program \text{minimize } \mathbf{1}^\top t \quad \text{s.t.} \quad -t \preceq Ax - b \preceq t .

The conic hierarchy

Definition 3.3 — LP, QP, SOCP, SDP

  • Linear program (LP): minimize c^\top x subject to Gx \preceq h and Ax = b.
  • Quadratic program (QP): minimize \frac12 x^\top P x + q^\top x with P \succeq 0, under affine constraints. A QCQP also allows convex quadratic constraints.
  • Second-order cone program (SOCP): minimize f^\top x subject to Fx = g and \|A_i x + b_i\|_2 \le c_i^\top x + d_i , \quad i = 1, \dots, m .
  • Semidefinite program (SDP): minimize c^\top x subject to Ax = b and the linear matrix inequality x_1 F_1 + \dots + x_n F_n + G \preceq 0 , \qquad F_i, G \in \mathbb{S}^k . Its standard form is: minimize \mathrm{tr}(CX) subject to \mathrm{tr}(A_i X) = b_i and X \succeq 0.

Each class contains the previous one: LP \subset QP \subset QCQP \subset SOCP \subset SDP.

Proposition 3.4 — From quadratic to cone to semidefinite constraints

  • A convex quadratic constraint x^\top P x + s(x) \le 0, with P = R^\top R and s(x) = q^\top x + r affine, is the second-order cone constraint \left\| \begin{pmatrix} 2 R x \\ 1 + s(x) \end{pmatrix} \right\|_2 \le 1 - s(x) .
  • A second-order cone constraint is a linear matrix inequality: \|u\|_2 \le t \iff \begin{pmatrix} t I & u \\ u^\top & t \end{pmatrix} \succeq 0 .

Proof.

  • Both sides of the cone constraint are nonnegative, so it is equivalent to its square, 4\|Rx\|_2^2 + (1 + s)^2 \le (1 - s)^2, that is \|Rx\|_2^2 \le -s.
  • For t > 0, the Schur complement (Proposition 0.3) with respect to the scalar block t gives tI - u u^\top / t \succeq 0, that is t^2 \ge \|u\|_2^2.
  • For t = 0, both sides force u = 0: a positive semidefinite matrix with a zero diagonal entry has a zero row. For t < 0 both sides fail. ∎

Example 3.5 — Modelling with the hierarchy

  • \ell_\infty regression \min \|Ax - b\|_\infty is the LP: minimize t subject to -t \mathbf{1} \preceq Ax - b \preceq t \mathbf{1}.
  • Least squares with bounds on x is a QP.
  • A robust constraint a^\top x \le b for every a in the ellipsoid \{\bar a + P u : \|u\|_2 \le 1\} is an SOC constraint. Indeed \sup_{\|u\|_2 \le 1} (Pu)^\top x = \|P^\top x\|_2, so it reads \bar a^\top x + \|P^\top x\|_2 \le b .
  • Minimizing the largest eigenvalue \lambda_{\max}(A_0 + \sum_i x_i A_i) is the SDP: minimize t subject to A_0 + \sum_i x_i A_i \preceq t I.

Example 3.6 — The Chebyshev centre of a polyhedron is an LP The ball \{x_c + u : \|u\|_2 \le r\} lies in the halfspace a_i^\top x \le b_i if and only if a_i^\top x_c + r \|a_i\|_2 \le b_i, because the supremum of a_i^\top u over the ball is r \|a_i\|_2 (Definition 0.1). Maximizing r under these linear constraints is an LP.

For the triangle x \succeq 0, x_1 + x_2 \le 4, cut by x_1 - 2 x_2 \le 2, the code finds the incircle of the triangle: x_c = (1.1716, \ 1.1716), \qquad r = 4 - 2\sqrt{2} = 1.1716 . The fourth constraint is inactive (slack 0.55) and the solver reports a zero dual variable for it, a fact that complementary slackness will explain.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

# Polyhedron {x : a_i^T x <= b_i}: a triangle cut by a fourth half-plane
A = np.array([[-1.0, 0.0], [0.0, -1.0], [1.0, 1.0], [1.0, -2.0]])
b = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 4.0, 2.0])

# Chebyshev centre: maximize r s.t. a_i^T x + r ||a_i||_2 <= b_i  (variables x1, x2, r)
norms = np.linalg.norm(A, axis=1)
res = linprog(c=[0, 0, -1], A_ub=np.c_[A, norms], b_ub=b,
              bounds=[(None, None), (None, None), (0, None)], method="highs")
xc, r = res.x[:2], res.x[2]
print("centre =", np.round(xc, 4), " radius =", round(r, 4))
print("slacks b - A xc - r||a|| =", np.round(b - A @ xc - r * norms, 4))
print("dual variables (marginals) =", np.round(-res.ineqlin.marginals, 4))

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • In a convex program every local minimum is global, and optimality is a first-order condition on feasible directions.
  • Epigraphs, slacks, splitting and auxiliary variables turn applied problems into standard forms.
  • LP, QP, SOCP and SDP form a nested hierarchy; Schur complements move constraints up the hierarchy.
  • Robust and norm constraints are second-order cone constraints; eigenvalue constraints are semidefinite.

4. Duality

Every optimization problem comes with a dual: a concave maximization whose value bounds the primal optimum from below. When the bound is tight, the dual variables certify optimality, measure the sensitivity of the optimum to the constraints, and often give a simpler problem to solve.

Definition 4.1 — Lagrangian and dual function For the problem of Definition 3.1, convex or not, the Lagrangian is L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f_0(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i f_i(x) + \nu^\top (Ax - b) , and the dual function is g(\lambda, \nu) = \inf_x L(x, \lambda, \nu), the infimum being over the common domain \mathcal{D} of the f_i. As a pointwise infimum of affine functions of (\lambda, \nu), g is concave.

The dual problem maximizes g(\lambda, \nu) over \lambda \succeq 0; its value is d^*.

Proposition 4.2 — Weak duality For every \lambda \succeq 0 and every \nu, g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^*. Hence d^* \le p^* .

Proof. For feasible x, each \lambda_i f_i(x) \le 0 and Ax - b = 0, so g(\lambda, \nu) \le L(x, \lambda, \nu) \le f_0(x). Take the infimum over feasible x. ∎

For a feasible x and a dual feasible (\lambda, \nu), the duality gap f_0(x) - g(\lambda, \nu) bounds the suboptimality of x. It is the stopping criterion of the solvers of ch. 6.

Example 4.3 — Three duals

  • LP in standard form: minimize c^\top x subject to Ax = b, x \succeq 0. The Lagrangian c^\top x - \lambda^\top x + \nu^\top (Ax - b) is affine in x.

    It is bounded below only if c + A^\top \nu - \lambda = 0. With y = -\nu, the dual is \text{maximize } b^\top y \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A^\top y \preceq c .

  • Least norm: minimize \|x\|_2^2 subject to Ax = b. The Lagrangian is minimized at x = -A^\top \nu / 2, and g(\nu) = -\tfrac14 \nu^\top A A^\top \nu - b^\top \nu .

  • Through conjugates: for minimize f_0(x) subject to Ax \preceq b and Cx = d, g(\lambda, \nu) = -b^\top \lambda - d^\top \nu - f_0^*(-A^\top \lambda - C^\top \nu) , so the table of conjugates (Example 2.7) is a table of duals.

Theorem 4.4 — Slater’s theorem Suppose the problem is convex and satisfies Slater’s condition: some \tilde x in the relative interior of \mathcal{D} is strictly feasible, f_i(\tilde x) < 0 for all i and A \tilde x = b. Then strong duality holds, and the dual optimum is attained when p^* is finite: d^* = p^* . Affine inequalities need not hold strictly; in particular a feasible LP with finite value has a zero duality gap.

Proof. We prove it with inequality constraints only and p^* finite; equality constraints add a relative-interior argument (Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004, §5.3.2).

  • Consider the set of pairs (u, t) achievable by some point of the domain: \mathcal{A} = \{ (u, t) : \exists x \in \mathcal{D}, \ f_i(x) \le u_i \text{ for all } i, \ f_0(x) \le t \} . It is convex because the f_i are. It does not meet \mathcal{B} = \{(0, s) : s < p^*\}.
  • Theorem 1.5 gives (\tilde\lambda, \mu) \ne 0 and \alpha with \tilde\lambda^\top u + \mu t \ge \alpha on \mathcal{A} and \mu t \le \alpha on \mathcal{B}.
  • \mathcal{A} is unbounded upwards in every coordinate, so \tilde\lambda \succeq 0 and \mu \ge 0. Letting s \to p^* in \mathcal{B} gives \mu p^* \le \alpha.
  • If \mu > 0, take (u, t) = (f(x), f_0(x)) and divide by \mu. This gives, for all x \in \mathcal{D}, L(x, \tilde\lambda / \mu) \ge p^* . So g(\tilde\lambda / \mu) \ge p^*, and weak duality makes \tilde\lambda / \mu dual optimal with d^* = p^*.
  • If \mu = 0, then \tilde\lambda \neq 0, \alpha \ge 0, and \sum_i \tilde\lambda_i f_i(x) \ge 0 for all x \in \mathcal{D}. At the Slater point the left-hand side is negative: a contradiction. ∎

Example 4.5 — When Slater’s condition fails Minimize e^{-x} subject to x^2 / y \le 0, on the domain y > 0. The constraint forces x = 0, so p^* = 1.

The dual function is g(\lambda) = \inf_{x, \, y > 0} (e^{-x} + \lambda x^2 / y) = 0: take x large and y much larger. So d^* = 0, a duality gap of 1. No point is strictly feasible.

Theorem 4.6 — Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions Let f_0, \dots, f_m be differentiable. The KKT conditions at (x, \lambda, \nu) are:

  • primal feasibility: f_i(x) \le 0 for all i, and Ax = b;
  • dual feasibility: \lambda \succeq 0;
  • complementary slackness: \lambda_i f_i(x) = 0 for all i;
  • stationarity: \nabla f_0(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla f_i(x) + A^\top \nu = 0 .

For a convex problem, every KKT point is primal and dual optimal, with zero gap. Under Slater’s condition, conversely, x is optimal if and only if some (\lambda, \nu) satisfies the KKT conditions with it.

Proof.

  • Sufficiency: L(\cdot, \lambda, \nu) is convex since \lambda \succeq 0, and stationary at x, so x minimizes it (Theorem 2.2). By complementary slackness and feasibility, g(\lambda, \nu) = L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f_0(x), and weak duality makes both optimal.
  • Necessity: let x^* be optimal and (\lambda^*, \nu^*) dual optimal (Theorem 4.4). Then f_0(x^*) = g(\lambda^*, \nu^*) \le L(x^*, \lambda^*, \nu^*) \le f_0(x^*) . Both inequalities are equalities. The second gives \sum_i \lambda_i^* f_i(x^*) = 0, a sum of nonpositive terms, hence complementary slackness. The first says that x^* minimizes L(\cdot, \lambda^*, \nu^*), hence stationarity. ∎

Proposition 4.7 — Sensitivity and shadow prices Perturb the constraints to f_i(x) \le u_i and Ax - b = v, and let p^*(u, v) be the optimal value. If strong duality holds with dual optimum (\lambda^*, \nu^*), then for all (u, v) p^*(u, v) \ge p^*(0, 0) - \lambda^{*\top} u - \nu^{*\top} v . Where p^* is differentiable, \partial p^* / \partial u_i = -\lambda_i^*. A large multiplier marks a constraint whose tightening is expensive; in economics, \lambda_i^* is the price of resource i.

Proof. Let x be feasible for the perturbed problem. Then \lambda_i^* f_i(x) \le \lambda_i^* u_i and Ax - b = v, so p^*(0, 0) = g(\lambda^*, \nu^*) \le L(x, \lambda^*, \nu^*) \le f_0(x) + \lambda^{*\top} u + \nu^{*\top} v . Take the infimum over such x. ∎

Proposition 4.8 — Semidefinite duality The dual of the SDP \text{minimize } c^\top x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x_1 F_1 + \dots + x_n F_n + G \preceq 0 is the SDP \text{maximize } \mathrm{tr}(G Z) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathrm{tr}(F_i Z) + c_i = 0, \ i = 1, \dots, n, \qquad Z \succeq 0 . The multiplier of a matrix inequality is a positive semidefinite matrix, because \mathbb{S}^k_+ is self-dual (Proposition 1.7). As in Theorem 4.4, strong duality holds when the primal is strictly feasible.

Example 4.9 — Water-filling To allocate a unit of power to n channels with noise levels \alpha_i > 0 and maximize capacity, solve \text{minimize } -\sum_{i=1}^n \log(\alpha_i + x_i) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x \succeq 0, \ \mathbf{1}^\top x = 1 . The KKT conditions are x_i \ge 0, \lambda_i \ge 0, \lambda_i x_i = 0 and -1/(\alpha_i + x_i) - \lambda_i + \nu = 0. Eliminating \lambda gives x_i = \max\Big(0, \ \frac{1}{\nu} - \alpha_i\Big) , where the water level 1/\nu is fixed by \mathbf{1}^\top x = 1: the channels are filled up to a common level.

For \alpha = (0.2, 0.5, 0.9, 1.6), bisection finds the level 1/\nu = 0.85, and x^* = (0.65, \ 0.35, \ 0, \ 0), \qquad \lambda^* = (0, \ 0, \ 0.065, \ 0.551) . The two noisiest channels receive nothing, and their multipliers are positive.

import numpy as np

alpha = np.array([0.2, 0.5, 0.9, 1.6])          # noise levels of 4 channels
def x_of(nu):                                   # KKT solution for a given multiplier
    return np.maximum(0.0, 1.0 / nu - alpha)

lo, hi = 1e-6, 1e6                              # bisection on the "water level" 1/nu
for _ in range(200):
    nu = np.sqrt(lo * hi)
    lo, hi = (nu, hi) if x_of(nu).sum() > 1.0 else (lo, nu)
x = x_of(nu)
lam = nu - 1.0 / (alpha + x)                    # multipliers of x >= 0
print("water level 1/nu =", round(1 / nu, 4))
print("x* =", np.round(x, 4), " sum =", round(x.sum(), 6))
print("lambda* =", np.round(lam, 4) + 0.0)        # + 0.0 turns -0.0 into 0.0
print("complementary slackness max |lambda_i x_i| =", np.abs(lam * x).max().round(10))
print("optimal value =", round(-np.log(alpha + x).sum(), 4))

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • The dual function is concave and bounds the primal value from below, convex or not (weak duality).
  • Under Slater’s condition the bound is tight; the proof is one separating hyperplane.
  • The KKT conditions characterize optimality of convex problems under Slater’s condition; complementary slackness says which constraints matter.
  • Dual variables are sensitivities of the optimal value, and the multiplier of a matrix inequality is a positive semidefinite matrix.

5. First-order methods and acceleration

When the dimension is large, a Newton step costs too much and one uses gradients only. This chapter proves how fast gradient methods converge, shows that no gradient method can beat a rate of order 1/k^2 on smooth convex functions, and gives Nesterov’s method, which attains it. Proximal versions extend everything to nonsmooth regularizers.

Definition 5.1 — First-order method A first-order method queries an oracle that returns f(x) and \nabla f(x), or a subgradient, and produces iterates with x_k \in x_0 + \mathrm{span}\{\nabla f(x_0), \dots, \nabla f(x_{k-1})\} . Its complexity on a class of functions is the number of oracle calls needed to guarantee f(x_k) - f^* \le \varepsilon (Nemirovski and Yudin 1983).

Theorem 5.2 — Gradient descent Let f be convex and L-smooth with a minimizer x^*, and x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac1L \nabla f(x_k). Then f(x_k) - f^* \le \frac{L \|x_0 - x^*\|_2^2}{2k} . If f is also \mu-strongly convex, \|x_k - x^*\|_2^2 \le \Big(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\Big)^k \|x_0 - x^*\|_2^2 .

Proof. Write g = \nabla f(x_k).

  • The descent lemma (Proposition 0.5) gives f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\|g\|^2: the values decrease.
  • Convexity gives f(x_k) \le f^* + g^\top (x_k - x^*). Adding, and expanding \|x_{k+1} - x^*\|^2 = \|x_k - x^* - g/L\|^2, f(x_{k+1}) - f^* \le g^\top (x_k - x^*) - \frac{1}{2L}\|g\|^2 = \frac{L}{2}\big(\|x_k - x^*\|^2 - \|x_{k+1} - x^*\|^2\big) .
  • Summing from 0 to k - 1 telescopes: the sum of the k gaps f(x_1) - f^*, \dots, f(x_k) - f^* is at most \frac{L}{2}\|x_0 - x^*\|^2. The gaps decrease, so the last one is at most their average.
  • Strongly convex case: Proposition 2.10, with y = x^*, gives g^\top (x_k - x^*) \ge f(x_k) - f^* + \frac{\mu}{2}\|x_k - x^*\|^2 , and the first step, with f^* \le f(x_{k+1}), gives \|g\|^2 / L^2 \le 2 (f(x_k) - f^*) / L. Insert both in \|x_{k+1} - x^*\|^2 = \|x_k - x^*\|^2 - \frac{2}{L} g^\top (x_k - x^*) + \frac{1}{L^2}\|g\|^2 . The function gaps cancel and \|x_{k+1} - x^*\|^2 \le (1 - \mu / L)\|x_k - x^*\|^2. ∎

Theorem 5.3 — Lower complexity bound For every k with 1 \le k \le (n - 1)/2 and every x_0, there is a convex L-smooth quadratic f on \mathbb{R}^n such that every first-order method satisfies f(x_k) - f^* \ge \frac{3 L \|x_0 - x^*\|_2^2}{32 (k + 1)^2} . The worst function (Nesterov 2004, §2.1) is f(x) = \frac{L}{4}\Big(\frac12 x^\top A x - x_1\Big) , where A is tridiagonal with 2 on the diagonal and -1 beside it, on the first 2k + 1 coordinates.

From x_0 = 0, each gradient reveals one more coordinate, so x_k lives on the first k coordinates, far from the minimizer. The complete computation is in ch. 10.

Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method

Algorithm 5.4 — Accelerated gradient method Start from z_0 = x_0 and set \theta_k = 2/(k + 2). For k = 0, 1, \dots y_k = (1 - \theta_k) x_k + \theta_k z_k, \qquad x_{k+1} = y_k - \tfrac{1}{L}\nabla f(y_k), \qquad z_{k+1} = z_k - \tfrac{1}{\theta_k L}\nabla f(y_k) . Eliminating z gives the familiar momentum form: x_{k+1} = y_k - \tfrac{1}{L}\nabla f(y_k), \qquad y_{k+1} = x_{k+1} + \frac{k}{k + 3}(x_{k+1} - x_k) .

Theorem 5.5 — The accelerated rate For convex L-smooth f, Algorithm 5.4 satisfies, for k \ge 1:

Key result — Nesterov’s rate f(x_k) - f^* \le \frac{2 L \|x_0 - x^*\|_2^2}{(k + 1)^2}

By Theorem 5.3 no first-order method can do better than this rate, up to a factor 64/3 \approx 21.

Proof. Let g = \nabla f(y_k) and r_k = \|z_k - x^*\|^2.

  • Descent lemma: f(x_{k+1}) \le f(y_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\|g\|^2.
  • Convexity at y_k, towards x_k with weight 1 - \theta_k and towards x^* with weight \theta_k: f(y_k) \le (1 - \theta_k) f(x_k) + \theta_k f^* + g^\top \big(y_k - (1 - \theta_k) x_k - \theta_k x^*\big) , and y_k - (1 - \theta_k) x_k = \theta_k z_k, so the last term is \theta_k g^\top (z_k - x^*).
  • Expanding the square in the z-update gives \theta_k g^\top (z_k - x^*) = \frac{\theta_k^2 L}{2}(r_k - r_{k+1}) + \frac{1}{2L}\|g\|^2 .
  • The three lines combine into f(x_{k+1}) - f^* \le (1 - \theta_k)\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big) + \frac{\theta_k^2 L}{2}(r_k - r_{k+1}) .
  • Divide by \theta_k^2 and note that \frac{1 - \theta_k}{\theta_k^2} = \frac{k(k + 2)}{4} \le \frac{(k + 1)^2}{4} = \frac{1}{\theta_{k-1}^2} . The quantity \theta_{k-1}^{-2}(f(x_k) - f^*) + \frac{L}{2} r_k never increases, and at k = 0 the coefficient (1 - \theta_0)/\theta_0^2 is zero.
  • Hence \theta_k^{-2}(f(x_{k+1}) - f^*) \le \frac{L}{2} r_0, which is the claim since \theta_k = 2/(k + 2). ∎

Strong convexity When \mu > 0 is known, the constant momentum (\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1) contracts the error by 1 - 1/\sqrt{\kappa} per step instead of 1 - 1/\kappa (Nesterov 2004, §2.2). For \kappa = 10^4 this needs about 100 times fewer iterations. Without \mu, restarting Algorithm 5.4 periodically recovers this rate (Ex. 15).

Example 5.6 — Acceleration on a badly conditioned quadratic The code minimizes a quadratic in dimension 500 with eigenvalues from 10^{-4} to 1, so \kappa = 10^4, from f(x_0) = 23.9.

iterations K 10 100 1000
gradient descent 1.14 0.133 0.0116
accelerated 0.573 0.0076 3.9 \times 10^{-6}

Both stay below their bounds (Theorems 5.2 and 5.5). Acceleration gains a factor 3000 at K = 1000 for the cost of one extra line.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 500
Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n)))
eig = np.logspace(-4, 0, n)                     # mu = 1e-4, L = 1: condition number 1e4
H = (Q * eig) @ Q.T
xstar = rng.normal(size=n)
f = lambda x: 0.5 * (x - xstar) @ H @ (x - xstar)  # f* = 0
grad = lambda x: H @ (x - xstar)
L, x0 = eig.max(), np.zeros(n)

def gd(K):
    x = x0.copy()
    for _ in range(K):
        x = x - grad(x) / L
    return f(x)

def agd(K):                                     # Nesterov, momentum k/(k+3)
    x_prev = x = y = x0.copy()
    for k in range(K):
        x_prev, x = x, y - grad(y) / L
        y = x + k / (k + 3) * (x - x_prev)
    return f(x)

R2 = np.sum((x0 - xstar) ** 2)
print(f"f(x0) = {f(x0):.3f}")
for K in (10, 100, 1000):
    print(f"K={K:5d}  GD {gd(K):.2e}  AGD {agd(K):.2e}  "
          f"bound L R^2/(2K) {L * R2 / (2 * K):.2e}  bound 2LR^2/(K+1)^2 {2 * L * R2 / (K + 1)**2:.2e}")

Nonsmooth functions: the subgradient method

Theorem 5.7 — Subgradient method Let f be convex and G-Lipschitz, so that every subgradient has \|g\|_2 \le G, and let \|x_0 - x^*\|_2 \le R. The iteration x_{i+1} = x_i - \alpha_i g_i with g_i \in \partial f(x_i) satisfies \min_{i \le k} f(x_i) - f^* \le \frac{R^2 + G^2 \sum_{i=0}^k \alpha_i^2}{2 \sum_{i=0}^k \alpha_i} . The constant step \alpha_i = R / (G \sqrt{k + 1}) gives \min_{i \le k} f(x_i) - f^* \le \frac{R G}{\sqrt{k + 1}} .

Proof. Expand the square: \|x_{i+1} - x^*\|^2 = \|x_i - x^*\|^2 - 2\alpha_i g_i^\top (x_i - x^*) + \alpha_i^2 \|g_i\|^2 , and use g_i^\top (x_i - x^*) \ge f(x_i) - f^* (Definition 2.8). Summing over i = 0, \dots, k gives 2 \sum_{i=0}^k \alpha_i \big(f(x_i) - f^*\big) \le R^2 + G^2 \sum_{i=0}^k \alpha_i^2 . Bound each f(x_i) below by the minimum. ∎

The rate 1/\sqrt{k} is optimal for nonsmooth Lipschitz functions (Nemirovski and Yudin 1983): nonsmoothness, not the dimension, makes these problems slow.

Proximal gradient methods

Definition 5.8 — Proximal operator For a closed convex h and \gamma > 0, \mathrm{prox}_{\gamma h}(v) = \arg\min_x \Big( h(x) + \frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x - v\|_2^2 \Big) , well defined because the objective is strongly convex. The indicator of a closed convex set gives the projection onto it. For h = \|\cdot\|_1, Fermat’s rule (Proposition 2.9) gives soft thresholding: \mathrm{prox}_{\gamma \|\cdot\|_1}(v)_i = \mathrm{sign}(v_i) \max(|v_i| - \gamma, 0) .

Theorem 5.9 — ISTA and FISTA Let F = f + h with f convex and L-smooth and h closed convex. The proximal gradient iteration x_{k+1} = \mathrm{prox}_{h/L}\big(x_k - \tfrac{1}{L}\nabla f(x_k)\big) satisfies F(x_k) - F^* \le L\|x_0 - x^*\|^2 / (2k). Its accelerated version FISTA (Beck and Teboulle 2009) satisfies F(x_k) - F^* \le 2L\|x_0 - x^*\|^2 / (k + 1)^2. With h = \lambda \|\cdot\|_1, the first is called ISTA.

The proofs of Theorems 5.2 and 5.5 use smoothness and convexity only through one inequality, which still holds for F. For x^+ the proximal step from y and G = L(y - x^+), for every z: F(z) \ge F(x^+) + G^\top (z - y) + \frac{1}{2L}\|G\|_2^2 .

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Gradient descent with step 1/L converges as 1/k on smooth convex functions, and linearly at rate 1 - 1/\kappa under strong convexity.
  • No first-order method beats 1/k^2 on smooth convex functions; Nesterov’s method attains it with one extra momentum step.
  • On nonsmooth Lipschitz functions the subgradient method converges as 1/\sqrt{k}, which is optimal.
  • Proximal steps handle nonsmooth regularizers at the price of a projection-like operation: ISTA and FISTA keep the rates of the smooth case.

6. Newton’s method and interior-point methods

Interior-point methods solve linear, second-order cone and semidefinite programs to high accuracy in a few dozen Newton steps. This chapter builds them in three moves: Newton’s method for smooth problems, its extension to equality constraints, and the logarithmic barrier that turns inequality constraints into a smooth penalty.

Definition 6.1 — Newton step and Newton decrement For f twice differentiable with \nabla^2 f(x) \succ 0, \Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}} = -\nabla^2 f(x)^{-1} \nabla f(x), \qquad \lambda(x) = \big(\nabla f(x)^\top \nabla^2 f(x)^{-1} \nabla f(x)\big)^{1/2} . The step minimizes the second-order Taylor model of f at x, and \lambda(x)^2 / 2 is the decrease the model predicts.

Both are affine invariant: minimizing y \mapsto f(Ty), for an invertible T, instead of f maps the iterates by T and leaves \lambda unchanged.

Algorithm 6.2 — Damped Newton method with backtracking Given x \in \mathrm{dom}\, f, a tolerance \varepsilon, \alpha \in (0, 1/2) and \beta \in (0, 1), repeat:

  1. compute \Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}} and \lambda(x); stop if \lambda(x)^2 / 2 \le \varepsilon;
  2. line search: start from s = 1 and multiply s by \beta as long as f(x + s \Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}}) > f(x) - \alpha s \lambda(x)^2 ;
  3. update x \leftarrow x + s \Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}}.

Theorem 6.3 — Local quadratic convergence Suppose \nabla^2 f \succeq m I near the minimizer x^*, and \nabla^2 f is M-Lipschitz there. Then the full Newton step x_{k+1} = x_k + \Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}} satisfies \|x_{k+1} - x^*\|_2 \le \frac{M}{2m} \|x_k - x^*\|_2^2 . Once \frac{M}{2m}\|x_k - x^*\|_2 < 1, the error is squared at each step: the number of correct digits doubles.

Proof. Since \nabla f(x^*) = 0, the integral form of Definition 0.4 gives, with H_k = \nabla^2 f(x_k), x_{k+1} - x^* = H_k^{-1} \int_0^1 \big( H_k - \nabla^2 f(x^* + s(x_k - x^*)) \big) (x_k - x^*) \, ds . The integrand has norm at most M (1 - s) \|x_k - x^*\|^2, so the integral is at most \frac{M}{2}\|x_k - x^*\|^2, and \|H_k^{-1}\| \le 1/m. ∎

Proposition 6.4 — Newton step with equality constraints To minimize f(x) subject to Ax = b from a feasible x, the Newton step minimizes the quadratic model over the directions with A \Delta x = 0. By the KKT conditions (Theorem 4.6) it solves \begin{pmatrix} \nabla^2 f(x) & A^\top \\ A & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}} \\ w \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -\nabla f(x) \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , where w estimates the multiplier \nu. The iterates stay feasible, and the analysis is unchanged.

The logarithmic barrier and the central path

Definition 6.5 — Logarithmic barrier and central path For the convex program of Definition 3.1, with twice differentiable f_i and a strictly feasible point, the logarithmic barrier is \phi(x) = -\sum_{i=1}^m \log(-f_i(x)) , convex and infinite on the boundary of the feasible set. For t > 0, the central point x^*(t) minimizes t f_0(x) + \phi(x) subject to Ax = b.

The curve t \mapsto x^*(t) is the central path: as t grows, the barrier weighs less and x^*(t) moves towards a solution.

Proposition 6.6 — Central points are m/t-suboptimal Every central point gives a dual feasible pair, \lambda_i^*(t) = -1/(t f_i(x^*(t))) and \nu^*(t) = w / t, where w is the multiplier of the centering problem. Consequently f_0(x^*(t)) - p^* \le \frac{m}{t} .

Proof. The optimality condition of the centering problem at x^* = x^*(t) reads t \nabla f_0(x^*) + \sum_i \frac{1}{-f_i(x^*)} \nabla f_i(x^*) + A^\top w = 0 . Divided by t, it says that x^* minimizes L(\cdot, \lambda^*(t), \nu^*(t)), with \lambda^*(t) \succ 0.

Moreover Ax^* = b, and \lambda_i^*(t) f_i(x^*) = -1/t for each i. Hence g(\lambda^*(t), \nu^*(t)) = f_0(x^*) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i^*(t) f_i(x^*) = f_0(x^*) - \frac{m}{t} , and weak duality concludes. ∎

The centrality conditions are the KKT conditions with complementary slackness relaxed to -\lambda_i f_i(x) = 1/t.

Algorithm 6.7 — Barrier method Given a strictly feasible x, t = t^{(0)} > 0, \mu > 1 and \varepsilon > 0, repeat:

  1. centering: compute x^*(t) by Newton’s method (Algorithm 6.2 with Proposition 6.4), starting from the current x;
  2. update x \leftarrow x^*(t);
  3. stop if m/t < \varepsilon; otherwise set t \leftarrow \mu t.

The method stops after a number of centerings equal to \Big\lceil \frac{\log\big(m / (\varepsilon t^{(0)})\big)}{\log \mu} \Big\rceil . A strictly feasible start comes from a phase I problem: minimize s subject to f_i(x) \le s and Ax = b. It starts from any x with Ax = b and a large s.

Example 6.8 — A barrier method for a linear program The code minimizes c^\top x subject to 100 random inequalities a_i^\top x \le b_i in dimension 50, with the barrier -\sum_i \log(b_i - a_i^\top x) and \mu = 20.

Each centering takes between 5 and 9 Newton steps while the gap m/t falls from 100 to 7.8 \times 10^{-8}: 54 Newton steps in total. The value agrees with the HiGHS solver to 4 \times 10^{-8}.

A larger \mu means fewer centerings but more Newton steps per centering; Ex. 6 measures the trade-off.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
m, n = 100, 50
A = rng.normal(size=(m, n))
b = rng.uniform(1, 2, size=m)                   # x = 0 is strictly feasible
c = -A.T @ rng.uniform(0, 1, size=m)            # c = -A^T lambda, lambda >= 0: LP is bounded

def centering(x, t, tol=1e-9, max_steps=50):
    """Newton's method with backtracking on t c^T x - sum log(b - A x)."""
    F = lambda z: t * c @ z - np.log(b - A @ z).sum()
    for steps in range(max_steps):
        d = 1.0 / (b - A @ x)
        g = t * c + A.T @ d
        Hs = A.T @ (A * d[:, None] ** 2)
        dx = -np.linalg.solve(Hs, g)
        lam2 = -g @ dx                          # Newton decrement squared
        if lam2 / 2 <= tol:
            break
        s = 1.0
        while np.min(b - A @ (x + s * dx)) <= 0:
            s *= 0.5                            # stay inside the domain
        while F(x + s * dx) > F(x) - 0.25 * s * lam2 and s > 1e-8:
            s *= 0.5                            # Armijo backtracking, alpha = 0.25
        x = x + s * dx
    return x, steps

x, t, mu, total = np.zeros(n), 1.0, 20.0, 0
while m / t > 1e-8:
    x, k = centering(x, t)
    total += k
    print(f"t = {t:9.1e}  Newton steps = {k:2d}  duality gap m/t = {m / t:.1e}")
    t *= mu
ref = linprog(c, A_ub=A, b_ub=b, bounds=[(None, None)] * n, method="highs")
print(f"barrier: {c @ x:.8f}   HiGHS: {ref.fun:.8f}   total Newton steps: {total}")

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Newton’s method is affine invariant and converges quadratically near a minimizer; the Newton decrement measures the distance to it.
  • Equality constraints add a KKT linear system and change nothing else.
  • The logarithmic barrier replaces inequalities by a smooth penalty; its minimizers form the central path.
  • Each central point carries a dual certificate with gap exactly m/t, so the barrier method knows when to stop.

7. Self-concordance and complexity

The classical analysis of Newton’s method depends on constants m and M that change with the coordinates, although the method itself does not. Nesterov and Nemirovski (1994) found the affine-invariant condition that makes the analysis exact, self-concordance, and with it a polynomial bound on the number of Newton steps of interior-point methods.

Definition 7.1 — Self-concordant function A convex function f of one variable, three times differentiable on an open interval, is self-concordant if |f'''(x)| \le 2 f''(x)^{3/2} . A convex f on \mathbb{R}^n is self-concordant if every restriction t \mapsto f(x + tv) to a line is. The local norm at x is \|h\|_x = (h^\top \nabla^2 f(x) h)^{1/2}, and \lambda(x) = \|\Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}}\|_x.

Example 7.2 — Self-concordant functions

  • -\log x: here f'' = 1/x^2 and f''' = -2/x^3, with equality in the definition. Affine and convex quadratic functions have f''' = 0.
  • The class is closed under sums, affine changes of variable, and multiplication by a constant a \ge 1.
  • Hence the LP barrier -\sum_i \log(b_i - a_i^\top x) is self-concordant, and so are -\log\det X and -\log(t^2 - \|u\|_2^2) on the second-order cone (Nesterov and Nemirovski 1994).
  • e^x is not self-concordant on \mathbb{R} (Ex. 11).

Proposition 7.3 — Hessian control along a segment Let f be self-concordant and r = \|y - x\|_x < 1. Then for s \in [0, 1] (y - x)^\top \nabla^2 f(x + s(y - x)) (y - x) \le \frac{r^2}{(1 - s r)^2} , and, with \omega_*(r) = -r - \log(1 - r), f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) + \omega_*(r) .

Proof.

  • Let \varphi(s) = f(x + s(y - x)). Self-concordance says that the derivative of \varphi''(s)^{-1/2}, which is -\frac12 \varphi''' \varphi''^{-3/2}, has absolute value at most 1.
  • Since \varphi''(0) = r^2, integrating this bound gives \varphi''(s)^{-1/2} \ge \varphi''(0)^{-1/2} - s = \frac{1 - sr}{r} , which is the first inequality.
  • Insert it in Taylor’s formula (Definition 0.4); a direct computation gives \int_0^1 (1 - s) \frac{r^2}{(1 - sr)^2} ds = \omega_*(r). ∎

The Dikin ellipsoid \{y : \|y - x\|_x < 1\} lies in the domain of f when f tends to infinity at the boundary of its domain (Nesterov 2004, §4.1).

Theorem 7.4 — Newton’s method on self-concordant functions Let f be self-concordant, bounded below, and tend to infinity at the boundary of its domain. Let \omega(t) = t - \log(1 + t).

  1. The damped step x^+ = x + \frac{1}{1 + \lambda(x)} \Delta x_{\mathrm{nt}} stays in the domain and decreases f by at least \omega(\lambda(x)).
  2. If \lambda(x) < 1, the full step satisfies \lambda(x^+) \le \Big( \frac{\lambda(x)}{1 - \lambda(x)} \Big)^2 .
  3. Damped steps while \lambda \ge 1/4, then full steps, reach f(x) - f^* \le \varepsilon after at most \frac{f(x_0) - f^*}{\omega(1/4)} + \log_2 \log_2 \frac{1}{\varepsilon} + c Newton steps, for an absolute constant c; here \omega(1/4) \approx 0.0269. No constant of f appears.

Proof. Part 1: the step has local norm r = \lambda / (1 + \lambda) < 1, and \nabla f(x)^\top (x^+ - x) = -\lambda^2 / (1 + \lambda). Proposition 7.3 gives f(x^+) \le f(x) - \frac{\lambda^2}{1 + \lambda} - \frac{\lambda}{1 + \lambda} + \log(1 + \lambda) = f(x) - \omega(\lambda) . Part 2 uses the matrix form of Proposition 7.3 (Nesterov 2004, §4.1).

Part 3: while \lambda \ge 1/4, each step gains at least \omega(1/4). Afterwards Part 2 gives \lambda^+ \le \frac{16}{9}\lambda^2, and f(x) - f^* \le \omega_*(\lambda) \le \lambda^2 for \lambda \le 0.68 (Nesterov 2004, §4.1). ∎

Example 7.5 — The two phases, observed The code computes the analytic centre of 200 random halfspaces in \mathbb{R}^{20}, starting close to several faces. Twelve damped steps bring \lambda from 4.7 to 0.23. Three full steps then give \lambda = 3.8 \times 10^{-3}, \quad 2.2 \times 10^{-6}, \quad 1.2 \times 10^{-12} , the doubling of digits of Theorem 7.4. The worst-case bound of Part 3 allows 833 damped steps; twelve were needed.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
m, n = 200, 20
A = rng.normal(size=(m, n))
b = rng.uniform(0.01, 1.0, size=m)              # x = 0 is strictly feasible, close to some faces
f = lambda x: -np.log(b - A @ x).sum()          # self-concordant (sum of -log of affine)

x, k = np.zeros(n), 0
while True:
    d = 1.0 / (b - A @ x)
    g, H = A.T @ d, A.T @ (A * d[:, None] ** 2)
    dx = -np.linalg.solve(H, g)
    lam = np.sqrt(-g @ dx)                      # Newton decrement lambda(x)
    print(f"k = {k:2d}  f = {f(x):10.6f}  lambda = {lam:.2e}")
    if lam < 1e-10:
        break
    step = 1.0 if lam < 0.25 else 1.0 / (1.0 + lam)   # damped phase, then pure Newton
    x, k = x + step * dx, k + 1
print("bound on damped steps: (f(x0) - f*) / omega(1/4) =",
      round((f(np.zeros(n)) - f(x)) / (0.25 - np.log(1.25)), 1))

Self-concordant barriers and the complexity of path following

Definition 7.6 — Self-concordant barrier A self-concordant \varphi whose domain is the interior of a closed convex set K is a \vartheta-self-concordant barrier for K if \nabla \varphi(x)^\top \nabla^2 \varphi(x)^{-1} \nabla \varphi(x) \le \vartheta \quad \text{for all } x . Three examples are used throughout:

  • -\sum_{i=1}^m \log(b_i - a_i^\top x) for a polyhedron with m faces, with \vartheta = m;
  • -\log\det X for \mathbb{S}^n_+, with \vartheta = n;
  • -\log(t^2 - \|u\|_2^2) for the second-order cone, with \vartheta = 2.

Theorem 7.7 — Complexity of path following Minimize a linear objective with a \vartheta-self-concordant barrier, by Algorithm 6.7 with exact centering. When t becomes \mu t, the new centering problem starts at most \vartheta \, (\mu - 1 - \log \mu) above its minimum, so it costs at most \vartheta(\mu - 1 - \log\mu)/\omega(1/4) + c' Newton steps (Theorem 7.4).

With \mu = 1 + 1/\sqrt{\vartheta}, this is a constant number of Newton steps per centering, since \mu - 1 - \log \mu \le (\mu - 1)^2 / 2. The total number of Newton steps is then O\Big( \sqrt{\vartheta} \, \log \frac{\vartheta}{t^{(0)} \varepsilon} \Big) .

Proof. For the logarithmic barrier with m = \vartheta inequalities, let x = x^*(t), x^+ = x^*(\mu t) and (\lambda, \nu) the dual pair of Proposition 6.6, so that -f_i(x) = 1/(t \lambda_i). The gap of the new centering problem equals \mu t f_0(x) - \mu t f_0(x^+) + \sum_i \log\big(-\mu t \lambda_i f_i(x^+)\big) - m \log \mu . - By \log a \le a - 1, the sum is at most -\mu t \sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x^+) - m. - Since Ax^+ = b, the definition of the dual function gives f_0(x^+) + \sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x^+) = L(x^+, \lambda, \nu) \ge g(\lambda, \nu) . - By Proposition 6.6, f_0(x) - g(\lambda, \nu) = m/t. Together, the gap is at most \mu m - m - m\log\mu.

For general \vartheta-self-concordant barriers, see Nesterov and Nemirovski (1994) and Renegar (2001). ∎

Primal–dual methods Practical solvers use primal–dual variants that update x, \lambda and \nu together, by Newton steps on the relaxed KKT system, with Mehrotra’s predictor–corrector (Mehrotra 1992). They need a few tens of iterations on most problems, almost independently of the size; each iteration solves one linear system, which is the real cost. Ch. 10 sketches them.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Self-concordance, |f'''| \le 2 f''^{3/2}, is affine invariant; logarithms of affine, log-determinant and second-order cone barriers satisfy it.
  • On self-concordant functions, damped Newton decreases f by a fixed amount, then converges quadratically, with bounds free of any constant of f.
  • A barrier’s parameter \vartheta measures the cost of a central path: O(\sqrt{\vartheta}\log(1/\varepsilon)) Newton steps suffice.
  • This is why interior-point methods solve LPs, SOCPs and SDPs in a number of iterations that barely depends on the data.

8. Applications: statistics, learning, finance and signal processing

The official syllabus names three fields where convex programs arise. This chapter models a representative problem from each, solves it with the algorithms of ch. 5–7, and reads its dual variables.

Statistics and machine learning

Proposition 8.1 — Convex maximum likelihood When the density of the observations is log-concave in the parameter, maximum likelihood is a convex program. Logistic regression with labels y_i \in \{-1, 1\} is the main example: \text{minimize} \ \sum_{i=1}^n \log\big(1 + e^{-y_i x_i^\top \beta}\big) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2 . Each term is log-sum-exp of (0, -y_i x_i^\top \beta), convex by Propositions 2.4–2.5.

The Hessian is X^\top D X + \rho I, where D is diagonal with entries \sigma(u)(1 - \sigma(u)) \le 1/4 and \sigma is the logistic function. So the objective is \rho-strongly convex and L-smooth with L = \frac14 \|X\|_2^2 + \rho .

Example 8.2 — The LASSO The LASSO (Tibshirani 1996) is the convex program \text{minimize} \ F(\beta) = \frac{1}{2n}\|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_1 . Fermat’s rule (Proposition 2.9) gives its optimality condition: \frac{1}{n} X^\top (y - X\beta) \in \lambda \, \partial \|\beta\|_1 . Each correlation with the residual equals \lambda \, \mathrm{sign}(\beta_j) on the support and lies in [-\lambda, \lambda] off it. In particular \beta = 0 is optimal exactly when \lambda \ge \lambda_{\max} = \|X^\top y\|_\infty / n.

The code solves a problem with 100 observations, 300 correlated features and 10 true nonzeros, at \lambda = 0.05 \lambda_{\max}. The gap F - F^* is:

iterations 10 100 1000
ISTA 2.4 0.59 8.7 \times 10^{-3}
FISTA 1.7 4.1 \times 10^{-3} 2.6 \times 10^{-7}

The solution has 23 nonzeros, including the 10 true ones, and matches scikit-learn’s coordinate descent to 6 \times 10^{-12}.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import Lasso

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, p, s = 100, 300, 10
C = 0.9 ** np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(p), np.arange(p)))   # correlated features
X = rng.normal(size=(n, p)) @ np.linalg.cholesky(C).T
beta0 = np.zeros(p); beta0[::30] = rng.choice([-2.0, 2.0], size=s)
y = X @ beta0 + 0.5 * rng.normal(size=n)

lam_max = np.abs(X.T @ y).max() / n             # beta = 0 is optimal above this value
lam = 0.05 * lam_max
F = lambda b: 0.5 / n * np.sum((y - X @ b) ** 2) + lam * np.abs(b).sum()
L = np.linalg.norm(X, 2) ** 2 / n               # Lipschitz constant of the smooth part
soft = lambda v, a: np.sign(v) * np.maximum(np.abs(v) - a, 0.0)
step = lambda b: soft(b - X.T @ (X @ b - y) / (n * L), lam / L)

def ista(K):
    b = np.zeros(p)
    for _ in range(K):
        b = step(b)
    return b

def fista(K):
    b = z = np.zeros(p); t = 1.0
    for _ in range(K):
        b_new = step(z)
        t_new = (1 + np.sqrt(1 + 4 * t * t)) / 2
        z = b_new + (t - 1) / t_new * (b_new - b)
        b, t = b_new, t_new
    return b

ref = Lasso(alpha=lam, fit_intercept=False, tol=1e-14, max_iter=1_000_000).fit(X, y).coef_
Fstar = min(F(ref), F(fista(20000)))
print(f"lambda_max = {lam_max:.3f}, lambda = {lam:.4f}, L = {L:.1f}")
for K in (10, 100, 1000):
    print(f"K={K:5d}  ISTA gap {F(ista(K)) - Fstar:.1e}   FISTA gap {F(fista(K)) - Fstar:.1e}")
b = fista(20000)
print("nonzeros:", np.sum(np.abs(b) > 1e-6), " true support recovered:",
      set(np.flatnonzero(beta0)) <= set(np.flatnonzero(np.abs(b) > 1e-6)))
print("max |FISTA - sklearn| =", f"{np.abs(b - ref).max():.1e}")

Finance

Example 8.3 — Markowitz portfolio optimization Asset returns r \in \mathbb{R}^n have mean \bar r and covariance \Sigma.

A portfolio w of wealth fractions, \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1, has mean return \bar r^\top w and variance w^\top \Sigma w. Markowitz (1952) minimizes the risk at a target return: \text{minimize } w^\top \Sigma w \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \bar r^\top w \ge r_{\min}, \ \ \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1, \ \ w \succeq 0 , a QP in which w \succeq 0 forbids short sales. Sweeping r_{\min} traces the efficient frontier. By Proposition 4.7, the multiplier of the return constraint is the marginal variance of one more unit of return; without w \succeq 0 the KKT conditions give the frontier in closed form (Ex. 12).

Proposition 8.4 — Value-at-risk and robust constraints are second-order cone constraints If r \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar r, \Sigma) and \eta \le 1/2, the constraint \mathbb{P}(r^\top w \le \alpha) \le \eta is equivalent to \bar r^\top w + \Phi^{-1}(\eta) \, \|\Sigma^{1/2} w\|_2 \ge \alpha , an SOC constraint since \Phi^{-1}(\eta) \le 0. A linear constraint that must hold for every vector of an ellipsoid is also an SOC constraint (Example 3.5).

Proof. r^\top w is Gaussian with mean \bar r^\top w and standard deviation \sigma = \|\Sigma^{1/2} w\|_2. The probability equals \Phi((\alpha - \bar r^\top w)/\sigma), which is at most \eta exactly when (\alpha - \bar r^\top w)/\sigma \le \Phi^{-1}(\eta). ∎

Signal processing

Example 8.5 — Denoising: quadratic smoothing and total variation We observe y = x + \text{noise} with x \in \mathbb{R}^n, and D is the first-difference matrix. Two denoisers compete: \min_x \ \|x - y\|_2^2 + \rho \|Dx\|_2^2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \min_x \ \|x - y\|_2^2 + \rho \|Dx\|_1 . Quadratic smoothing, on the left, is one linear system but blurs jumps. Total variation, on the right (Rudin, Osher and Fatemi 1992), keeps jumps: the \ell_1 norm tolerates a few large differences.

Writing \rho \|Dx\|_1 = \max_{\|u\|_\infty \le \rho} u^\top D x and minimizing over x gives the dual, a box-constrained QP solved by projected gradient: \text{minimize} \ \|y - D^\top z\|_2^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \|z\|_\infty \le \rho / 2 , and the denoised signal is x = y - D^\top z.

Theorem 8.6 — Exact recovery by \ell_1 minimization Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} with m < n. Basis pursuit, minimize \|x\|_1 subject to Ax = y (Chen, Donoho and Saunders 1998), is an LP.

It recovers every s-sparse x_0 from y = A x_0 if and only if A has the null space property of order s: \|h_S\|_1 < \|h_{S^c}\|_1 \quad \text{for all } h \in \ker A \setminus \{0\} \text{ and all } |S| \le s .

Proof. Sufficiency: let x_0 be supported on S and x_0 + h, with h \in \ker A \setminus \{0\}, another feasible point. Then \|x_0 + h\|_1 = \|x_0 + h_S\|_1 + \|h_{S^c}\|_1 \ge \|x_0\|_1 - \|h_S\|_1 + \|h_{S^c}\|_1 > \|x_0\|_1 . Necessity: if \|h_S\|_1 \ge \|h_{S^c}\|_1 for some h \in \ker A, the s-sparse vector h_S and the vector -h_{S^c} have the same measurements, and the second has no larger \ell_1 norm. ∎

Gaussian matrices with m \ge C s \log(n/s) rows have this property with high probability (Candès, Romberg and Tao 2006; Donoho 2006): this is compressed sensing. In the code, with n = 200 unknowns and m = 60 measurements, basis pursuit recovers 20 out of 20 random vectors with 10 nonzeros, 15 with 15, 4 with 20, and none with 25.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n, m = 200, 60                                   # unknowns, measurements
A = rng.normal(size=(m, n)) / np.sqrt(m)

def basis_pursuit(y):
    """min ||x||_1 s.t. Ax = y, as an LP in (u, v) with x = u - v, u, v >= 0."""
    res = linprog(np.ones(2 * n), A_eq=np.c_[A, -A], b_eq=y,
                  bounds=[(0, None)] * (2 * n), method="highs")
    return res.x[:n] - res.x[n:]

for s in (5, 10, 15, 20, 25):
    ok = 0
    for trial in range(20):
        x0 = np.zeros(n)
        x0[rng.choice(n, s, replace=False)] = rng.normal(size=s)
        ok += np.linalg.norm(basis_pursuit(A @ x0) - x0) < 1e-6 * np.linalg.norm(x0)
    print(f"sparsity s = {s:2d}: exact recovery in {ok:2d}/20 trials")

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Log-concave likelihoods, the LASSO and SVMs are convex programs; optimality conditions explain sparsity and give \lambda_{\max}.
  • On correlated designs, FISTA beats ISTA by orders of magnitude at equal cost.
  • Mean–variance portfolios are QPs; Gaussian value-at-risk and robust constraints are second-order cone constraints.
  • Total variation and basis pursuit are LPs or QPs; the null space property says exactly when \ell_1 minimization recovers sparse signals.

9. Combinatorial problems and convex relaxations

Many combinatorial problems are quadratic programs over \{-1, 1\}^n. Duality gives them a convex bound for free, and rounding the solution of that bound often gives a provably good feasible point. This chapter develops the semidefinite relaxation and the Goemans–Williamson algorithm for MAXCUT.

Example 9.1 — Two-way partitioning and its Lagrangian bound Consider the NP-hard problem p^* = \min_x \ x^\top W x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad x_i^2 = 1, \ i = 1, \dots, n . Its Lagrangian x^\top (W + \mathrm{diag}(\nu)) x - \mathbf{1}^\top \nu is bounded below in x exactly when W + \mathrm{diag}(\nu) \succeq 0. The dual is the SDP \text{maximize} \ -\mathbf{1}^\top \nu \quad \text{s.t.} \quad W + \mathrm{diag}(\nu) \succeq 0 , a lower bound on p^*, since weak duality (Proposition 4.2) needs no convexity. The feasible choice \nu = -\lambda_{\min}(W)\mathbf{1} already gives p^* \ge n \lambda_{\min}(W).

Proposition 9.2 — The lifted relaxation Write x^\top W x = \mathrm{tr}(W X) with X = x x^\top. The constraints become X \succeq 0, X_{ii} = 1 and \mathrm{rank}\, X = 1. Dropping the rank gives the SDP \text{minimize} \ \mathrm{tr}(W X) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad X \succeq 0, \ X_{ii} = 1 , whose value is at most p^*. It is the conic dual of the SDP of Example 9.1. Both are strictly feasible (X = I, and \nu large), so the two bounds coincide (Proposition 4.8).

MAXCUT and randomized rounding

Definition 9.3 — MAXCUT Given a graph with weights w_{ij} \ge 0 and Laplacian L = \mathrm{diag}(W\mathbf{1}) - W, find x \in \{-1, 1\}^n maximizing the weight of the edges cut: \mathrm{cut}(x) = \sum_{i < j} w_{ij} \frac{1 - x_i x_j}{2} = \frac14 x^\top L x . Its SDP relaxation, \mathrm{SDP} = \max \frac14 \mathrm{tr}(L X) subject to X \succeq 0 and X_{ii} = 1, is an upper bound on the maximum cut.

Theorem 9.4 — Goemans–Williamson rounding Let X solve the SDP and factor it as X_{ij} = v_i^\top v_j with unit vectors v_i. Draw a standard Gaussian vector g and set x_i = \mathrm{sign}(v_i^\top g). Then \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{cut}(x)] \ge \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} \, \mathrm{SDP} \ge \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} \, \mathrm{MAXCUT}, \qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} = \min_{0 < \theta \le \pi} \frac{2}{\pi} \frac{\theta}{1 - \cos\theta} \approx 0.8786 .

Proof.

  • The hyperplane orthogonal to g separates v_i from v_j with probability \theta_{ij}/\pi, where \theta_{ij} = \arccos(v_i^\top v_j).

    Indeed, the projection of g on the plane of the two vectors has a uniform direction, and the separating directions form two arcs of total angle 2\theta_{ij}.

  • Hence, by definition of \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} and since w_{ij} \ge 0, \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{cut}(x)] = \sum_{i < j} w_{ij} \frac{\theta_{ij}}{\pi} \ge \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} \sum_{i < j} w_{ij} \frac{1 - \cos\theta_{ij}}{2} = \alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} \, \mathrm{SDP} . The last equality uses X_{ij} = \cos\theta_{ij}. ∎

Under the Unique Games Conjecture, no polynomial algorithm guarantees a better constant (Khot, Kindler, Mossel and O’Donnell 2007).

Example 9.5 — Solving and rounding a MAXCUT relaxation The code solves the dual SDP, minimize \mathbf{1}^\top \nu subject to \mathrm{diag}(\nu) - L/4 \succeq 0, by the barrier method of ch. 6. Its barrier t\mathbf{1}^\top \nu - \log\det S, with S = \mathrm{diag}(\nu) - L/4, is self-concordant, so the damped step of Theorem 7.4 needs no line search.

The primal matrix comes for free. On the central path, X = S^{-1}/t is positive definite with unit diagonal (the centering condition), and the duality gap is \mathrm{tr}(SX) = n/t.

On a random graph with 14 vertices, the SDP bound is 34.158. Enumerating all 2^{13} cuts gives the maximum cut, 33. The mean rounded cut is 32.03, above the guarantee 0.8786 \times 34.158 = 30.01, and the best of 1000 roundings is optimal.

import itertools
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 14
W = np.triu((rng.random((n, n)) < 0.5).astype(float), 1); W = W + W.T
Lap = np.diag(W.sum(1)) - W                     # cut(x) = x^T Lap x / 4 for x in {-1, 1}^n

# Dual SDP: minimize 1^T nu  s.t.  S(nu) = diag(nu) - Lap/4 >= 0, by the barrier method.
# t 1^T nu - log det S(nu) is self-concordant: damped Newton needs no line search.
def newton_center(nu, t):
    for _ in range(50):
        Sinv = np.linalg.inv(np.diag(nu) - Lap / 4)
        g, H = t - np.diag(Sinv), Sinv ** 2      # gradient and Hessian (Hadamard square)
        d = -np.linalg.solve(H, g)
        lam = np.sqrt(-g @ d)                    # Newton decrement
        if lam < 1e-6:                           # then f - f* <= lam^2 (Theorem 7.4)
            break
        nu = nu + (1.0 if lam < 0.25 else 1.0 / (1.0 + lam)) * d
    return nu

nu, t = np.full(n, np.linalg.eigvalsh(Lap / 4)[-1] + 1.0), 1.0
while True:
    nu = newton_center(nu, t)
    if n / t < 1e-5:
        break
    t *= 10
X = np.linalg.inv(np.diag(nu) - Lap / 4) / t    # primal point on the central path
sdp = np.trace(Lap @ X) / 4
print(f"SDP bound: dual {nu.sum():.5f}, primal {sdp:.5f}, gap {nu.sum() - sdp:.1e}, "
      f"max |diag(X) - 1| = {np.abs(X.diagonal() - 1).max():.1e}")

# Exact MAXCUT by enumeration (x_0 = +1 by symmetry)
best = max(np.array((1,) + s) @ Lap @ np.array((1,) + s) / 4
           for s in itertools.product((-1, 1), repeat=n - 1))

# Goemans-Williamson random-hyperplane rounding
w, U = np.linalg.eigh(X)
V = U * np.sqrt(np.maximum(w, 0))               # rows v_i with v_i . v_j = X_ij
cuts = [x @ Lap @ x / 4 for x in np.sign(rng.normal(size=(1000, n)) @ V.T)]
theta = np.linspace(1e-4, np.pi, 200_000)
alpha = np.min(2 / np.pi * theta / (1 - np.cos(theta)))
print(f"max cut = {best:.0f}, GW mean cut = {np.mean(cuts):.2f}, best of 1000 = {max(cuts):.0f}")
print(f"alpha_GW = {alpha:.5f}, guarantee alpha * SDP = {alpha * sdp:.2f}")

Other convex relaxations

  • Cardinality and rank. On the box \|x\|_\infty \le 1, the largest convex function below \mathrm{card}(x) is \|x\|_1. On the unit ball of the spectral norm, the largest convex function below \mathrm{rank}\, X is the nuclear norm, the sum of singular values (Recht, Fazel and Parrilo 2010). This explains basis pursuit and the LASSO (ch. 8), and low-rank matrix completion (Candès and Recht 2009).
  • Sparse PCA. Maximizing x^\top \Sigma x over unit vectors with few nonzeros has the SDP relaxation: maximize \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma X) - \rho \sum_{ij} |X_{ij}| subject to \mathrm{tr}\, X = 1, X \succeq 0 (d’Aspremont, El Ghaoui, Jordan and Lanckriet 2007).
  • Hierarchies. Lifting to higher-degree moments gives the sums-of-squares hierarchies of Lasserre (2001) and Parrilo (2003), whose levels tighten the relaxations of polynomial problems.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Weak duality bounds any problem, convex or not; for quadratic problems over \{-1, 1\}^n the bound is an SDP.
  • Lifting x to X = xx^\top and dropping the rank gives the same bound from the primal side.
  • Random-hyperplane rounding of the MAXCUT relaxation is within a factor 0.8786 of the optimum in expectation.
  • The \ell_1 and nuclear norms are the convex envelopes of cardinality and rank: relaxation explains sparse and low-rank recovery.

The lower complexity bound

Proposition 10.1 — Proof of Theorem 5.3 Let p = 2k + 1 \le n, let A_p be the tridiagonal matrix with 2 on the diagonal and -1 beside it on the first p coordinates (zero elsewhere), and f(x) = \frac{L}{4}\Big(\frac12 x^\top A_p x - x_1\Big) . Every first-order method started at x_0 = 0 satisfies f(x_k) - f^* \ge \frac{3L\|x_0 - x^*\|_2^2}{32(k+1)^2}. Other starting points follow by translation.

Proof.

  • Smoothness: the quadratic form is a sum of squares, x^\top A_p x = x_1^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1}(x_i - x_{i+1})^2 + x_p^2 . It lies between 0 and 4\|x\|_2^2, because (a - b)^2 \le 2a^2 + 2b^2. So 0 \preceq \nabla^2 f \preceq L I.
  • Minimizer: A_p x = e_1 is solved by x_i^* = 1 - \frac{i}{p+1} for i \le p and 0 beyond. At a minimizer f^* = -\frac{L}{8} x_1^*, so f^* = \frac{L}{8}\Big(-1 + \frac{1}{p+1}\Big) .
  • Distance: summing the squares of the coordinates, \|x^*\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{(p+1)^2}\sum_{j=1}^p j^2 = \frac{p(2p+1)}{6(p+1)} \le \frac{p+1}{3} .
  • Krylov property: if x is supported on the first i < p coordinates, \nabla f(x) = \frac{L}{4}(A_p x - e_1) is supported on the first i + 1. By induction, x_k is supported on the first k coordinates.
  • Restricted minimum: on those coordinates f is the same function with A_k in place of A_p, so f(x_k) \ge \frac{L}{8}\big(-1 + \frac{1}{k+1}\big).
  • Conclusion: with p = 2k + 1, f(x_k) - f^* \ge \frac{L}{8}\Big(\frac{1}{k+1} - \frac{1}{2k+2}\Big) = \frac{L}{16(k+1)} , and \|x_0 - x^*\|^2 \le \frac{2k+2}{3} gives the claim. ∎

Frank–Wolfe: optimization without projections

Theorem 10.3 — Conditional gradient (Frank and Wolfe 1956) Let f be convex and L-smooth on a compact convex set C of diameter D. The iteration s_k \in \arg\min_{s \in C} \nabla f(x_k)^\top s, \qquad x_{k+1} = x_k + \gamma_k (s_k - x_k), \qquad \gamma_k = \frac{2}{k + 2}, stays in C and satisfies, for k \ge 1, f(x_k) - f^* \le \frac{2 L D^2}{k + 2} . The quantity \nabla f(x_k)^\top (x_k - s_k) is an upper bound on f(x_k) - f^*, computed for free.

Proof. Let h_k = f(x_k) - f^*. The descent lemma and the choice of s_k give h_{k+1} \le h_k + \gamma_k \nabla f(x_k)^\top (s_k - x_k) + \frac{L \gamma_k^2 D^2}{2} \le (1 - \gamma_k) h_k + \frac{L \gamma_k^2 D^2}{2}, because, by the choice of s_k and then by convexity, \nabla f(x_k)^\top (s_k - x_k) \le \nabla f(x_k)^\top (x^* - x_k) \le -h_k .

With \gamma_0 = 1, h_1 \le LD^2/2. If h_k \le 2LD^2/(k+2), then h_{k+1} \le 2LD^2 (k + 1)/(k + 2)^2 \le 2LD^2/(k + 3). ∎

Each step solves a linear problem over C instead of a projection. On the \ell_1 ball the linear problem picks one signed vertex, so the iterates stay sparse; on the nuclear-norm ball it needs only the top singular pair (Jaggi 2013). In the code, Frank–Wolfe on an \ell_1-ball constrained least-squares problem with 200 variables has 14 nonzeros after 200 steps, with a certified gap of 0.30.

import numpy as np

# Nesterov's worst function f(x) = L/4 (x^T A x / 2 - x_1), A tridiagonal (2, -1), size p
L, K = 1.0, 50
p = 2 * K + 1
A = 2 * np.eye(p) - np.eye(p, k=1) - np.eye(p, k=-1)
f = lambda x: L / 4 * (0.5 * x @ A @ x - x[0])
grad = lambda x: L / 4 * (A @ x - np.eye(p)[0])
xstar = 1 - np.arange(1, p + 1) / (p + 1)
fstar = f(xstar)

x_prev = x = y = np.zeros(p)
for k in range(K):                              # accelerated gradient, x_0 = 0
    x_prev, x = x, y - grad(y) / L
    y = x + k / (k + 3) * (x - x_prev)
lower = 3 * L * xstar @ xstar / (32 * (K + 1) ** 2)
upper = 2 * L * xstar @ xstar / (K + 1) ** 2
print(f"K = {K}: lower bound {lower:.2e} <= AGD gap {f(x) - fstar:.2e} <= upper bound {upper:.2e}")
print("nonzero coordinates of x_K:", np.count_nonzero(np.abs(x) > 0), "out of", p)

# Frank-Wolfe on min ||Bz - c||^2 / 2 over the l1 ball of radius 1
rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
B, c = rng.normal(size=(50, 200)), rng.normal(size=50)
z = np.zeros(200)
for k in range(200):
    g = B.T @ (B @ z - c)
    i = np.argmax(np.abs(g))
    s = np.zeros(200); s[i] = -np.sign(g[i])     # linear minimization oracle: a vertex
    gap = g @ (z - s)                           # Frank-Wolfe duality gap >= f(z) - f*
    z = z + 2 / (k + 2) * (s - z)
print(f"Frank-Wolfe after 200 steps: gap {gap:.2e}, nonzeros {np.count_nonzero(z)}")

Mirror descent

Proposition 10.4 — Mirror descent on the simplex To minimize a convex f over the probability simplex, with subgradients bounded by G_\infty in \ell_\infty norm, replace the Euclidean step by the exponentiated gradient update x_{k+1, i} = \frac{x_{k,i} \, e^{-\alpha g_{k,i}}}{\sum_j x_{k,j} \, e^{-\alpha g_{k,j}}} . From the uniform distribution, with a well-chosen constant step \alpha, \min_{i \le k} f(x_i) - f^* \le G_\infty \sqrt{\frac{2 \log n}{k + 1}} . The dependence on the dimension is \sqrt{\log n}, against \sqrt{n} for the projected subgradient method in the worst case. The update is a proximal step with the entropy in place of the squared Euclidean distance (Nemirovski and Yudin 1983; Beck and Teboulle 2003).

Primal–dual interior-point methods

Proposition 10.5 — The primal–dual Newton system for LP For the LP, minimize c^\top x subject to Ax = b and x \succeq 0, with dual slack s = c - A^\top y, the central path is defined by Ax = b, \qquad A^\top y + s = c, \qquad x_i s_i = 1/t, \qquad x, s \succ 0 . A primal–dual method takes Newton steps on these equations with target x_i s_i = \sigma \mu, where \mu = x^\top s / n and \sigma \in (0, 1): \begin{pmatrix} 0 & A^\top & I \\ A & 0 & 0 \\ S & 0 & X \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \\ \Delta s \end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix} A^\top y + s - c \\ Ax - b \\ X S \mathbf{1} - \sigma \mu \mathbf{1} \end{pmatrix} , with X = \mathrm{diag}(x) and S = \mathrm{diag}(s), followed by a step that keeps x and s positive.

The iterates need not be feasible: the residuals shrink along the way. Mehrotra’s predictor–corrector (Mehrotra 1992) chooses \sigma adaptively from an affine-scaling step and adds a second-order correction; Wright (1997) gives the theory. The homogeneous self-dual embedding (Ye, Todd and Mizuno 1994) also detects infeasible or unbounded problems. The same ideas extend to SOCP and SDP through the symmetric-cone framework (Nesterov and Todd 1997).

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • The worst function for first-order methods is a chain: each gradient reveals one more coordinate, which forces the 1/k^2 lower bound.
  • Frank–Wolfe replaces projections by linear minimization, gives sparse or low-rank iterates and a free certificate, at rate 1/k.
  • Mirror descent adapts the step to the geometry of the constraints; on the simplex it pays \sqrt{\log n} instead of \sqrt{n}.
  • Primal–dual methods run Newton’s method on the relaxed KKT system and are what solvers implement.

11. Research directions and open problems

Convex optimization is a mature field whose questions are now about exact constants, scale, structure and the boundary with nonconvex problems. This chapter, for self-study, points to active lines of work with references that open each of them.

Exact worst cases and optimal step sizes

Work by Drori and Teboulle (2014) shows that the worst case of a first-order method on smooth convex functions is itself the value of a small semidefinite program, the performance estimation problem. Taylor, Hendrickx and Glineur (2017) make this exact through interpolation conditions, so that the best possible bound of a method can be computed.

This approach found the optimized gradient method of Kim and Fessler (2016), whose bound is about half of Nesterov’s, and Drori (2017) proved that no method does better. Altschuler and Parrilo (2023) show that plain gradient descent, with a fractal schedule of long steps, already beats the 1/k rate. The optimal step-size schedules of gradient descent without momentum are not yet known.

Restart, sharpness and adaptivity

Accelerated methods are not monotone and do not adapt to strong convexity. O’Donoghue and Candès (2015) restart them when the objective increases, and Roulet and d’Aspremont (2020) show that restart schemes adapt to sharpness, a growth condition weaker than strong convexity, with near-optimal rates.

Scieur, d’Aspremont and Bach (2016) accelerate any convergent sequence by nonlinear extrapolation, and the monograph of d’Aspremont, Scieur and Taylor (2021) surveys acceleration. Removing the need to know L without line search, as in the adaptive method of Malitsky and Mishchenko (2020), remains an active topic.

Interior-point methods at scale

On the theory side, Lee and Sidford (2014) solve linear programs in about \sqrt{\mathrm{rank}} iterations, and Cohen, Lee and Song (2021) in the time of matrix multiplication. Whether linear programming admits a strongly polynomial algorithm, with a number of operations independent of the size of the numbers, is open: it is Smale’s ninth problem.

In practice, very large LPs and conic programs are now solved by first-order primal–dual methods, such as the PDHG method of Chambolle and Pock (2011) in the PDLP solver (Applegate and coauthors 2021), the operator splitting of SCS (O’Donoghue, Chu, Parikh and Boyd 2016), and ADMM (Boyd, Parikh, Chu, Peleato and Eckstein 2011). How to reach interior-point accuracy at first-order cost is open.

Low-rank semidefinite programming

An SDP in n \times n matrices costs at least n^2 memory. Burer and Monteiro (2003) factor X = VV^\top with V of few columns and optimize V directly, a nonconvex problem.

Boumal, Voroninski and Bandeira (2016) show that, for generic smooth SDPs with m constraints, second-order critical points are optimal once the number of columns exceeds about \sqrt{2m}. Yurtsever, Tropp, Fercoq, Udell and Cevher (2021) combine Frank–Wolfe with sketching to store only a low-rank approximation.

When are convex relaxations tight?

Amelunxen, Lotz, McCoy and Tropp (2014) predict the phase transitions of convex recovery problems with random data, such as the one observed in ch. 8, from a single geometric quantity, the statistical dimension of a descent cone. Chandrasekaran, Recht, Parrilo and Willsky (2012) build convex penalties for general structured signals from atomic norms.

Phase retrieval can be relaxed to an SDP (Candès, Strohmer and Voroninski 2013) or written as a MAXCUT-like problem (Waldspurger, d’Aspremont and Mallat 2015). For community detection, the semidefinite relaxation reaches the information-theoretic threshold of exact recovery (Hajek, Wu and Xu 2016). Explaining when relaxations of higher levels of the sums-of-squares hierarchy help is a central question of average-case complexity.

Convex optimization inside learning systems

Amos and Kolter (2017) and Agrawal, Amos, Barratt, Boyd, Diamond and Kolter (2019) use convex programs as layers of neural networks, differentiating the solution through the KKT conditions (Theorem 4.6). Making such layers fast and robust when the problem is degenerate is open.

Questions to explore

  • Find, with the performance estimation approach, the best constant step for gradient descent on smooth convex functions over k steps, and compare it with 1/L.
  • For which random graphs does the MAXCUT relaxation of ch. 9 return a rank-one solution, so that rounding is not needed?
  • Can a barrier method with Newton systems solved by conjugate gradients match the accuracy of an exact interior-point method on a large LP, and at what cost?
  • How sharp is the Goemans–Williamson constant on graphs from applications rather than worst-case instances?

  4. Question (4 points). Write each problem as an LP, an SOCP or an SDP: (a) \min_x \|Ax - b\|_1; (b) \min_x \|Ax - b\|_2 + \|x\|_1.

    1. \min c^\top x subject to a^\top x \le 1 for all a with \|a - \bar a\|_2 \le \rho; (d) \min_x \lambda_{\max}(A_0 + \sum_i x_i A_i).

    Expected, one point each:

      1. LP: minimize \mathbf{1}^\top t s.t. -t \preceq Ax - b \preceq t.
      1. SOCP: minimize s + \mathbf{1}^\top u s.t. \|Ax - b\|_2 \le s and -u \preceq x \preceq u.
      1. SOCP: minimize c^\top x s.t. \bar a^\top x + \rho\|x\|_2 \le 1 (Example 3.5).
      1. SDP: minimize t s.t. A_0 + \sum_i x_i A_i \preceq tI.

Total: 5 + 6 + 5 + 4 = 20 points.
