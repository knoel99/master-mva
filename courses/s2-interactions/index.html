0. Review: continuous-time Markov chains

Later chapters are continuous-time Markov chains, or limits of such chains. This chapter records the finite-state facts they use. Liggett (2005) treats countable spaces. Here the state space S is finite.

The generator and the semigroup

Definition 0.1 — Generator

A matrix Q indexed by S is a generator when every off-diagonal entry is nonnegative and every row sums to 0.

The entry q_{ij} for i \neq j is the jump rate from i to j. The holding rate in i is -q_{ii}.

The holding time in i is exponential with rate -q_{ii}. At a jump, the chain moves from i to j with probability q_{ij}/(-q_{ii}) when the holding rate is positive. A zero row is an absorbing state.

Theorem 0.2 — Finite-state semigroup

There is a unique matrix family (P_t)_{t \ge 0} with P_0 = I and \frac{d}{dt} P_t = Q P_t = P_t Q. The family is the series P_t = \exp(tQ). The entry (P_t)_{ij} is the probability of j at time t starting from i.

Proof.

  • Finite dimension makes \sum_k (tQ)^k / k! converge absolutely, so term-by-term differentiation is valid.
  • Differentiating and shifting the index produces both Q \exp(tQ) and \exp(tQ)\, Q.
  • Those products agree because Q commutes with its powers. At time 0 the series equals I.

∎

A row vector \pi is stationary when \pi Q = 0 and the entries of \pi sum to 1. An irreducible finite chain has one such row, and P_t converges to the matrix with every row equal to \pi.

Detailed balance is the identity \pi_i q_{ij} = \pi_j q_{ji} for every pair. Summing on i recovers \pi Q = 0. Exclusion and Ising dynamics are built so that this identity holds and the stationary law is explicit.

On a countable space the same series needs a rate bound, or a graphical construction, before the semigroup exists. Chapter 1 uses the graphical picture on a graph and leaves the general existence proof to Liggett (2005).

Key 0.3 — Master equation

If \mu_t is the row vector of the law at time t, \frac{d}{dt} \mu_t = \mu_t Q.

A two-state chain

Example 0.4 — Rates 1 and 2

Take states \{0,1\}, with rate 1 from 0 to 1 and rate 2 from 1 to 0. Then Q = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}. The stationary row is (2/3,\ 1/3). Let p(t) be the probability of state 1 at time t, starting from 0. The master equation is p' = 1 - 3p with p(0) = 0, hence p(t) = \frac{1 - e^{-3t}}{3}. The limit is 1/3. The gap 3 is the sum of the two jump rates, and it is the nonzero eigenvalue of -Q. Exercise 2 simulates this chain on a finite horizon.

Three checks recur after this chapter. Write Q before writing a simulation. Check that every row sums to 0. Test a claimed stationary row on the master equation.

From state 0, the probability that the first clock has not yet rung at time t is e^{-t}. Paths that jump to state 1 and return also contribute to the chance of being at 0. Example 0.4 sums every such path. The survival function of the first clock does not.

The scalar ODE is Theorem 0.2 on a 2 \times 2 matrix. Unequal rates bias occupation time toward the slower state. The semigroup law P_{t+s} = P_t P_s is the Markov property in matrix form, and it is the exponential identity \exp((t+s)Q) = \exp(tQ)\exp(sQ).

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A finite-state generator has nonnegative off-diagonal entries, and its rows sum to 0.
  • The law at time t is \exp(tQ). A stationary row solves \pi Q = 0.
  • Detailed balance implies stationarity. Later chapters use it for exclusion and for Ising dynamics.

1. Contact, voter and exclusion processes

An interacting particle system is a Markov chain on configurations on a graph. This chapter treats the contact process, the voter model and symmetric exclusion, using the generators of ch. 0. Aldous (2013) reads these processes as social dynamics. Liggett (2005) is the reference for infinite lattices.

The contact process

Definition 1.1 — Contact process

A configuration is a map \eta : V \to \{0,1\}. Site x is infected when \eta(x) = 1.

Each infected site recovers at rate 1. Each healthy site x becomes infected at rate \lambda times the number of infected neighbours of x.

The critical value \lambda_c is the infimum of rates at which one infection survives with positive probability. On a finite graph the healthy state is absorbing, so survival is a question about infinite graphs of bounded degree.

Theorem 1.2 — Branching lower bound

Let \Delta be the maximum degree of G. The contact process started from finitely many infections dies out almost surely whenever \lambda \le 1/\Delta. In particular \lambda_c \ge 1/\Delta.

Proof.

  • Compare the infected set with a branching process that ignores the rule “a site cannot be infected twice”.
  • An infected site lives an \mathrm{Exp}(1) time and sends a Poisson process of rate \lambda along each edge.
  • The mean number of arrows from one site is at most \lambda \Delta.
  • A Poisson offspring law of mean at most 1 dies out almost surely.
  • The contact process has fewer infections than this branching process, so it dies out as well.

∎

The comparison stops once \lambda \Delta > 1. Liggett (2005) proves that \lambda_c is finite on \mathbb{Z}^d. These notes do not quote a numerical interval.

Key 1.3 — Degree bound

\lambda_c \ge \frac{1}{\Delta}.

The voter model

Each site holds an opinion in \{0,1\} and copies a uniform neighbour at rate 1. Poisson clocks on ordered pairs are the graphical construction. Read backward, the arrows are coalescing random walks, and the opinion at time t is the initial opinion at the walk’s position.

Example 1.4 — Two sites

Let two sites each copy the other at rate 1. If they start with different opinions, the first copy makes them agree, and they stay agreed. The waiting time is exponential of rate 2, so the probability they still differ at time t is e^{-2t}.

At time 1 that probability is e^{-2}. Liggett (2005) uses the same duality on lattices. The walks meet on \mathbb{Z} and on \mathbb{Z}^2, so the model clusters. In dimension 3 and higher, both opinions can survive.

Symmetric exclusion

Symmetric simple exclusion uses the same Poisson clocks on edges, with a different mark. When the clock of an edge rings, the two endpoints exchange their occupation variables. A particle moves only into an empty neighbour, and the number of particles is constant.

Proposition 1.5 — Bernoulli invariance

For every density \rho \in [0,1], the product Bernoulli(\rho) measure is invariant for symmetric exclusion on a finite graph.

Proof.

  • A product measure gives equal mass to a configuration and to the configuration obtained by swapping two sites of different occupation, because the swap preserves the number of particles.
  • The clock on that edge rings at the same rate in both directions.
  • Detailed balance holds for every edge. A sum of such generators still satisfies detailed balance.

∎

The particle count is conserved, so every density \rho is stationary. With exactly k particles the chain is irreducible, and the uniform law is the only stationary law at that k. Exercise 4 treats one particle on two sites.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • The contact process dies out when \lambda \Delta \le 1, by comparison with a branching process.
  • Voter-model opinions follow backward random walks. Two sites that copy each other agree after an \mathrm{Exp}(2) time.
  • Symmetric exclusion preserves every product Bernoulli measure, and it preserves the number of particles.

2. Stochastic Ising models

The Ising model is the syllabus example of a Gibbs measure with local flips. Statistical physics is not a prerequisite. The definitions below are self-contained. Glauber dynamics means single-spin flip dynamics. Metropolis and heat bath are two choices of flip rate. Kawasaki dynamics exchanges spins and keeps the magnetization fixed. Sethna (2021) is the reference used on the course page for order parameters. This chapter only needs the generator and detailed balance.

Gibbs measure and local fields

Let each spin be \sigma_i \in \{-1,+1\}, and let E be the edge set. Fix a coupling J and a field h. The energy and the local field at i are H(\sigma) = -\sum_{\{i,j\} \in E} J\, \sigma_i \sigma_j - \sum_i h_i \sigma_i, h_i^{\mathrm{loc}}(\sigma) = h_i + \sum_{j \sim i} J\, \sigma_j.

Definition 2.1 — Gibbs measure

For an inverse temperature \beta > 0, the Gibbs measure is \pi(\sigma) = \frac{e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \sum_{\sigma} e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}.

Flipping the spin \sigma_i changes the energy by 2 \sigma_i h_i^{\mathrm{loc}}(\sigma). Only the terms that contain \sigma_i move, and each of them changes sign.

Metropolis, heat bath, Kawasaki

Definition 2.2 — Three rates

Metropolis. Each site rings at rate 1. The proposed flip of i is accepted with probability \min(1, e^{-\beta \Delta H}).

Heat bath. Each site rings at rate 1 and is redrawn from the conditional Gibbs law of \sigma_i given the other spins. The conditional probability of +1 is \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2\beta h_i^{\mathrm{loc}}}}.

Kawasaki. An edge rings at rate 1. The two spins at its ends exchange, and the exchange is accepted with the Metropolis probability of its energy change.

Heat bath is the Gibbs sampler on one coordinate. Metropolis uses the same proposals but a different acceptance. Both are Glauber dynamics. Kawasaki is not a single-spin flip. It stays inside a level set of \sum_i \sigma_i.

Theorem 2.3 — Metropolis detailed balance

Let q(\sigma,\sigma') be symmetric: the proposal rate from \sigma to a neighbour \sigma' equals the reverse proposal rate. Let the acceptance probability be a(\sigma,\sigma') = \min(1, \pi(\sigma')/\pi(\sigma)). Then \pi is invariant, and \pi(\sigma)\, q(\sigma,\sigma')\, a(\sigma,\sigma') = \pi(\sigma')\, q(\sigma',\sigma)\, a(\sigma',\sigma).

Proof.

  • Symmetry of q removes the proposal rates from the identity to be checked.
  • After cancelling q, the remaining identity is symmetric in the two configurations: u \min(1, v/u) = \min(u, v) for u = \pi(\sigma) and v = \pi(\sigma').
  • Summing the balance identity over \sigma shows that \pi is stationary.

∎

Heat bath satisfies the same balance because the kernel replaces \sigma_i by a draw from \pi(\sigma_i \mid \sigma_{-i}), and the Gibbs measure factors as \pi(\sigma_{-i}) times that conditional. The flow from \sigma to a flipped configuration equals the reverse flow.

Kawasaki inherits Theorem 2.3 inside each magnetization class. The stationary law is the Gibbs measure conditioned on \sum_i \sigma_i, because the chain never leaves that class and Metropolis balance holds there.

Detailed balance does not give a mixing time. Near a critical inverse temperature the single-spin chain slows down. These notes do not estimate that time. They only identify the stationary measure.

Key 2.4 — Flip cost

\Delta H = 2 \sigma_i h_i^{\mathrm{loc}}.

Example 2.5 — Two spins

Take two spins, zero field, and e^{\beta J} = 3. Aligned states each have weight 3. Mixed states each have weight 1/3. The partition function is Z = 20/3.

Thus \pi(++) = 9/20 and \pi(+-) = 1/20. A flip out of the aligned state ++ has acceptance probability 1/9. The reverse flip is always accepted.

The products agree: (9/20) \times (1/9) = 1/20. Exercise 5 asks for this check and for the general argument of Theorem 2.3.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The Gibbs weight is e^{-\beta H}/Z. Flipping spin i costs 2 \sigma_i h_i^{\mathrm{loc}}.
  • Metropolis and heat bath are single-spin Glauber dynamics, and both leave \pi invariant.
  • Kawasaki exchanges preserve magnetization. The stationary law is \pi conditioned on that sum.

3. Agent-based models and self-organized criticality

An agent-based model, in the sense of Epstein (2012), is a population plus a local rule. Emergence means a pattern the rule does not name. Self-organized criticality, as in Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld (1987) and Sethna (2021), is the case of a scale-free avalanche with no tuned temperature.

Schelling’s rule

Definition 3.1 — Schelling update

Agents of two types, and some empty cells, sit on a grid. The neighbourhood of a cell is fixed in advance.

An occupied cell is unhappy when the fraction of its occupied neighbours that share its type is strictly below a threshold T. The update swaps a chosen unhappy agent with a chosen empty cell.

Schelling (1971) reported segregation at mild thresholds, near one third. Exercise 6 uses T = 1/3 on a torus with the eight-cell neighbourhood. The threshold is a local test, not a target for the global likeness.

Example 3.2 — A cycle that oscillates

Four sites in a cycle use the two adjacent cells as a neighbourhood, with threshold 1/3. Start from (A, A, empty, B).

The first A sees one A and one B, so its fraction is 1/2 and it stays. The second A sees only an A. The B sees only an A, so it is unhappy. The only swap produces (A, A, B, empty).

That B is again the only unhappy agent, and the next swap returns the first row. The cycle oscillates. Exercise 6 prints a frozen run on a larger torus.

The Abelian sandpile

Definition 3.3 — Chip firing with a sink

Each site x holds a height h(x). One site is a sink. A non-sink site is unstable when its height is at least its degree. Toppling it sends one chip along each edge. Chips that reach the sink leave the system.

Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld (1987) drove a sandpile by adding one chip at a time and relaxing. Avalanches are the toppling cascades. Dhar (1990) isolated the algebraic fact that makes the final configuration unambiguous.

Theorem 3.4 — Abelian property

If two non-sink sites are unstable, toppling one leaves the other unstable. As linear updates, the toppling maps commute. Any two sequences of legal topplings that reach a stable configuration perform the same number of topplings at each site and finish at the same heights.

Proof.

  • Toppling x decreases the height at x by its degree and increases each neighbour by 1. It does not decrease the height at a different unstable site.
  • Adding the toppling vectors in either order gives the same configuration, so the linear maps commute.
  • Dhar (1990) proves that legal topplings on a finite graph with a sink reach a stable configuration.
  • Commuting reductions that terminate end at the same heights, so the toppling count at each site is unique.

∎

Avalanche sizes are deferred to the branching process in ch. 4.

Key 3.5 — What emergence names

A statistic is emergent when the agent rule does not contain it as a parameter, and frozen configurations still show it.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • A Schelling agent moves when its local same-type fraction is below T. The global likeness is not a parameter of that test.
  • On a four-cycle with one vacancy the same rule oscillates. On a large torus it can freeze.
  • Sandpile topplings commute. With a sink, the stable configuration does not depend on the order of legal topplings.

4. Epidemics, markets and delay cascades

This chapter gives one exact law for epidemics, for a herding market, and for a delay avalanche. Adam (2020), Kirman (1993) and Moran and coauthors (2024) are the course-page references. The delay tree below is a course model, not a quotation of Moran and coauthors.

Well-mixed SIR

A well-mixed SIR epidemic splits a population into susceptible, infected and removed fractions s, i and r. With infection coefficient \beta and recovery coefficient \gamma, s' = -\beta s i, \qquad i' = \beta s i - \gamma i, \qquad r' = \gamma i. The basic reproduction number is R_0 = \beta / \gamma. Assume s(0) + i(0) + r(0) = 1 and r(0) = 0, with i(0) vanishing as the population grows.

Theorem 4.1 — Final size

Let r_\infty = \lim_{t \to \infty} r(t). Then i(\infty) = 0 and 1 - r_\infty = e^{-R_0 r_\infty}. When R_0 \le 1 the only root in [0,1] is 0. When R_0 > 1 there is one root in (0,1).

Proof.

  • While i > 0, the chain rule gives ds/dr = s'/r' = -R_0 s.
  • Hence \log s(t) - \log s(0) = -R_0 r(t). A vanishing initial infection sends s(0) to 1, so s(t) = e^{-R_0 r(t)}.
  • The infected fraction returns to 0, and s + r = 1 at the end. Substitute s(\infty) = 1 - r_\infty.
  • The function 1 - r - e^{-R_0 r} has derivative -1 + R_0 at 0, so a second root in (0,1) appears exactly when R_0 > 1.

∎

For R_0 = 2 the positive root is about 0.797. That number is the removed fraction after infection dies, not the peak of i(t). Adam (2020) surveys individual-based simulators used in the COVID-19 response. Theorem 4.1 is the homogeneous comparison for those simulators. The group project measures a network gap at the same R_0.

A herding market

The course page pairs markets with socio-technical systems. The model here is the smallest piece of that pairing.

Definition 4.3 — Kirman chain

N agents each choose A or B. Let K be the number choosing A.

Each agent in B switches to A at rate a + b K.

Each agent in A switches to B at rate a + b(N-K), with a > 0 and b \ge 0.

The upward rate at K is (N-K)(a+bK). The downward rate is K times (a+b(N-K)).

Detailed balance fixes the stationary masses. When a/b is small, mass piles up near the extremes. Kirman (1993) uses that pile-up as a model of herding with no external shock.

Example 4.4 — Two agents

Take N = 2, a = 1 and b = 4. The upward rate from 0 is 2, and the downward rate from 1 is 5. Detailed balance gives \pi(1) = (2/5)\pi(0). The same calculation from 1 to 2 gives \pi(2) = \pi(0).

Normalization yields \pi(0) = \pi(2) = 5/12 and \pi(1) = 2/12. The centre is lighter than either extreme. Replacing b by a smaller value is an intervention inside the model. Chapter 5 returns to what such an intervention does and does not mean.

Delay cascades

Definition 4.5 — Delay avalanche

One late task creates a random number of downstream late tasks, with mean m, and those tasks do the same, independently. The total number of late tasks, including the first, is the total progeny S of a Galton-Watson tree.

Proposition 4.6 — Mean progeny

If m < 1 and the offspring mean is m, then \mathbb{E}[S] = 1/(1-m). If m = 1 and the offspring law is not concentrated at 1, then \mathbb{E}[S] = \infty.

Proof.

  • Condition on the first generation. S = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{N} S_j with S_j independent copies of S and \mathbb{E}[N] = m.
  • If the expectation is finite, \mathbb{E}[S] = 1 + m\,\mathbb{E}[S], so \mathbb{E}[S] = 1/(1-m) whenever m < 1.
  • If m = 1, the same identity becomes \mathbb{E}[S] = 1 + \mathbb{E}[S], which no finite number satisfies.

∎

The sandpile of ch. 3 is a spatial cascade of the same kind. At m = 1 the mean avalanche is infinite, so a typical delay is not a planning number. Moran and coauthors (2024) study timeliness criticality for scheduled systems. Proposition 4.6 is the course model of that critical point.

Key 4.7 — Final-size equation

1 - r_\infty = e^{-R_0 r_\infty}.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • In well-mixed SIR, the attack fraction solves 1 - r = e^{-R_0 r}. For R_0 = 2 the positive root is about 0.797.
  • The Kirman chain with N = 2, a = 1 and b = 4 has stationary masses 5/12, 2/12 and 5/12.
  • A subcritical delay avalanche has mean 1/(1-m). At m = 1 the mean is infinite.

5. Aggregation, learned laws and causes

Krakauer (2023) links complexity science and machine learning. This chapter lumps a chain, estimates which pairs interact, and estimates a pairwise force. Banisch (2015) is the course-page reference for aggregation of agent-based models.

Strong lumpability

Definition 5.1 — Strong lumpability

Let \{A, B, \ldots\} be a partition of a finite state space. The partition is strongly lumpable for Q when, for every ordered pair of blocks, every state in the source block has the same total rate into the target block.

Theorem 5.2 — The block chain

If the partition is strongly lumpable, the block occupied by X_t is a Markov chain for every initial law. Its rate from block A to block B is that common total rate.

Proof.

  • Let m_A(t) be the probability of block A. Its derivative is a sum of probability flows across block boundaries.
  • By the rate hypothesis, the flow from A to B equals the common rate times m_A(t).
  • The derivatives of the block masses therefore close. They do not depend on how mass sits inside a block.
  • A closed linear system of that form is a finite-state master equation, so the block process is Markov.

∎

If two states in A have different rates into B, the block process need not be Markov. Kemeny and Snell (1960) isolate this strong form. Weak lumpability is not used here.

Example 5.3 — Three states

Label states 1,2,3 and blocks A = \{1,2\}, B = \{3\}. Let both 1 and 2 jump to 3 at rate 2, and let 3 jump to A at total rate 2. The partition is strongly lumpable.

The lumped chain is a two-state chain with both off-diagonal rates equal to 2. Its stationary mass on each block is 1/2. Exercise 8 uses a different matrix, with stationary mass 1/7 on the singleton block.

Which pairs interact

The conditional law of one Ising spin is the heat-bath law of ch. 2. Besag (1975) maximizes the product of those conditionals. On a finite sample this is a logistic regression of each spin on the others.

A zero coefficient is a guess that the edge is absent. The product is not the full likelihood, and these notes state no finite-sample error bound.

Learning a pairwise law

A different inverse problem keeps the pairs and estimates the force. Let N particles in the plane have trajectories X_i(t). A model with an unknown radial law \phi is \dot X_i = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j \neq i} \phi(r_{ij})\, u_{ij}, where r_{ij} is the distance and u_{ij} is the unit vector from i to j.

Expand \phi in a basis and fit the coefficients by least squares on the velocities. Lu, Zhong, Tang and Maggioni (2019) give a nonparametric estimator from trajectories. The solo project uses synthetic paths, where the error is computable. These notes do not restate a rate.

Causes inside a model

Definition 5.4 — In-model intervention

An intervention replaces one parameter or one rule, resimulates, and compares a statistic to the original run. The random seed of the comparison is part of the definition.

In Example 4.4, lowering b moves mass from the extremes toward the centre. That contrast is causal inside the Kirman chain. Manzo (2022) asks when such a contrast supports a claim about a social system. These notes stop at the in-model calculation.

Key 5.5 — Lumpability test

Every state of a block must share the same total rate into each other block. Otherwise the block process need not be Markov.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Strong lumpability is a test on exit rates. When it holds, the block chain is Markov for every initial law.
  • Pseudolikelihood estimates Ising edges. Least squares on velocities estimates a radial law \phi.
  • An intervention inside an agent-based model identifies a contrast in that model. Transfer to a policy claim is a separate argument.

6. Rare events and convex hulls

A rare large value can come from many moderate contributions, or from one dominant jump. Nair, Wierman and Zwart (2022), cited as Nair 2022 on the course page, call these conspiracy and catastrophe. The rest of the chapter is the planar convex hull of Majumdar, Comtet and Randon-Furling (2010), then resetting.

Definition 6.1 — Subexponential law

A law on the nonnegative reals is subexponential when, for every n, the tail of the sum of n i.i.d. copies is asymptotic to n times the tail of one copy.

Chernoff and one big jump

Theorem 6.2 — Chernoff bound

Let X_i be i.i.d. with M(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[e^{\theta X_1}] finite for some \theta > 0. For a in that range, P(S_n \ge n a) \le e^{-n I(a)}, \qquad I(a) = \sup_{\theta \ge 0}\big(\theta a - \log M(\theta)\big).

Proof.

  • For \theta \ge 0, the event S_n \ge n a is the event e^{\theta S_n} \ge e^{\theta n a}.
  • Markov’s inequality bounds the probability by e^{-\theta n a} M(\theta)^n.
  • The exponent is -n(\theta a - \log M(\theta)). The best upper bound is the supremum over \theta.

∎

For unit exponentials, M(\theta) = 1/(1-\theta) when \theta < 1. For a > 1 the rate is I(a) = a - 1 - \log a, so I(2) = 1 - \log 2, about 0.307. This is an upper bound only.

Proposition 6.3 — Elementary sum bounds

Let X_1 and X_2 be i.i.d. and nonnegative, and let S_2 = X_1 + X_2. Then P(X_1 > x) \le P(S_2 > x) \le 2\, P(X_1 > x/2).

Proof.

  • If X_2 \ge 0 and X_1 > x, then S_2 > x. This is the left inequality.
  • If both variables are at most x/2, then S_2 \le x. The complementary event is contained in the union of the two events \{X_i > x/2\}.

∎

For unit exponentials the ratio P(S_2 > x) / P(X_1 > x) equals 1+x and grows. A constant one-big-jump factor is already false.

Nair, Wierman and Zwart (2022) prove Definition 6.1 for the usual heavy-tailed laws. Proposition 6.3 only shows why that hypothesis is needed.

Convex hull of planar Brownian motion

Let B_t be standard planar Brownian motion and let H_T be the convex hull of the path on [0,T]. Write L_T for the perimeter of H_T.

Theorem 6.4 — Mean perimeter

\mathbb{E}[L_T] = \sqrt{8\pi T}.

Proof.

  • For a planar convex body, the perimeter equals the integral from 0 to \pi of the width. The width in direction \theta is the range of the support in that direction.
  • The projection of B onto a unit vector is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion. The expected width is therefore the expected range of that motion.
  • By the reflection principle, P(M_T \ge a) = 2P(B_T \ge a) for a > 0. Integrating the tail gives \mathbb{E}[M_T] = \sqrt{2T/\pi}.
  • The range has mean 2\mathbb{E}[M_T] because the running minimum is a reflected maximum. Integrate the constant expected width over an angle of length \pi to obtain 2\sqrt{2\pi T}, which is \sqrt{8\pi T}.

∎

The integral of the Gaussian tail converts the reflection bound into \sqrt{2T/\pi}. Majumdar, Comtet and Randon-Furling (2010) pass from planar Lévy paths to one-dimensional extrema by a combinatorial lemma. These notes prove the Brownian perimeter only.

Key 6.5 — Brownian perimeter

\mathbb{E}[L_T] = \sqrt{8\pi T}.

Resetting

A Brownian searcher starts at distance L from a target at 0 and is reset to the start at rate r. Evans and Majumdar (2011) compute the mean hitting time. With diffusivity D, set \alpha = \sqrt{r/D}. The mean time from x solves D t''(x) - r t(x) + r t(L) = -1, with t(0) = 0 and t bounded for large x. The value at the reset point is t(L) = \frac{e^{\alpha L} - 1}{r}. The bounded solution is a multiple of e^{-\alpha x} plus a constant. The condition at 0 fixes the multiple, and the value at L is the display above.

Example 6.6 — One optimal rate

Take D = 1 and L = 1. Set z = \sqrt{r}. The mean time is (e^z - 1)/z^2. Its critical points satisfy e^{-z} = 1 - z/2.

The positive root is z = 1.594 to three decimals, so r = 2.540. The mean time at that rate is 1.544. As r \to 0 and as r \to \infty the mean time tends to infinity, so the critical point is a minimum.

de Bruyne, Randon-Furling and Redner (2020) study optimization of first-passage resetting. The numbers in this example are the one-searcher diffusion problem, not a table copied from that paper.

Exercise 9 reconstructs the perimeter argument. The reflection step fails for a bridge, because the projection is no longer a free Brownian motion.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • A finite moment generating function gives a Chernoff bound with rate I(a). For a unit exponential, I(2) = 1 - \log 2.
  • Nonnegative variables satisfy a crude sum bound. The sharp one-big-jump asymptotic needs a subexponential hypothesis.
  • The mean perimeter of a planar Brownian convex hull of duration T is \sqrt{8\pi T}.

7. Heavy tails in gradient descent

Constant-step stochastic gradient descent can have a heavy-tailed stationary law even when each noise variable has a light tail. The mechanism is multiplicative noise, as in a random recurrence. Gurbuzbalaban, Hu, Simsekli, Yuan and Zhu (2022) study that phenomenon for decentralized SGD. This chapter proves the scalar index calculation that makes the mechanism readable. It does not reproduce their network formulas.

The Kesten step

Definition 7.1 — Random recurrence

Let (A_n, B_n) be i.i.d. pairs, independent of X_0. The recurrence is X_{n+1} = A_{n+1} X_n + B_{n+1}.

Theorem 7.2 — A two-point index

Let A equal 2 with probability 1/3 and equal 1/2 with probability 2/3.

Then \mathbb{E}[A] = 1 and \mathbb{E}[\log A] = -(\log 2)/3, which is negative. The choice \alpha = 1 solves \mathbb{E}[|A|^\alpha] = 1.

Proof.

  • The arithmetic mean is \frac{1}{3}\cdot 2 + \frac{2}{3}\cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1.
  • The log-mean is (1/3)\log 2 plus (2/3)\log(1/2), which equals -(\log 2)/3.
  • Since A > 0, the identity \mathbb{E}[|A|] = \mathbb{E}[A] is the index equation at \alpha = 1.

∎

Kesten (1973) gives a power-law tail when the log-mean of |A| is negative, the index equation holds, and B is not degenerate at 0. Goldie (1991) identifies the prefactor under a non-lattice condition. For Theorem 7.2 the index is 1. These notes do not compute the prefactor.

If B = 0, the log-mean hypothesis sends X_n to 0. The log-modulus is a sum of i.i.d. terms with negative mean, so the strong law drives it to -\infty.

A nonzero additive term keeps a stationary tail alive. A Gaussian additive term is enough. The heavy tail then comes from A, not from that Gaussian.

Key 7.3 — Index equation

\mathbb{E}[|A|^\alpha] = 1, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\log |A|] < 0.

What this says about SGD

A constant-step update with state-dependent noise can be written in the form of Definition 7.1. A linear model makes the picture exact. For a quadratic with curvature \mu, a step of size \eta, and a multiplicative noise \xi_{n+1}, \theta_{n+1} = (1 - \eta \mu + \eta \sigma \xi_{n+1})\, \theta_n + \eta \zeta_{n+1}. The term in parentheses is A_{n+1}. The additive term is B_{n+1}. If the law of \xi is chosen so that A matches Theorem 7.2, the stationary tail is of index 1 whenever the Kesten hypotheses hold.

An additive-noise recursion, with A equal to the deterministic factor 1 - \eta\mu and |\eta\mu| < 1, is an autoregression. Its stationary tails are no heavier than the tails of \zeta. Multiplicative noise is the ingredient that changes the conclusion.

Decentralized SGD couples several such updates along a graph. Gurbuzbalaban, Hu, Simsekli, Yuan and Zhu (2022) analyse heavy tails in that algorithm. Copying a slope from their figures would require their code and their step-size grid. The calculation required here is Theorem 7.2. Exercise 10 adds the almost-sure argument for B = 0.

Example 7.4 — Why the mean of A can equal 1

\mathbb{E}[\log A] < 0 and \mathbb{E}[A] = 1 do not contradict each other. The logarithm is concave, so Jensen’s inequality says \mathbb{E}[\log A] \le \log \mathbb{E}[A] = 0, and the inequality is strict unless A is constant.

The two-point law is not constant, so the log-mean is strictly negative while the arithmetic mean of A equals 1. The index \alpha = 1 is this arithmetic fact. It is not an extra assumption.

The same Jensen gap is why a naive Gaussian approximation of \theta_n is the wrong tail. The approximation replaces A by its mean and deletes the fluctuation that Kesten’s theorem uses.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • For the two-point step of Theorem 7.2, \mathbb{E}[A] = 1 and \mathbb{E}[\log A] = -(\log 2)/3.
  • With B = 0 the product X_n tends to 0. With a nonzero light-tailed B, Kesten’s theorem gives a tail of index 1.
  • Constant-step SGD with multiplicative noise is a recurrence of this type. Decentralized SGD is studied in Gurbuzbalaban et al. (2022).

8. Research directions and open problems

The theorems in the lectures stop at a precise hypothesis. The questions below are what those hypotheses leave out. The voice is that of a reading list. Each item names a paper that already exists.

The contact-process bound \lambda_c \ge 1/\Delta is not a formula for \lambda_c on \mathbb{Z}. Liggett (2005) contains sharper bounds. A closed form for the one-dimensional critical value is not known. Numerical estimates exist in the literature. These notes do not adopt one, because the course does not need a disputed digit.

Schelling likeness on a torus is a many-to-one map. Several thresholds and vacancy rates can freeze at similar fractions. Identifying T from the single number printed in Exercise 6 is not settled by Definition 3.1. A useful project states which extra statistics would make T identifiable.

Majumdar, Comtet and Randon-Furling (2010) treat planar hulls and give a combinatorial reduction to one-dimensional extrema. The mean volume of the convex hull of a Lévy process in dimension 3 and higher is not given by Theorem 6.4. That identity uses a planar Cauchy formula.

Theorem 7.2 is a linear recurrence. Stochastic gradient descent on a non-quadratic loss is a nonlinear map. Gurbuzbalaban, Hu, Simsekli, Yuan and Zhu (2022) treat decentralized SGD in a regime where a linear model is informative. Extending the index equation past that regime is open in these notes.

Manzo (2022) asks when an agent-based intervention supports a causal sentence about a population. Definition 5.4 defines the in-model contrast and refuses the population sentence. A research project would state a sufficient condition for the transfer, and a counter-example where the condition fails.

Moran and coauthors (2024) study timeliness criticality. Proposition 4.6 is a Galton-Watson sketch with a single mean m. A transport network has several means, one per link, and a schedule. Classifying which networks sit at a critical timetable is the open problem their title points to.

de Bruyne, Randon-Furling and Redner (2020) optimize first-passage resetting. Example 6.6 solves the one-searcher diffusion problem with reset to a single point. Several searchers, or a reset distribution that is not a point mass, change both the ODE and the equation for z.

Lu, Zhong, Tang and Maggioni (2019) estimate one radial law from many trajectories. An agent-based model whose rule is not a function of pairwise distance, such as the Schelling threshold, is outside that theorem. Krakauer (2023) frames the general meeting of complexity and learning. A concrete open case is a mixed model, part Ising graph and part radial force, and the design of one loss that estimates both pieces.

  • Which statistic of a Schelling grid identifies the threshold T?
  • Is there a closed form for the contact-process critical value on \mathbb{Z}?
  • What replaces \sqrt{8\pi T} for the mean volume in dimension 3?
  • When does an in-model intervention transfer to a policy sentence?
  • How should the Kesten index be stated for a non-quadratic loss?

Exercises

Levels: ★ apply the course, ★★ combine two results or prove a short statement, ★★★ a longer proof or a research-flavoured argument. Exercises 2 and 6 are computational.

  1. ★ A two-state generator. Take the chain of Example 0.4. Write Q, the stationary row, and p(t) = P(X_t = 1 \mid X_0 = 0).

    Solution. The generator is the matrix in Example 0.4. The stationary row is (2/3,\ 1/3). The master equation p' = 1 - 3p with p(0) = 0 gives p(t) = \frac{1 - e^{-3t}}{3}.

  2. ★ Occupation time, in Python. Simulate Example 0.4 from state 0 up to time 200. Use an exponential clock and the seed below. The printout is the fraction of time spent in state 1.

import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
t = 0.0
state = 0
time_in_1 = 0.0
T = 200.0
while t < T:
    rate = 1.0 if state == 0 else 2.0
    dt = float(rng.exponential(1.0 / rate))
    if t + dt > T:
        if state == 1:
            time_in_1 += T - t
        break
    if state == 1:
        time_in_1 += dt
    t += dt
    state = 1 - state
print(f"{time_in_1 / T:.4f}")
*Solution.* The fence prints 0.3132. The exact stationary probability of state 1 is $1/3$, about 0.3333. The gap is Monte Carlo error on a horizon of length 200. State 1 has the larger holding rate, so the chain spends less time there.

  1. ★ Two voters. Two sites copy each other at rate 1 and start with different opinions. Show that the probability they still differ at time t is e^{-2t}.

    Solution. Either site can copy the other, so the time of the first copy is exponential of rate 2. That copy makes the opinions equal, and further copies do nothing. The survival function of an \mathrm{Exp}(2) law is e^{-2t}.

  2. ★ One particle, two sites. Symmetric exclusion on one edge rings at rate 1 and swaps the two occupation bits. Start with a single particle. Find the stationary distribution on the two configurations.

    Solution. A swap always moves the particle, so the particle jumps at rate 1. The two configurations communicate at rate 1 in each direction. The unique stationary law is uniform, with mass 1/2 on each. This is Proposition 1.5 at density 1/2, conditioned on one particle.

  3. ★★ Metropolis check on two spins. Use Example 2.5, where e^{\beta J} = 3. Compute the acceptance probability of a flip out of ++, and verify detailed balance on that pair. Then prove Theorem 2.3 in general.

    Solution. The acceptance probability out of ++ is 1/9, and the reverse acceptance probability is 1. The products with the Gibbs masses are \frac{9}{20} \cdot \frac{1}{9} = \frac{1}{20}. For a symmetric proposal, \pi(\sigma) \min(1, \pi(\sigma')/\pi(\sigma)) equals \min(\pi(\sigma), \pi(\sigma')), which is unchanged if \sigma and \sigma' are swapped. That is Theorem 2.3.

  4. ★★ Schelling on a torus, in Python. Run the rule of Definition 3.1 on a 20 by 20 torus with 40 empty cells and 180 agents of each type. The threshold is 1/3. The neighbourhood is the eight adjacent cells. Swap a uniform unhappy agent with a uniform empty cell until every agent is happy or 4000 swaps have been tried. Print the initial likeness, the final likeness, and the step at which the loop stops.

import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
n = 20
grid = numpy.array([0] * 40 + [1] * 180 + [2] * 180)
rng.shuffle(grid)
grid = grid.reshape(n, n)

def likeness(grid):
    acc = []
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            s = grid[i, j]
            if s == 0:
                continue
            nb = []
            for di in (-1, 0, 1):
                for dj in (-1, 0, 1):
                    if di == 0 and dj == 0:
                        continue
                    v = grid[(i + di) % n, (j + dj) % n]
                    if v != 0:
                        nb.append(v)
            if nb:
                acc.append(sum(v == s for v in nb) / len(nb))
    return sum(acc) / len(acc)

def movers(grid, thresh=1 / 3):
    out = []
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            s = grid[i, j]
            if s == 0:
                continue
            nb = []
            for di in (-1, 0, 1):
                for dj in (-1, 0, 1):
                    if di == 0 and dj == 0:
                        continue
                    v = grid[(i + di) % n, (j + dj) % n]
                    if v != 0:
                        nb.append(v)
            if nb and (sum(v == s for v in nb) / len(nb) < thresh):
                out.append((i, j))
    return out

start = likeness(grid)
empties = [(i, j) for i in range(n) for j in range(n) if grid[i, j] == 0]
steps = 0
for steps in range(1, 4001):
    bad = movers(grid)
    if not bad:
        break
    i, j = bad[int(rng.integers(len(bad)))]
    k = int(rng.integers(len(empties)))
    ei, ej = empties[k]
    grid[ei, ej] = grid[i, j]
    grid[i, j] = 0
    empties[k] = (i, j)
end = likeness(grid)
print(f"{start:.4f}")
print(f"{end:.4f}")
print(steps)
*Solution.* The fence prints 0.5023, then 0.7356, then 147. The run starts near one half and stops with no unhappy agent. A threshold of one third is a mild local preference. The same-type fraction still rises to 0.7356.

  1. ★★ Final size at R_0 = 2. Derive the identity 1 - r = e^{-R_0 r} under the hypotheses of Theorem 4.1. For R_0 = 2, give the root in (0,1) to three decimals and say why it is not the peak prevalence.

    Solution. From ds/dr = -R_0 s and a vanishing seed, s = e^{-R_0 r}. At the end, s + r = 1.

    For R_0 = 2 the root in (0,1) is about 0.797. The final size is the removed fraction after infection returns to 0. The peak is the maximum of the infected fraction along the way.

  2. ★★ A lumpable triple. States 1,2,3 have blocks A = \{1,2\} and B = \{3\}. Both 1 and 2 jump to 3 at rate 1. State 3 jumps to 1 at rate 2 and to 2 at rate 4. Other off-diagonal rates inside A may be chosen freely. Show that the partition is strongly lumpable, and find the stationary mass of B for the lumped chain.

    Solution. Each state of A sends rate 1 into B, so Definition 5.1 holds. The lumped rates are 1 from A to B and 6 from B to A.

    Stationarity of the two-state chain gives mass 1/7 on B.

  3. ★★★ Mean perimeter, reconstructed. Prove Theorem 6.4. Mark the step that uses a free Brownian projection rather than a Brownian bridge. A bridge would replace the tail P(M_T \ge a) = 2P(B_T \ge a). Do not invent a bridge formula.

    Hints and expected result. The perimeter is the integral of the width over an angle of length \pi. Each projection is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion, with \mathbb{E}[M_T] = \sqrt{2T/\pi} and expected range twice that. The product is \sqrt{8\pi T}. The reflection identity is the step that fails for a bridge. Majumdar, Comtet and Randon-Furling (2010) treat Lévy paths by reducing the hull to one-dimensional extrema.

  4. ★★★ The product with no kick. For the step A of Theorem 7.2 and B = 0, prove that X_n \to 0 almost surely whenever X_0 is finite and nonzero. Then state the index that appears if a nonzero B with a finite mean is restored, and name the theorem that supplies the tail.

    Solution. The log-modulus of X_n is a sum of i.i.d. copies of \log |A|, whose mean is -(\log 2)/3. The strong law sends the sum to -\infty, so X_n goes to 0.

    A nonzero B with a finite mean restores Kesten’s theorem. Theorem 7.2 says the index equals 1. Replacing A by its mean deletes the fluctuation that creates the tail.
