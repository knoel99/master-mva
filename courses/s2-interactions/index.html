Audience: a student with a good M1 in probability, statistics or physics who wants to model systems made of many interacting units, simulate them, learn their rules from data, and understand the extreme events they produce.

Empirical first: place two kinds of agents at random on a 40 × 40 grid and let every agent move whenever fewer than 30% of its neighbours are of its own kind. Nobody asks for more than 30%. Yet after ten rounds of moves everyone is satisfied, and the average share of same-kind neighbours has climbed from 0.50 to 0.74 (Schelling’s model, code in ch. 5). Mild individual rules, strong collective pattern: this gap between the micro and the macro level is what the course is about.

The course studies systems whose behaviour comes from the interactions between their parts. It starts with interacting particle systems, the probabilistic theory of such systems, and moves on to agent-based models of societies, epidemics and markets.

It then asks how to infer interactions and causal effects from data, and closes with the extreme and rare events that interactions generate, from avalanches to heavy tails in stochastic gradient descent.

It shares tools with Introduction to Probabilistic Graphical Models and Deep Generative Models, Graphical Models: Discrete Inference and Learning, Graphs in Machine Learning and Statistical Learning with Extreme Values.

What you will be able to do

  • Write the generator of an interacting particle system from its flip or jump rates, and use it to find invariant measures and martingales.
  • Analyse the contact process, the voter model and the exclusion process with couplings, duality and generator computations.
  • Build Metropolis, heat-bath (Glauber) and Kawasaki dynamics for the Ising model and prove that they are reversible.
  • Design an agent-based model, simulate it exactly with the Gillespie algorithm, and use it as an in silico experiment.
  • Explain emergence and self-organized criticality on the sandpile and on critical branching processes.
  • Reduce an agent-based model to a smaller Markov chain by lumping, and compute absorption probabilities and times.
  • Recover an interaction network with penalised pseudo-likelihood, and learn an interaction law by least squares from trajectories.
  • Separate correlation from causation in data produced by an agent-based model, and adjust for confounding.
  • Tell a light-tailed rare event (conspiracy) from a heavy-tailed one (catastrophe), and compute their probabilities.
  • Compute the mean perimeter of the convex hull of a random walk with a combinatorial lemma, and the tail index of stochastic gradient descent with the Kesten–Goldie theorem.

0. Review: Markov chains, exponential clocks and branching

Every model of this course is a Markov chain on a large state space, run in continuous time by exponential clocks. This chapter collects the facts used later, with short proofs.

The Probability and Statistics refresher covers the same ground at a slower pace.

Discrete-time Markov chains

Definition 0.1 — Markov chain, stationarity, reversibility A Markov chain on a finite set \Sigma with transition matrix P satisfies \mathbb{P}(Z_{t+1} = y \mid Z_0, \dots, Z_t) = P(Z_t, y). A probability vector \pi is stationary if \pi P = \pi. The chain is reversible with respect to \pi when detailed balance holds: \pi(x)\, P(x, y) = \pi(y)\, P(y, x) \qquad \text{for all } x, y \in \Sigma.

Proposition 0.2 — Detailed balance implies stationarity If \pi satisfies detailed balance for P, then \pi is stationary. Moreover, the chain started from \pi has the same law as its time reversal on every window [0, T].

Proof. Sum detailed balance over x: \sum_x \pi(x) P(x, y) = \pi(y) \sum_x P(y, x) = \pi(y).

The path x_0, \dots, x_T has probability \pi(x_0) P(x_0, x_1) \cdots P(x_{T-1}, x_T). Applying detailed balance T times moves \pi to the last point, which gives the probability of the reversed path. ∎

Two standard theorems complete the picture. If P is irreducible and aperiodic, \pi is unique and P^t(x, \cdot) \to \pi from every x. The ergodic theorem adds that time averages converge to averages under \pi.

These facts justify Markov chain Monte Carlo (ch. 3) and long simulations of agent-based models.

Continuous time: exponential clocks

Lemma 0.3 — Competing exponential clocks Let T_1, \dots, T_m be independent, with T_i \sim \mathrm{Exp}(q_i), and let q = q_1 + \dots + q_m.

Then the first ringing time T = \min_i T_i is \mathrm{Exp}(q), the index of the first clock is i with probability q_i / q, and the two are independent.

Proof. For t \geq 0, \mathbb{P}(T > t,\ T = T_1) = \int_t^\infty q_1 e^{-q_1 s} \prod_{i \geq 2} e^{-q_i s}\, ds = \frac{q_1}{q}\, e^{-q t}. This factorises into a function of t times a function of the index. ∎

Definition 0.4 — Continuous-time Markov chain A rate matrix Q on \Sigma has off-diagonal entries q(x, y) \geq 0, the jump rates, and rows summing to zero. Write q(x) = -q(x, x) for the total rate out of x.

The chain waits in x an \mathrm{Exp}(q(x)) time, then jumps to y with probability q(x, y) / q(x). Its transition matrices form the semigroup P_t = e^{tQ}, which solves the Kolmogorov equations \frac{d}{dt} P_t = Q P_t = P_t Q, \qquad P_0 = I. A distribution \pi is stationary if and only if \pi Q = 0; detailed balance reads \pi(x) q(x, y) = \pi(y) q(y, x).

By Lemma 0.3, a state with several possible transitions behaves as a set of independent clocks, one per transition. This gives an exact simulation method.

Algorithm 0.5 — Gillespie (1977) From the current state x:

  1. compute the rates q(x, y) of all possible transitions and their sum q(x);
  2. draw the waiting time \Delta t \sim \mathrm{Exp}(q(x)) and advance the clock;
  3. jump to y with probability q(x, y) / q(x).

By Lemma 0.3 the simulated path has exactly the law of the chain.

Example 0.6 — Immigration and death Individuals arrive at rate a and each one leaves at rate b. The number n of individuals is a birth–death chain. Detailed balance gives \pi(n)\, a = \pi(n+1)\, b\, (n+1), \qquad \text{so} \qquad \pi = \mathrm{Poisson}(a / b). The Gillespie simulation below spends a fraction of time in each state that matches the Poisson weights to two or three decimals.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import poisson

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a, b = 5.0, 1.0                 # arrival rate, death rate per individual
n, t, t_end = 0, 0.0, 20000.0
occupation = np.zeros(40)       # time spent in each state
while t < t_end:
    rates = np.array([a, b * n])            # birth, death
    total = rates.sum()
    dt = rng.exponential(1 / total)         # waiting time ~ Exp(total rate)
    occupation[min(n, 39)] += dt
    t += dt
    n += 1 if rng.random() < rates[0] / total else -1   # which clock rang
emp = occupation / occupation.sum()
for k in [2, 5, 8]:
    print(f"state {k}: time fraction {emp[k]:.4f}   Poisson(a/b) {poisson.pmf(k, a / b):.4f}")

The output is 0.0863, 0.1752 and 0.0656 against 0.0842, 0.1755 and 0.0653.

Branching processes

Definition 0.7 — Galton–Watson process Start from Z_0 = 1 individual. Each individual of generation t has an independent number of children distributed as \xi, with mean m and generating function G(s) = \mathbb{E}\, s^{\xi}: Z_{t+1} = \sum_{i=1}^{Z_t} \xi_{t, i}.

Theorem 0.8 — Extinction The extinction probability q is the smallest root of G(s) = s in [0, 1]. Unless \xi = 1 almost surely,

q = 1 \iff m \leq 1 .

Proof. Conditioning on the first generation, q_t = \mathbb{P}(Z_t = 0) satisfies q_{t+1} = G(q_t) with q_0 = 0. The sequence increases to q, and continuity of G gives q = G(q).

If s^\ast \in [0, 1] is another root, induction with G increasing gives q_t \leq s^\ast, hence q \leq s^\ast.

The generating function is convex, with G(1) = 1 and G'(1) = m.

If m \leq 1 and \xi is not constant, the convex function G(s) - s is positive on [0, 1), so q = 1.

If m > 1, G(s) < s just below 1 while G(0) \geq 0, so a root lies in [0, 1). ∎

Branching processes describe the early phase of an epidemic (ch. 5), avalanches (ch. 4) and herding clusters (ch. 6). In each case the critical value m = 1 separates small events from macroscopic ones.

Key formula — the semigroup of a continuous-time chain P_t = e^{tQ}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt} P_t = Q P_t = P_t Q

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Detailed balance implies stationarity and time reversibility; it is the easiest way to find a stationary law.
  • A continuous-time chain is a set of competing exponential clocks: the first one rings after an \mathrm{Exp}(q(x)) time, and it is clock y with probability q(x, y)/q(x). The Gillespie algorithm simulates it exactly.
  • A Galton–Watson process dies out surely if and only if its mean offspring m \leq 1; its extinction probability is the smallest fixed point of G.

1. Interacting particle systems: Markov processes, semigroups and generators

An interacting particle system is a Markov process on configurations: every site of a graph carries a state, which changes at a rate depending on the states of nearby sites. This chapter sets up the framework (semigroups, generators, construction) and two tools used throughout: monotone coupling and duality.

The standard references are Liggett (2005) and, for the social-dynamics reading of the same models, Aldous (2013).

Configurations, semigroups and generators

Definition 1.1 — Spin system Let S be the countable vertex set of a graph of bounded degree, and X = \{0, 1\}^S the configurations \eta, with the product topology (compact and metrisable). Write \eta^x for \eta with the value at x flipped.

A spin system is given by flip rates c(x, \eta) \geq 0: the value \eta(x) flips at rate c(x, \eta). It has finite range when c(x, \cdot) depends only on \eta(x) and on the values at the neighbours of x.

Definition 1.2 — Markov semigroup A Markov semigroup on C(X) is a family (P_t)_{t \geq 0} of linear operators such that P_0 = I, \qquad P_t P_s = P_{t+s}, \qquad P_t f \geq 0 \text{ if } f \geq 0, \qquad P_t 1 = 1, and \|P_t f - f\|_\infty \to 0 as t \to 0.

A Markov process (\eta_t) defines one by P_t f(\eta) = \mathbb{E}_\eta f(\eta_t). It is a Feller process when P_t maps C(X) into itself.

Definition 1.3 — Generator The generator of (P_t) is L f = \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{P_t f - f}{t}, defined on the set D(L) of functions for which the limit exists in sup norm. For a spin system, on functions that depend on finitely many coordinates, L f(\eta) = \sum_{x \in S} c(x, \eta)\, \big[f(\eta^x) - f(\eta)\big].

Proposition 1.4 — Finite systems If S is finite, L is the rate matrix Q of Definition 0.4 acting on functions, and P_t = e^{tL}. Moreover:

  1. (Dynkin) for every f, the following process is a martingale: M_t^f = f(\eta_t) - f(\eta_0) - \int_0^t L f(\eta_s)\, ds ;
  2. a probability \mu is invariant, \mu P_t = \mu for all t, if and only if \int L f\, d\mu = 0 for every f.

Proof. (1) By the Markov property, the conditional expectation of M^f_{t+h} - M^f_t given the past is P_h f(\eta_t) - f(\eta_t) - \int_0^h P_s L f(\eta_t)\, ds = 0, because \frac{d}{ds} P_s f = P_s L f.

(2) \frac{d}{dt} \int P_t f\, d\mu = \int L (P_t f)\, d\mu. If \int L g\, d\mu = 0 for all g, this derivative vanishes and \int P_t f\, d\mu = \int f\, d\mu. Conversely, differentiate \mu P_t f = \mu f at t = 0. ∎

On an infinite graph the sum defining L f is infinite, and existence is a theorem.

Theorem 1.5 — Existence (Liggett, 2005, ch. I and III) Suppose the flip rates are bounded, \sup_{x, \eta} c(x, \eta) < \infty, and depend weakly on far-away sites: \sup_x \sum_{u \in S} \sup_\eta |c(x, \eta^u) - c(x, \eta)| < \infty. Then the closure of L on finitely-supported functions generates a unique Feller semigroup on C(X).

The graphical construction

For finite-range systems there is a direct construction (Harris, 1972) that also couples all initial configurations. Let \bar c bound the rates. Give every site x two independent Poisson clocks of rate \bar c, an up clock and a down clock, and attach an independent uniform U to every ring.

At a ring of the up clock of x, a configuration with \eta(x) = 0 flips to 1 if U < c(x, \eta)/\bar c. Down rings act in the same way on sites in state 1. By thinning, each site flips at the right rate. Infinite graphs need one more argument, given in ch. 12.

Definition 1.6 — Attractive system Order configurations coordinatewise. A spin system is attractive if, whenever \eta \leq \zeta, c(x, \eta) \leq c(x, \zeta) \ \text{ if } \eta(x) = \zeta(x) = 0, \qquad c(x, \eta) \geq c(x, \zeta) \ \text{ if } \eta(x) = \zeta(x) = 1 . More ones around x make a 0 at x more likely to become 1, and a 1 less likely to become 0.

Proposition 1.7 — Monotone coupling For an attractive system, run the graphical construction from \eta_0 \leq \zeta_0 with the same clocks and uniforms. Then

\eta_t \leq \zeta_t \quad \text{for all } t \geq 0 .

Proof. Up and down rings never coincide, so it suffices to check one ring at a site x. At an up ring, if \eta(x) = \zeta(x) = 0, attractiveness gives c(x, \eta) \leq c(x, \zeta): if \eta flips up, so does \zeta.

If \eta(x) = 0 < \zeta(x) = 1, the order holds whatever \eta does. Down rings are symmetric. ∎

Duality

Definition 1.8 — Duality Two Markov processes (\eta_t) on X and (\xi_t) on Y are dual with respect to a bounded function H on X \times Y if, for all initial states and times, \mathbb{E}_\eta H(\eta_t, \xi) = \mathbb{E}^\xi H(\eta, \xi_t). For finite state spaces it suffices that the generators match, L_X H(\cdot, \xi)(\eta) = L_Y H(\eta, \cdot)(\xi). Then the derivative in s of \mathbb{E}\, H(\eta_s, \xi_{t-s}), with independent processes, vanishes.

Duality turns a question about an infinite configuration into one about a few dual particles. In ch. 2, the voter model is dual to coalescing random walks and the contact process is dual to itself.

The code below writes the generator of the contact process of ch. 2 on a cycle of 4 sites as a 16 \times 16 matrix and computes P_t = e^{tL}. The empty configuration is absorbing, and the probability of having reached it from the full configuration grows to 1.

import itertools
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm

# Contact process on the cycle with 4 sites: the generator as a 16 x 16 matrix.
S, lam = 4, 1.5
nbrs = {x: [(x - 1) % S, (x + 1) % S] for x in range(S)}
states = list(itertools.product([0, 1], repeat=S))
index = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(states)}
Q = np.zeros((2**S, 2**S))
for s in states:
    for x in range(S):
        rate = 1.0 if s[x] == 1 else lam * sum(s[y] for y in nbrs[x])  # c(x, eta)
        if rate > 0:
            flipped = list(s); flipped[x] = 1 - s[x]
            Q[index[s], index[tuple(flipped)]] += rate
    Q[index[s], index[s]] = -Q[index[s]].sum()

full, empty = index[(1,) * S], index[(0,) * S]
for t in [1.0, 5.0, 20.0]:
    P = expm(t * Q)                                  # P_t = exp(tQ)
    print(f"t={t:5.1f}: rows sum to 1: {np.allclose(P.sum(1), 1)}, "
          f"P_t(full, empty) = {P[full, empty]:.4f}")

The output gives 0.0555, 0.4494 and 0.9301 at times 1, 5 and 20: every finite contact process dies out, and the question of ch. 2 is how long it takes.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • A spin system is defined by its flip rates; its generator is L f(\eta) = \sum_x c(x, \eta) [f(\eta^x) - f(\eta)].
  • Dynkin’s martingale and the criterion \int L f\, d\mu = 0 turn questions about the dynamics into computations with L.
  • The graphical construction builds the process from Poisson clocks and couples all initial conditions; for attractive systems the coupling preserves order.
  • Duality replaces a configuration by a few dual particles.

2. The contact process, the voter model and the exclusion process

Three models carry most of the theory of interacting particle systems: an infection that spreads and heals, opinions that are copied, and particles that cannot overlap. Each one shows a different tool at work: coupling and a generator bound, a martingale and duality, and an explicit invariant measure.

The contact process

Definition 2.1 — Contact process On a graph G, each site is infected (1) or healthy (0). An infected site recovers at rate 1; a healthy site is infected at rate \lambda times its number of infected neighbours n_x(\eta): c(x, \eta) = \eta(x) + \lambda\, \big(1 - \eta(x)\big)\, n_x(\eta). The empty configuration is absorbing. The system is attractive, so by Proposition 1.7 the survival probability from one infected site, \theta(\lambda), is non-decreasing in \lambda. The critical value is \lambda_c = \inf\{\lambda : \theta(\lambda) > 0\}.

Theorem 2.2 — The critical value is positive On \mathbb{Z}^d, started from finitely many infected sites, \mathbb{E}\, |\eta_t| \leq |\eta_0|\, e^{(2d\lambda - 1)t}, \qquad \text{hence} \qquad \lambda_c \geq \frac{1}{2d} .

Proof. Take f(\eta) = |\eta|, the number of infected sites. A healthy site with n_x infected neighbours adds 1 at rate \lambda n_x; an infected site removes 1 at rate 1. Since every infected site has 2d neighbours, L f(\eta) = \lambda \sum_{x : \eta(x) = 0} n_x(\eta) - |\eta| \leq (2 d \lambda - 1)\, |\eta| . Dynkin’s formula (Proposition 1.4, for the process stopped when it leaves a large box, which is then let grow) and Gronwall’s lemma give the bound. If 2d\lambda < 1, then \mathbb{P}(\eta_t \neq \emptyset) \leq \mathbb{E}\, |\eta_t| \to 0 . ∎

Better bounds are known: 1/(2d-1) \leq \lambda_c \leq 2/d (Liggett, 2005, ch. VI). On \mathbb{Z}, simulations give \lambda_c \approx 1.649. The graphical construction also shows that the contact process is self-dual: reversing time and arrows maps it onto itself.

On the complete graph with N sites and infection rate \lambda / N per pair, the density of infected sites follows, as N \to \infty, the mean-field equation \dot\rho = \lambda \rho (1 - \rho) - \rho . The endemic state \rho^\ast = 1 - 1/\lambda exists and is stable if and only if \lambda > 1. On any finite graph the process still dies out almost surely, but above criticality only after a time that grows very fast with the size: a metastable state.

The voter model

Definition 2.3 — Voter model On a connected graph with degrees d_x, each site holds opinion 0 or 1. At rate 1, a voter picks a uniform neighbour and adopts its opinion: c(x, \eta) = \frac{1}{d_x}\, \#\{y \sim x : \eta(y) \neq \eta(x)\}. The two consensus configurations are absorbing.

Proposition 2.4 — Who wins? On a finite connected graph with |E| edges, the degree-weighted magnetisation M_t = \frac{1}{2|E|} \sum_x d_x\, \eta_t(x) is a martingale. Consensus is reached almost surely, and

\mathbb{P}(\text{consensus on } 1) = \frac{\sum_x d_x\, \eta_0(x)}{2|E|} .

Proof. When x copies its neighbour y, which happens at rate 1/d_x, \sum_z d_z \eta(z) changes by d_x (\eta(y) - \eta(x)). Hence L \Big( \sum_z d_z \eta(z) \Big) = \sum_x \sum_{y \sim x} \big( \eta(y) - \eta(x) \big) = 0, because every edge appears in both directions. By Dynkin, M_t is a martingale.

From any configuration, a finite sequence of copies leads to consensus, so the absorption time \tau is finite almost surely. Optional stopping for the bounded martingale gives \mathbb{E}\, M_\tau = M_0, and M_\tau = 1 exactly on consensus on 1. ∎

A hub counts as much as all its leaves. On a star with five leaves and a path of three more sites hanging from a leaf, a single opinion-1 voter at the centre wins with probability 5/16 \approx 0.31, not 1/9:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
# A star (centre 0, leaves 1..5) with a path 5-6-7-8 hanging from leaf 5.
edges = [(0, k) for k in range(1, 6)] + [(5, 6), (6, 7), (7, 8)]
n = 9
nbrs = [[] for _ in range(n)]
for u, v in edges:
    nbrs[u].append(v); nbrs[v].append(u)
deg = np.array([len(a) for a in nbrs])

eta0 = np.zeros(n, dtype=int); eta0[0] = 1          # only the centre holds opinion 1
wins, trials = 0, 4000
for _ in range(trials):
    eta = eta0.copy()
    while 0 < eta.sum() < n:
        x = rng.integers(n)                          # every voter rings at rate 1
        eta[x] = eta[nbrs[x][rng.integers(deg[x])]]  # copy a uniform neighbour
    wins += eta[0]
print(f"P(consensus on 1): simulated {wins / trials:.3f}, "
      f"degree-weighted prediction {deg @ eta0 / deg.sum():.3f}, "
      f"unweighted fraction {eta0.mean():.3f}")

The output is 0.313 against the prediction 0.312.

Proposition 2.5 — Duality with coalescing random walks In the graphical construction, draw an arrow from y to x each time x copies y. Follow the arrows backwards from (x, t): the lineage of x is a continuous-time random walk run backwards in time, and

\eta_t(x) = \eta_0\big(X^x_t\big).

Lineages started from different sites move independently until they meet, and then coalesce.

Proof. The opinion at (x, t) was copied from the site at the tail of the last arrow into x before t, and so on back to time 0. Arrows into x come at rate 1 from uniform neighbours, which is the jump rule of a simple random walk. Two lineages at the same site at the same time follow the same arrows afterwards. ∎

On \mathbb{Z}^d, two lineages meet surely if and only if the random walk is recurrent: opinions form ever larger clusters in d \leq 2 and coexist in d \geq 3 (Theorem 12.2).

The exclusion process

Definition 2.6 — Exclusion process Particles occupy the sites of S, at most one per site. A particle at x tries to jump to y at rate p(x, y), and the jump is suppressed if y is occupied.

Write \eta^{xy} for the configuration after exchanging the values at x and y. The generator is L f(\eta) = \sum_{x, y} \eta(x)\, (1 - \eta(y))\, p(x, y)\, \big[f(\eta^{xy}) - f(\eta)\big].

Proposition 2.7 — Invariant measures and one-point means Let S be finite and p symmetric.

  1. For every \rho \in [0, 1], the product Bernoulli measure \nu_\rho is reversible.
  2. The mean occupations u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}\, \eta_t(x) solve the discrete heat equation \frac{d}{dt} u_t(x) = \sum_y p(x, y)\, \big(u_t(y) - u_t(x)\big).

Proof. (1) \nu_\rho(\eta) = \rho^{|\eta|} (1 - \rho)^{|S| - |\eta|} depends only on the number of particles, which jumps conserve. The jump \eta \to \eta^{xy} has rate p(x, y) and the reverse jump rate p(y, x) = p(x, y), so detailed balance holds.

(2) Apply L to f(\eta) = \eta(x): the gain from jumps into x and the loss from jumps out of x are L f = \sum_y p(y, x)\, \eta(y) (1 - \eta(x)) - \sum_y p(x, y)\, \eta(x) (1 - \eta(y)) . By symmetry the products \eta(x)\eta(y) cancel, leaving \sum_y p(x, y)(\eta(y) - \eta(x)). Take expectations. ∎

The particles interact, yet one-point means move like independent random walks. For a doubly stochastic p the measures \nu_\rho stay invariant (Liggett, 2005, ch. VIII). The totally asymmetric version and its hydrodynamic limit are in ch. 12.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The contact process survives only above a positive critical value: a generator bound gives \lambda_c \geq 1/(2d), and mean field gives the endemic density 1 - 1/\lambda.
  • In the voter model the degree-weighted magnetisation is a martingale, which gives the winning probability; duality with coalescing random walks gives clustering in d \leq 2 and coexistence in d \geq 3.
  • Symmetric exclusion has Bernoulli product invariant measures, and its one-point means solve the heat equation.

3. Stochastic Ising models

The Ising model is the simplest system in which interactions alone create a phase transition. Its stochastic dynamics are both models of physical relaxation and Markov chain Monte Carlo samplers, the MCMC of Introduction to Probabilistic Graphical Models.

Gibbs measures and reversible dynamics

Definition 3.1 — Ising model On a finite graph G = (V, E), a configuration is \sigma \in \{-1, +1\}^V. With coupling J > 0 and field h, the energy and the Gibbs measure at inverse temperature \beta are H(\sigma) = -J \sum_{\{x, y\} \in E} \sigma_x \sigma_y - h \sum_x \sigma_x, \qquad \mu_\beta(\sigma) = \frac{e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}}{Z_\beta} . With the local field m_x(\sigma) = \sum_{y \sim x} \sigma_y, flipping the spin at x changes the energy by \Delta_x(\sigma) = H(\sigma^x) - H(\sigma) = 2 \sigma_x \big( J m_x(\sigma) + h \big).

Definition 3.2 — Stochastic Ising model A spin-flip system with positive finite-range rates c(x, \sigma) is a stochastic Ising model if it satisfies detailed balance with respect to \mu_\beta: c(x, \sigma) = c(x, \sigma^x)\, e^{-\beta \Delta_x(\sigma)} .

Proposition 3.3 — Metropolis, heat-bath and Glauber dynamics The following rates define stochastic Ising models:

  • Metropolis: c(x, \sigma) = \min\big(1, e^{-\beta \Delta_x(\sigma)}\big) ;
  • heat-bath, or Gibbs sampler, which resamples \sigma_x from \mu_\beta(\cdot \mid \sigma_{V \setminus x}): c(x, \sigma) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\beta \Delta_x(\sigma)}} .

For h = 0, the heat-bath rate equals Glauber’s (1963) rate, c(x, \sigma) = \tfrac12 \big( 1 - \sigma_x \tanh(\beta J m_x(\sigma)) \big).

Proof. Write \Delta = \Delta_x(\sigma) and note \Delta_x(\sigma^x) = -\Delta. For Metropolis with \Delta \geq 0, the ratio of the rates is e^{-\beta\Delta} / 1; the case \Delta < 0 is symmetric. For heat-bath, \frac{c(x, \sigma)}{c(x, \sigma^x)} = \frac{1 + e^{-\beta \Delta}}{1 + e^{\beta \Delta}} = e^{-\beta \Delta}. The conditional law of \sigma_x is proportional to e^{\beta s (J m_x + h)} for s = \pm 1, so the flipped value has probability 1/(1 + e^{\beta\Delta}).

For h = 0, 1/(1 + e^{2u}) = \frac12 (1 - \tanh u) with u = \beta J \sigma_x m_x, and \tanh(\sigma_x v) = \sigma_x \tanh v. ∎

Proposition 3.4 — Kawasaki dynamics Let neighbouring spins with \sigma_x \neq \sigma_y exchange their values at the rate \min\big(1, e^{-\beta (H(\sigma^{xy}) - H(\sigma))}\big). This dynamics conserves the magnetisation \sum_x \sigma_x, and it is reversible with respect to \mu_\beta conditioned on the magnetisation.

Proof. The exchange is an involution, (\sigma^{xy})^{xy} = \sigma, so the Metropolis argument applies verbatim. On a slice of fixed magnetisation the field term is constant and the conditioned measure is proportional to e^{-\beta H}. ∎

Kawasaki dynamics is an exclusion process (ch. 2) with interactions between particles, a lattice gas. The relocation moves of Schelling’s model (ch. 5) are of the same kind.

The Curie–Weiss phase transition

Theorem 3.5 — Curie–Weiss model On the complete graph with N sites, take h = 0 and coupling J/N, so that H = -\frac{J N}{2} m^2 up to a constant, with m = \frac1N \sum_x \sigma_x. Under \mu_\beta, the magnetisation has probability \mu_\beta(m) = \exp\big(-N f_\beta(m) + o(N)\big), \qquad f_\beta(m) = I(m) - \frac{\beta J}{2} m^2 + \text{const}, with I(m) = \frac{1+m}{2} \ln(1+m) + \frac{1-m}{2} \ln(1-m). The minimisers of f_\beta solve

m = \tanh(\beta J m) .

If \beta J \leq 1 the only solution is m = 0. If \beta J > 1 there are two minimisers \pm m^\ast, with m^\ast \approx \sqrt{3(\beta J - 1)} near the critical point.

Proof. The number of configurations with magnetisation m is a binomial coefficient, and Stirling’s formula gives e^{N(\ln 2 - I(m)) + o(N)}. Each has weight e^{\beta J N m^2 / 2}.

Stationary points solve I'(m) = \operatorname{artanh} m = \beta J m. At m = 0, f_\beta''(0) = 1 - \beta J, so 0 is a minimum if and only if \beta J < 1; at \beta J = 1 the quartic term of I keeps it a minimum.

For \beta J > 1, the concave function \tanh(\beta J m) crosses m once on (0, 1). Expanding \tanh u \approx u - u^3/3 gives m^2 \approx 3(\beta J - 1)/(\beta J)^3. ∎

The dynamics follow the landscape f_\beta. Above the critical temperature, heat-bath dynamics on the complete graph mixes in order N \log N steps (Levin, Peres and Wilmer, 2009, ch. 15). Below it, going from +m^\ast to -m^\ast means crossing the barrier N[f_\beta(0) - f_\beta(m^\ast)]: the time is exponential in N, a metastable state again.

On the square lattice with J = 1, Onsager’s critical temperature is T_c = 2 / \ln(1 + \sqrt 2) \approx 2.269. Below it the spontaneous magnetisation is (1 - \sinh(2\beta)^{-4})^{1/8} (Yang, 1952), which equals 0.986 at T = 1.5. The Metropolis simulation below updates the two colours of a checkerboard in turn, because spins of one colour do not interact.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
L = 32
i, j = np.indices((L, L))
colours = [(i + j) % 2 == 0, (i + j) % 2 == 1]       # checkerboard: no two neighbours updated together

def sweep(s, beta):
    for mask in colours:
        nb = np.roll(s, 1, 0) + np.roll(s, -1, 0) + np.roll(s, 1, 1) + np.roll(s, -1, 1)
        dH = 2 * s * nb                               # energy change of a flip (J = 1, h = 0)
        accept = rng.random(s.shape) < np.exp(-beta * dH)   # Metropolis: min(1, e^{-beta dH})
        s[mask & accept] *= -1

for T in [1.5, 2.27, 3.5]:
    s = np.ones((L, L), dtype=int)
    m = []
    for t in range(3000):
        sweep(s, 1 / T)
        if t >= 1000:
            m.append(abs(s.mean()))
    print(f"T = {T:4.2f}: mean |magnetisation| = {np.mean(m):.3f}")

The output is 0.986, 0.636 and 0.063: ordered, critical with large fluctuations, and disordered. Random couplings of both signs give spin glasses (ch. 12).

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • A stochastic Ising model is a spin-flip dynamics in detailed balance with the Gibbs measure; Metropolis and heat-bath (Glauber) rates both qualify, and heat-bath is the Gibbs sampler.
  • Kawasaki exchanges conserve the magnetisation and sample the canonical measure.
  • In mean field the magnetisation solves m = \tanh(\beta J m), with a transition at \beta J = 1; below the transition the dynamics is metastable.

4. Complex systems, emergence and self-organized criticality

What is a complex system, and what does it mean to explain one of its collective patterns? This chapter gives the working answer of the course: grow the pattern from interacting micro-rules, and recognise the signatures of criticality.

Emergence and generative explanation

Definition 4.1 — Micro, macro, emergence A system of N units has a micro-state Z_t = (z_1, \dots, z_N) \in S^N that evolves by local rules, and macro-observables \Phi(Z_t) \in \mathbb{R}^k: a density, a magnetisation, a segregation index.

A macro-regularity is emergent when it is a property of \Phi(Z_t), often in the limit N \to \infty, that no single unit’s rule contains: consensus, segregation, a phase transition.

Complex systems combine many units, an interaction network, feedback, heterogeneity and no central controller (Sethna, 2021). Krakauer (2023) argues that complexity science and machine learning should be unified: both try to compress high-dimensional interacting systems into predictive models.

Definition 4.2 — Agent-based model An agent-based model (ABM) consists of agents i = 1, \dots, N with states z_i, an environment or interaction network, behavioural rules that give the probability or rate of a change of z_i as a function of z_i and of the states in a neighbourhood, and a schedule (synchronous, random sequential or continuous-time).

An ABM with random rules is a Markov chain on S^N, usually too large to write down; ch. 7 reduces it.

Epstein’s generative social science holds that explaining a macro-regularity means growing it in silico from micro-rules: “If you didn’t grow it, you didn’t explain it” (Epstein, 1999).

An ABM is also a laboratory: changing one rule while everything else, random numbers included, stays fixed is a controlled experiment that the real system rarely allows, such as closing schools (ch. 5) or randomising a treatment (ch. 8).

Critical branching: the mean-field avalanche

At a critical point fluctuations have no characteristic size, and event sizes follow power laws (ch. 12 shows why scale invariance forces them). An avalanche in which each unstable unit destabilises a random number of others is a branching process (ch. 0). Its total size is explicit for Poisson offspring.

Theorem 4.3 — Total progeny and the exponent 3/2 Let S be the total progeny of a Galton–Watson process with Poisson(\mu) offspring, \mu \leq 1. Then S has the Borel law \mathbb{P}(S = s) = \frac{e^{-\mu s} (\mu s)^{s-1}}{s!}, \qquad s \geq 1 . For \mu < 1, \mathbb{E}\, S = 1/(1 - \mu). At criticality, \mu = 1,

\mathbb{P}(S = s) \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, s^{-3/2} .

Proof. Explore the tree one individual at a time. After k explorations, the number of individuals found but not yet explored is W_k = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\xi_i - 1), and S is the first k with W_k = 0.

Cycle lemma. Let y_1, \dots, y_s be integers \geq -1 with sum -1. Exactly one of the s cyclic shifts has all its partial sums \geq 0 before the last one.

Indeed, let j be the first index in \{1, \dots, s\} at which the partial sums reach their minimum \mu; the good shift starts at y_{j+1}. Later partial sums in the period are \geq \mu. Earlier ones are \geq \mu + 1, and drop by exactly 1 when moved to the next period.

The increments are exchangeable, so the cycle lemma gives the hitting-time theorem (Dwass, 1969): \mathbb{P}(S = s) = \frac{1}{s}\, \mathbb{P}(\xi_1 + \dots + \xi_s = s - 1). A sum of s Poisson(\mu) variables is Poisson(\mu s), which gives the Borel law. The mean is \sum_t \mu^t, the expected sum of the generation sizes. At \mu = 1, Stirling’s formula s! \sim \sqrt{2\pi s}\, s^s e^{-s} gives the tail. ∎

Self-organized criticality: the sandpile

Definition 4.4 — Abelian sandpile (Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld, 1987) On an L \times L grid, each site carries z_x \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\} grains in a stable state. A grain is added at a uniform site. Any site with z_x \geq 4 topples: it loses 4 grains and each neighbour gains one, and grains that leave the grid are lost. The avalanche size is the number of topplings.

Proposition 4.5 — Abelian property (Dhar, 1990) The final stable configuration and the number of topplings at each site do not depend on the order in which unstable sites are toppled.

Proof. Toppling a site only adds grains elsewhere, so an unstable site stays unstable until it topples. Toppling x then y gives the same configuration as y then x, because the changes are additions.

Every relaxation terminates: grains are lost at the boundary, and an infinite relaxation would topple some site infinitely often and its neighbours too, up to the boundary. Local confluence and termination imply a unique final state (Newman’s lemma); the toppling counts are determined in the same way. ∎

Nothing is tuned, yet the stationary state is critical. The drive is slow (one grain at a time), relaxation is fast, and grains leave only at the boundary. If avalanches were subcritical, grains would accumulate and make them larger; if supercritical, large avalanches would empty the grid.

In mean field, a toppling destabilises each of 4 neighbours with probability p_3, the fraction of sites at height 3. The branching ratio 4 p_3 is driven to 1, the critical case of Theorem 4.3, with exponent 3/2. In two dimensions the exponent differs and is not known exactly.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
L = 30
z = np.zeros((L, L), dtype=int)          # heights; grains falling off the border are lost

def add_grain(z):
    """Drop one grain at a random site, relax, return the avalanche size (number of topplings)."""
    x, y = rng.integers(L, size=2)
    z[x, y] += 1
    stack, size = [(x, y)] if z[x, y] >= 4 else [], 0
    while stack:
        x, y = stack.pop()
        if z[x, y] < 4:
            continue
        z[x, y] -= 4; size += 1
        for u, v in ((x + 1, y), (x - 1, y), (x, y + 1), (x, y - 1)):
            if 0 <= u < L and 0 <= v < L:
                z[u, v] += 1
                if z[u, v] >= 4:
                    stack.append((u, v))
        if z[x, y] >= 4:
            stack.append((x, y))
    return size

sizes = np.array([add_grain(z) for _ in range(40000)])[10000:]   # discard the transient
print(f"mean height {z.mean():.3f}, fraction of grains causing an avalanche {np.mean(sizes > 0):.3f}")
s = sizes[sizes > 0]
for lo, hi in [(1, 10), (10, 100), (100, 1000)]:
    print(f"P({lo} <= S < {hi} | S > 0) = {np.mean((s >= lo) & (s < hi)):.3f}")
# slope of the log-log histogram on logarithmic bins between 3 and 300 topplings
bins = np.unique(np.logspace(0.5, 2.5, 9).astype(int))
hist, _ = np.histogram(s, bins=bins)
dens = hist / np.diff(bins)
slope = np.polyfit(np.log(np.sqrt(bins[:-1] * bins[1:])), np.log(dens), 1)[0]
print(f"log-log slope of the size density on [3, 300]: {slope:.2f}")

The mean height settles at 2.098, close to the infinite-lattice value 17/8 = 2.125. The avalanche sizes spread evenly over three decades: 41%, 36% and 23% of them fall in [1, 10), [10, 100) and [100, 1000). The fitted slope is -1.06 on this small grid.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • An emergent regularity belongs to a macro-observable, not to any individual rule; an ABM explains it by growing it, and is a laboratory for controlled experiments.
  • Scale invariance forces a power law; criticality is the usual source of scale invariance.
  • A critical Poisson branching process has the Borel total progeny, with tail s^{-3/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}; the proof is a cycle lemma.
  • The sandpile reaches a critical state without tuning: slow drive, fast relaxation and boundary dissipation.

5. Agent-based models: segregation and epidemics

Two classic agent-based models, one from social science and one from public health, show how individual rules produce collective outcomes. The second also shows how a model becomes a decision tool.

Schelling’s segregation model

Definition 5.1 — Schelling’s model (Schelling, 1971) On an L \times L torus, a fraction v of the cells is vacant and the others hold agents of two types in equal numbers. An agent is satisfied when at least a fraction T of its occupied neighbours (8-neighbourhood) share its type. In each round, the unsatisfied agents, in random order, move to uniformly chosen vacant cells.

With L = 40, v = 0.1 and the tolerant threshold T = 0.3, the mean share of same-type neighbours rises from 0.50 to 0.74, and all agents are satisfied after ten rounds:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
L, vacancy, T = 40, 0.1, 0.3          # grid side, vacant fraction, tolerance threshold
grid = rng.choice([0, 1, 2], size=(L, L), p=[vacancy, 0.45, 0.45])  # 0 = vacant

def like_fraction(g):
    """For each cell: fraction of occupied neighbours (8-neighbourhood, torus) of the same type."""
    same = np.zeros(g.shape); occ = np.zeros(g.shape)
    for dx in (-1, 0, 1):
        for dy in (-1, 0, 1):
            if dx or dy:
                h = np.roll(np.roll(g, dx, 0), dy, 1)
                same += (h == g) & (h > 0); occ += h > 0
    return np.where(occ > 0, same / np.maximum(occ, 1), 1.0)

def segregation(g):
    f = like_fraction(g)
    return f[g > 0].mean()

print(f"initial mean like-neighbour fraction: {segregation(grid):.3f}")
for sweep in range(60):
    f = like_fraction(grid)
    unhappy = np.argwhere((grid > 0) & (f < T))
    if len(unhappy) == 0:
        break
    for i, j in unhappy[rng.permutation(len(unhappy))]:
        empty = np.argwhere(grid == 0)
        k = empty[rng.integers(len(empty))]
        grid[k[0], k[1]], grid[i, j] = grid[i, j], 0
print(f"rounds of moves: {sweep}, unhappy agents left: {len(unhappy)}")
print(f"final mean like-neighbour fraction  : {segregation(grid):.3f}")

A smoother version of the rules has an explicit stationary law. Let the utility u of an agent be its number of same-type neighbours, and let \Phi count the adjacent pairs of same-type agents.

Proposition 5.2 — Logit Schelling dynamics is a Gibbs sampler At each step, pick a uniform agent and a uniform vacant cell, and move the agent there with probability 1 / (1 + e^{-\beta \Delta u}), where \Delta u is its utility after the move minus before. This chain is reversible with respect to

\pi_\beta(\text{configuration}) \propto e^{\beta \Phi(\text{configuration})} .

Proof. Only the pairs that contain the moving agent change, so \Delta \Phi = \Delta u exactly: the old cell is vacant after the move and the new cell was vacant before. The proposal is symmetric, because the numbers of agents and vacancies are fixed and the reverse move picks the same agent and the old cell.

The acceptance is the heat-bath rule of Proposition 3.3 with energy -\Phi, and its ratio for a move and its reverse is e^{\beta \Delta \Phi}. ∎

Each agent maximises its own utility, and the collective state samples a Gibbs measure for the potential \Phi. This is Kawasaki dynamics (Proposition 3.4) for a lattice gas with two species; large \beta means phase separation. Blume (1993) gives the general statement for potential games.

Stochastic epidemics

In the stochastic SIR model, a population of N individuals is split into susceptible, infected and removed. Each infective meets a uniform individual at rate \beta and infects it if it is susceptible; it is removed at rate \gamma. The total rates are \beta S I / N and \gamma I, and the basic reproduction number is R_0 = \beta / \gamma.

Proposition 5.3 — Probability of a major outbreak In the early phase, an infective infects a Poisson number of others with mean \beta D, where D \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\gamma) is its infectious period. This mixture is geometric, and started from one case the outbreak dies out early with probability

q = \min(1, 1/R_0) .

Proof. With p = \gamma / (\beta + \gamma), mixing gives \mathbb{P}(\xi = k) = p (1 - p)^k, with generating function G(s) = p / (1 - (1 - p)s). The equation G(s) = s becomes (1 - p) s^2 - s + p = 0, with roots 1 and p/(1 - p) = 1/R_0. Theorem 0.8 picks the smaller. ∎

Proposition 5.4 — Final size As N \to \infty, the fractions s, i, r follow \dot s = -\beta s i, \dot r = \gamma i. Starting from a vanishing fraction of infectives, the final fraction ever infected, z, solves

z = 1 - e^{-R_0 z},

which has a positive root if and only if R_0 > 1.

Proof. Divide the two equations: ds/dr = -R_0 s, so s(\infty) = s(0)\, e^{-R_0 r(\infty)}. With s(0) \to 1 and r(\infty) = z = 1 - s(\infty), the equation follows. The function 1 - e^{-R_0 z} is concave with slope R_0 at 0. ∎

For R_0 = 2.5, z = 0.893. Infections stop growing once the immune fraction reaches the herd-immunity threshold 1 - 1/R_0 = 0.6, but the epidemic overshoots it. Reducing transmission by 40% (R_0 = 1.5) brings z down to 0.583.

The simulation of the jump chain below has N = 1000 and one initial case. It finds 41.0% of minor outbreaks, against 1/R_0 = 0.40, and a mean major outbreak of 0.891, against 0.893.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
N, R0, gamma = 1000, 2.5, 1.0
beta = R0 * gamma

def final_size(S, I):
    """Stochastic SIR (well-mixed): only the jump chain matters for the final size."""
    while I > 0:
        p_inf = beta * S * I / N / (beta * S * I / N + gamma * I)
        if rng.random() < p_inf:
            S, I = S - 1, I + 1
        else:
            I -= 1
    return 1 - S / N

z = np.array([final_size(N - 1, 1) for _ in range(400)])
z_det = brentq(lambda z: z - 1 + np.exp(-R0 * z), 1e-6, 1)   # z = 1 - exp(-R0 z)
print(f"minor outbreaks (< 10%): {np.mean(z < 0.1):.3f}   branching prediction 1/R0 = {1 / R0:.3f}")
print(f"mean size of major outbreaks: {z[z >= 0.1].mean():.3f}   final-size equation: {z_det:.3f}")
print(f"herd-immunity threshold 1 - 1/R0 = {1 - 1 / R0:.3f}")

Proposition 5.5 — Epidemics on networks (Newman, 2002) On a random network with degree distribution (p_k), let each infective infect each neighbour with probability T. A major outbreak is possible if and only if T\, \frac{\mathbb{E}[k^2] - \mathbb{E}[k]}{\mathbb{E}[k]} > 1 . Sketch. An individual reached along an edge has degree k with probability k p_k / \mathbb{E}[k] (the friendship paradox) and k - 1 onward edges. The early outbreak is a branching process with this mean offspring. With heavy-tailed degrees, \mathbb{E}[k^2] is huge and the threshold almost vanishes.

Public health modelling

Adam (2020) describes the simulations that drove the response to COVID-19: individual-based models of whole countries, with households, schools and workplaces, used to compare interventions. They add what the equations above average out: contact structure, heterogeneity, targeted interventions, and the randomness of the early phase.

Such a model carries three uncertainties: parameters (R_0, the infectious period), stochasticity (Proposition 5.3) and structure (the model itself). Hence ensembles of runs with reported intervals, sensitivity analyses, calibration on data, and scenarios compared with common random numbers, so that a difference between runs measures the intervention and not the noise.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • In Schelling’s model a tolerant threshold of 30% produces 74% of same-type neighbours; with logit moves the dynamics samples a Gibbs measure of the number of same-type contacts.
  • A stochastic SIR epidemic from one case dies out early with probability 1/R_0; otherwise its final size solves z = 1 - e^{-R_0 z} and overshoots the herd-immunity threshold.
  • On networks the threshold involves the mean excess degree; ABMs for policy must report parametric, stochastic and structural uncertainty.

6. Agent-based models: markets, socio-technical systems and avalanches

When agents copy each other or depend on each other, small causes can have large collective effects. This chapter studies herding in markets and the propagation of delays in socio-technical systems, and finds in both a critical point.

Herding: Kirman’s model

Kirman (1993) observed ants that, offered two identical food sources, crowd at one of them and switch from time to time. In his model, N agents choose between two options. An agent switches spontaneously at rate \varepsilon, and each agent of the other option recruits it at rate h/(N - 1).

With k agents on option 1, the count is a birth–death chain with rates k \to k + 1 : \ (N - k)\Big(\varepsilon + \frac{h k}{N - 1}\Big), \qquad k \to k - 1 : \ k \Big(\varepsilon + \frac{h (N - k)}{N - 1}\Big).

Theorem 6.1 — Stationary law of Kirman’s model Let a = \varepsilon (N - 1)/h. The stationary law of k is the beta-binomial law with parameters (N, a, a): \pi(k) = \binom{N}{k} \frac{B(k + a,\, N - k + a)}{B(a, a)} . As N \to \infty with a fixed, k/N converges in law to \mathrm{Beta}(a, a).

This limit is bimodal, with the crowd near one option, if a < 1, and concentrated around 1/2 if a > 1.

Proof. For a birth–death chain, detailed balance says that \pi(k + 1)/\pi(k) is the up rate at k divided by the down rate at k + 1. Dividing both rates by h/(N-1), \frac{\pi(k+1)}{\pi(k)} = \frac{(N - k)(a + k)}{(k + 1)(a + N - k - 1)} . The beta-binomial weights satisfy the same recursion, by B(x + 1, y) = B(x, y)\, x/(x + y). The limit follows from Stirling’s formula, or from the representation of the beta-binomial law as a binomial with a \mathrm{Beta}(a, a) success probability. ∎

Herding wins, a < 1, when spontaneous switching is rarer than recruitment by one given agent. Read as a market, the majority persists for long periods and then flips, in bursts.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import betabinom

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
N, h = 100, 1.0
for eps in [0.004, 0.04]:
    a = eps * (N - 1) / h                      # the parameter of Theorem 6.1
    k, time_in = N // 2, np.zeros(N + 1)
    for _ in range(400000):                     # jump chain + exponential holding times
        up = (N - k) * (eps + h * k / (N - 1))
        down = k * (eps + h * (N - k) / (N - 1))
        time_in[k] += rng.exponential(1 / (up + down))
        k += 1 if rng.random() < up / (up + down) else -1
    emp = time_in / time_in.sum()
    x = np.arange(N + 1)
    exact = betabinom.pmf(x, N, a, a)
    tv = 0.5 * np.abs(emp - exact).sum()
    print(f"a = {a:.2f}: P(k <= 10 or k >= 90) simulated {emp[(x <= 10) | (x >= 90)].sum():.3f}, "
          f"exact {exact[(x <= 10) | (x >= 90)].sum():.3f}; total variation {tv:.3f}")

For a = 0.40 the crowd spends half of the time beyond 90% on one side (0.520 simulated, 0.496 exact); for a = 3.96, about 1% of the time (0.009 and 0.011).

Herding and heavy-tailed returns

Proposition 6.2 — Cont–Bouchaud clusters Link each pair of N traders independently with probability c/N, and let each connected cluster buy, sell or stay out as a block. As N \to \infty, the cluster of a given trader has the Borel(c) size law of Theorem 4.3. At c = 1, the fraction of clusters of size s decays as

n_s \propto s^{-5/2}, \qquad \text{so the cluster sizes have tail } \mathbb{P}(|C| > x) \propto x^{-3/2} .

Proof. Explore the cluster of a trader: each newly found trader has a \mathrm{Binomial}(N', c/N) number of new partners, with N' \approx N traders not yet found, which converges to Poisson(c). The exploration is the Galton–Watson process of Theorem 4.3.

A cluster of size s contains s traders, so the number of such clusters is N/s times the probability that a given trader’s cluster has size s, of order s^{-3/2}. ∎

The aggregate demand is a sum of cluster sizes with random signs, a sum of heavy-tailed variables. By ch. 9, its large values come from the largest cluster acting alone: one coordinated block moves the price. Empirical returns have lighter tails than this critical model, so the model shows a mechanism rather than the numbers.

Delays in socio-technical systems

In supply chains, transport networks and production schedules, agents need inputs from others and keep buffers of spare time. Delays propagate along the dependencies, and buffers absorb part of them. Smaller buffers are more efficient, until delays accumulate.

Moran and coauthors (2024) call the resulting phase transition timeliness criticality, with avalanches of delays near it. A stylised mean-field model in the same spirit: agent i waits for K inputs drawn at random at each step, absorbs a buffer B, and adds its own random delay \varepsilon_i(t) \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1): \tau_i(t + 1) = \max\Big(0,\ \max_{j \in J_i(t)} \tau_j(t) - B\Big) + \varepsilon_i(t).

Theorem 6.3 — Critical buffer As N \to \infty, the delays grow linearly at speed v_K - B if B < v_K, and stay bounded in law if B > v_K, where

B_c = v_K = \inf_{0 < \theta < 1} \frac{\ln K - \ln(1 - \theta)}{\theta} .

For K = 2, B_c = 2.678, although the mean of the largest of two inputs is only 1.5.

Proof. Sketch. When N \to \infty, the inputs of different agents are distinct, so the backward dependencies of an agent form a K-ary tree. Unrolling the recursion, up to the last noise, \tau_i(t) = \max_{s \leq t} (M_s - B s), where M_s is the largest sum of noises along the K^s paths of length s: the maximum of a branching random walk.

Upper bound. With \Lambda(\theta) = -\ln(1 - \theta), the log-moment generating function of the noise, Markov’s inequality gives \mathbb{E}\, \#\{\text{paths with sum} \geq v s\} \leq \exp\big(s\, (\ln K + \Lambda(\theta) - \theta v)\big). This decays exponentially for every v > v_K, so M_s \leq v s eventually.

The matching lower bound, M_s / s \to v_K almost surely, is the theorem of Hammersley (1974), Kingman (1975) and Biggins (1977). Hence \sup_s (M_s - B s) is finite if B > v_K and grows like (v_K - B)\, t otherwise. ∎

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
K = 2
# speed of the maximum of a branching random walk with K children and Exp(1) steps
speed = minimize_scalar(lambda th: (np.log(K) - np.log(1 - th)) / th,
                        bounds=(1e-3, 1 - 1e-3), method="bounded").fun
print(f"critical buffer B_c = v_K = {speed:.3f}")

N, T = 5000, 600
for B in [2.0, 2.4, 2.6, 2.8, 3.2]:
    tau = np.zeros(N)
    for t in range(T):
        inputs = rng.integers(N, size=(N, K))                 # fresh random inputs (mean field)
        tau = np.maximum(tau[inputs].max(axis=1) - B, 0) + rng.exponential(1.0, N)
        if t == T // 2:
            tau_half = tau.mean()
    print(f"B = {B:.1f}: growth rate of the mean delay {(tau.mean() - tau_half) / (T / 2):.3f}, "
          f"BRW prediction {max(speed - B, 0):.3f}")

With 5000 agents, the delays grow at rates 0.471 and 0.113 for buffers 2.0 and 2.4, and not at all from 2.6: a finite population cannot explore all K^s paths.

The speed at B = 2 is 2.38, 2.48 and 2.58 for N = 500, 5000 and 50000. It approaches v_K slowly, as the (\ln N)^{-2} correction of Brunet and Derrida (1997) predicts.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Kirman’s herding model has a beta-binomial stationary law, bimodal when spontaneous switching is rarer than recruitment.
  • In the Cont–Bouchaud model, clusters of traders at the percolation threshold have Borel sizes, which makes aggregate demand heavy-tailed.
  • Delays propagating through dependencies with buffers have a critical buffer, the speed of a branching random walk; finite systems approach it slowly.

7. Markov chain aggregation

An ABM is a Markov chain on S^N, but what we observe and want to predict is a macro-variable: a count, a density, a share. This chapter asks when the macro-variable is itself a Markov chain, and uses the answer to compute exact consensus times (Banisch, 2015).

Lumpability

Definition 7.1 — Lumpability Let P be a transition matrix on a finite set \Sigma, and \{A_1, \dots, A_m\} a partition with projection \varphi(x) = k for x \in A_k.

The chain is lumpable with respect to the partition if, for all blocks k and l, the probability P(x, A_l) takes the same value \hat P(k, l) for every x \in A_k.

Theorem 7.2 — Kemeny and Snell (1960) If P is lumpable, then for every initial law the macro-process \varphi(Z_t) is a Markov chain with transition matrix \hat P. Conversely, if \varphi(Z_t) is Markov with the same transition matrix for every initial law, P is lumpable.

Proof. Given the micro-history, the next macro-state is l with probability P(Z_t, A_l) = \hat P(\varphi(Z_t), l), a function of \varphi(Z_t) only. Conditioning on the coarser macro-history (tower property) keeps this value, so \varphi(Z_t) is Markov with matrix \hat P.

Conversely, start from x \in A_k: the law of \varphi(Z_1) is P(x, A_l), which must equal \hat P(k, l) for every such x. ∎

Proposition 7.3 — Symmetries give lumpable partitions Let G be a group of bijections of \Sigma with P(gx, gy) = P(x, y) for all g, x, y. Then the partition of \Sigma into G-orbits is lumpable.

Proof. Let A be an orbit, so g^{-1} A = A. For g \in G, substituting y = g y', P(gx, A) = \sum_{y' \in g^{-1} A} P(gx, gy') = P(x, g^{-1}A) = P(x, A). Every point of the orbit of x is of the form gx. ∎

On the complete graph, an ABM with exchangeable agents is invariant under all permutations of the agents, and its orbits are the vectors of counts. On a general network, the automorphism group of the graph gives a finer lumpable partition.

Exact consensus times

Example 7.4 — The voter model on the complete graph At each step a uniform agent copies a uniform other agent. The number k of agents with opinion 1 is a lumped chain that moves up or down by one with the same probability p_k = \frac{k (N - k)}{N (N - 1)} .

The absorption probability at N is k/N, as in Proposition 2.4. The expected absorption time \tau_k solves p_k (\tau_{k+1} - 2 \tau_k + \tau_{k-1}) = -1 with \tau_0 = \tau_N = 0.

With \tau_k = T(k/N), the second difference is about T''(x)/N^2, so T''(x) = -N(N-1)/(x(1-x)). With H(x) = -x \ln x - (1 - x) \ln(1 - x), this gives \tau_k \approx N (N - 1)\, H(k/N). From an even split, consensus takes about N \ln 2 updates per agent.

The code solves the linear system exactly for N = 100 and checks it against the agent-level model, without any lumping:

import numpy as np

# Voter model on the complete graph K_N, one random agent updated per step.
# Lumped chain on k = number of agents with opinion 1: p_k = k (N - k) / (N (N - 1)) up and down.
N = 100
k = np.arange(1, N)
p = k * (N - k) / (N * (N - 1))
# Expected absorption time tau_k: p_k (tau_{k+1} - 2 tau_k + tau_{k-1}) = -1, tau_0 = tau_N = 0.
A = np.diag(-2 * p) + np.diag(p[1:], -1) + np.diag(p[:-1], 1)
tau = np.linalg.solve(A, -np.ones(N - 1))
x = k / N
H = -(x * np.log(x) + (1 - x) * np.log(1 - x))       # entropy in nats
for kk in [10, 50]:
    print(f"k = {kk}: exact tau = {tau[kk - 1]:8.0f} steps, N(N-1) H(k/N) = {N * (N - 1) * H[kk - 1]:8.0f}")

# Monte Carlo check from k = 50 on the full (unlumped) agent-level model
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
times = []
for _ in range(300):
    eta, ones, t = [1] * 50 + [0] * 50, 50, 0
    I = rng.integers(N, size=200000)
    J = ((I + 1 + rng.integers(N - 1, size=200000)) % N).tolist()  # agent i copies a uniform other j
    I = I.tolist()
    while 0 < ones < N:
        i, j = I[t], J[t]
        ones += eta[j] - eta[i]; eta[i] = eta[j]; t += 1
    times.append(t)
print(f"agent-level simulation from k = 50: mean {np.mean(times):.0f} steps (s.e. {np.std(times) / np.sqrt(300):.0f})")

The exact times are 3170 and 6813 steps, the approximation gives 3218 and 6862, and the agent-level simulation gives 7068 \pm 308.

When lumping fails

On the path with three sites, the configurations (1, 0, 0) and (0, 1, 0) both have one opinion-1 voter. From the first, k increases only when site 2 copies site 1, at rate 1/2. From the second, it increases when site 1 or site 3 copies site 2, at total rate 2. The count is not lumpable.

Two remedies exist. The automorphisms of the graph (here the reflection) give a finer partition that is lumpable. Approximate aggregation, such as mean-field or pair approximations, keeps a small macro-chain and accepts a quantified error. Either way, the micro-model provides the ground truth against which the reduced model is tested.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • A partition is lumpable when all micro-states of a block have the same probability of entering each block; the macro-process is then Markov (Kemeny–Snell).
  • Symmetries of the model, permutations of exchangeable agents or automorphisms of the network, give lumpable partitions.
  • On the complete graph the voter model lumps to a birth–death chain, with consensus time N(N-1) H(k/N) steps.

8. Inference in interacting systems: structure, laws and causes

The previous chapters went from rules to patterns. Inference goes back: from observed configurations or trajectories to who interacts with whom, how strongly, and with what causal effect. ABMs play a double role here, as the models to fit and as test benches with a known truth.

Inferring interaction structure

Write the Ising measure with site-dependent couplings, the inverse temperature absorbed: \mu(\sigma) \propto \exp\Big( \sum_{x < y} J_{xy}\, \sigma_x \sigma_y + \sum_x h_x \sigma_x \Big). The interaction graph is the set of pairs with J_{xy} \neq 0.

Proposition 8.1 — Each spin is a logistic regression on the others For every site x, \mathbb{P}(\sigma_x = +1 \mid \sigma_{-x}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\big(-2 (h_x + \sum_{y} J_{xy} \sigma_y)\big)} . The coefficients are 2 J_{xy}; they are non-zero exactly on the neighbours of x.

Proof. The two values of \sigma_x have conditional probabilities proportional to e^{\pm (h_x + \sum_y J_{xy} \sigma_y)}; divide one by their sum. ∎

Neighbourhood selection fits, for each site, an \ell_1-penalised logistic regression of \sigma_x on the other spins, and keeps the edge \{x, y\} when both coefficients are non-zero. This pseudo-likelihood method never computes the partition function.

Theorem 8.2 — Graph recovery (Ravikumar, Wainwright and Lafferty, 2010) Consider p spins on a graph of maximum degree d. Assume incoherence and dependency conditions on the Fisher information, and take a penalty of order \sqrt{\log p / n}. Then n of order d^3 \log p samples suffice for neighbourhood selection to recover the graph with probability tending to 1.

Correlation is not interaction. On a chain with couplings J, spins at distance 2 have correlation \tanh^2 J without any coupling. With 16 spins on a cycle with 4 chords and J = 0.5, from 3000 samples, neighbourhood selection finds the 20 edges and no false ones, while the 20 most correlated pairs contain only 18 edges.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
p, n, beta = 16, 3000, 0.5
# true interaction graph: a cycle plus 4 random chords, couplings beta on each edge
E = {(i, (i + 1) % p) for i in range(p)}
while len(E) < p + 4:
    i, j = sorted(rng.choice(p, 2, replace=False)); E.add((i, j))
J = np.zeros((p, p))
for i, j in E:
    J[i, j] = J[j, i] = beta

# n independent samples by Gibbs sampling (heat-bath), n chains run in parallel
s = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(n, p))
for sweep in range(300):
    for i in range(p):
        field = s @ J[i]
        s[:, i] = np.where(rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * field)), 1, -1)

# neighbourhood selection: l1-penalised logistic regression of each spin on the others
# (scikit-learn >= 1.8 syntax; older versions write penalty="l1")
J_hat = np.zeros((p, p))
for i in range(p):
    others = np.delete(np.arange(p), i)
    clf = LogisticRegression(l1_ratio=1.0, C=0.05, solver="liblinear").fit(s[:, others], s[:, i])
    J_hat[i, others] = clf.coef_[0] / 2           # logit = 2 * sum_j J_ij s_j
found = {(i, j) for i in range(p) for j in range(i + 1, p) if min(abs(J_hat[i, j]), abs(J_hat[j, i])) > 0.1}
true = {tuple(sorted(e)) for e in E}
C = np.corrcoef(s.T)
top = sorted(((abs(C[i, j]), (i, j)) for i in range(p) for j in range(i + 1, p)), reverse=True)[:len(true)]
print(f"true edges {len(true)}, logistic-l1 finds {len(found & true)} of them and {len(found - true)} false ones")
print(f"thresholding correlations (top {len(true)} pairs) finds {len({e for _, e in top} & true)} true edges")
print(f"mean estimated coupling on true edges: {np.mean([J_hat[i, j] for i, j in true]):.2f} (true {beta})")

The estimated couplings average 0.45 instead of 0.5: the \ell_1 penalty shrinks them. From observed trajectories, the likelihood of a spin system is a product of flip rates and waiting times, and its maximisation is again one regression per site.

Learning interaction laws

Definition 8.3 — First-order system of agents N agents at positions x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d interact through a kernel \phi of the distance: \dot x_i = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \phi\big(\|x_j - x_i\|\big)\, (x_j - x_i). Opinion dynamics with bounded confidence (Hegselmann and Krause, 2002) take \phi = \mathbb{1}_{[0, R]}.

Lu, Zhong, Tang and Maggioni (2019) learn \phi from M trajectories observed at L times.

The right-hand side f_\phi(x) is linear in \phi. Minimising over a finite-dimensional space \mathcal{H}_n, such as piecewise constant functions on n bins, is therefore a least-squares problem: \hat\phi = \arg\min_{\varphi \in \mathcal{H}_n} \sum_{m, l, i} \big\| \dot x^{(m)}_i(t_l) - f_\varphi\big(x^{(m)}(t_l)\big)_i \big\|^2 . The error is measured on r \mapsto r \phi(r) in L^2(\rho_T), where \rho_T is the law of pairwise distances along the trajectories.

Theorem 8.4 — Dimension-free learning rate (Lu, Zhong, Tang and Maggioni, 2019) Assume a coercivity condition: \mathbb{E}\, \|f_\varphi(x)\|^2 / N bounds \|\varphi(\cdot)\, \cdot\|^2_{L^2(\rho_T)} from below, up to a constant, so that \varphi is identifiable. For an s-Hölder kernel and n chosen as a power of M / \ln M, the estimator converges at the rate

\Big( \frac{\ln M}{M} \Big)^{s/(2s + 1)},

the optimal rate of one-dimensional nonparametric regression, whatever the dimension dN of the state space.

The pairwise structure is what makes the problem one-dimensional: all pairs of all trajectories inform the same function of one variable.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
N, d, dt, L = 10, 2, 0.05, 20                   # agents, dimension, time step, observations per trajectory
phi = lambda r: np.where(r < 1.0, 1.0, 0.1)     # true interaction kernel (opinion-dynamics type)
edges = np.linspace(0, 3, 16)                   # hypothesis space: piecewise constant on 15 bins

def velocity(x, kernel):
    diff = x[None, :, :] - x[:, None, :]        # diff[i, j] = x_j - x_i
    r = np.linalg.norm(diff, axis=2)
    return (kernel(r)[:, :, None] * diff).sum(1) / N

def learn(M, noise=0.05):
    rows, targets, dists = [], [], []
    for _ in range(M):
        x = rng.uniform(0, 2, size=(N, d))
        for _ in range(L):
            v = velocity(x, phi) + noise * rng.standard_normal((N, d))   # noisy observed velocities
            diff = x[None, :, :] - x[:, None, :]
            r = np.linalg.norm(diff, axis=2)
            b = np.clip(np.digitize(r, edges) - 1, 0, len(edges) - 2)
            for i in range(N):
                feat = np.zeros((d, len(edges) - 1))
                for j in range(N):
                    if j != i:
                        feat[:, b[i, j]] += diff[i, j] / N
                        dists.append(r[i, j])
                rows.append(feat); targets.append(v[i])
            x = x + dt * velocity(x, phi)                                 # Euler step of the true system
    A, y = np.concatenate(rows), np.concatenate(targets)
    c = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]
    dists = np.array(dists)
    b = np.clip(np.digitize(dists, edges) - 1, 0, len(edges) - 2)
    err = np.sqrt(np.mean(((c[b] - phi(dists)) * dists) ** 2) / np.mean((phi(dists) * dists) ** 2))
    return c, err

for M in [5, 50]:
    c, err = learn(M)
    print(f"M = {M:3d} trajectories: relative L2(rho) error {err:.3f}; "
          f"phi_hat on [0.4, 0.6) = {c[2]:.2f}, on [1.4, 1.6) = {c[7]:.2f}")

The relative error is 0.033 with 5 trajectories and 0.011 with 50; the estimated kernel takes the values 1.02 and 0.12, then 1.01 and 0.10, against 1 and 0.1.

Causal inference with agent-based models

Definition 8.5 — Structural causal model and intervention A structural causal model defines each variable by an assignment V_j := f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j, U_j) from its parents and an independent noise. The intervention \mathrm{do}(X = x) replaces the assignment of X by the constant x. The causal effect of a binary X on Y is \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathrm{do}(X = 1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathrm{do}(X = 0)] .

The code of an ABM is a structural causal model of its own variables, and running it with a modified rule is an intervention. Manzo (2022) develops this mechanism-based view of causality in the social sciences.

Proposition 8.6 — Back-door adjustment If a variable Z is not a descendant of X and blocks every path between X and Y that starts with an arrow into X, then \mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid \mathrm{do}(X = x)) = \sum_z \mathbb{P}(Y = y \mid X = x, Z = z)\, \mathbb{P}(Z = z) .

Proof. Sketch, for the graph Z \to X, Z \to Y, X \to Y. The intervention removes the factor \mathbb{P}(x \mid z) from the factorisation of the joint law (truncated factorisation): \mathbb{P}(z, y \mid \mathrm{do}(x)) = \mathbb{P}(z)\, \mathbb{P}(y \mid x, z). Sum over z. The general case is in Pearl (2009, ch. 3). ∎

In the model below, cautious agents both wear masks more often and have fewer contacts. The naive comparison of infection rates doubles the effect of masks; adjusting for caution, or randomising masks in silico, gives the true effect.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n, p = 200_000, 0.02                         # agents, transmission probability per risky contact

def run(mask_rule):
    caution = rng.random(n) < 0.5            # confounder: cautious agents...
    mask = mask_rule(caution)
    contacts = rng.poisson(np.where(caution, 5, 15))   # ...also have fewer contacts
    p_inf = 1 - (1 - p * np.where(mask, 0.5, 1.0)) ** contacts   # a mask halves transmission
    return caution, mask, rng.random(n) < p_inf

# observational world: cautious agents wear masks more often
C, M, Y = run(lambda c: rng.random(n) < np.where(c, 0.8, 0.2))
naive = Y[M].mean() - Y[~M].mean()
adjusted = sum((Y[M & (C == c)].mean() - Y[~M & (C == c)].mean()) * np.mean(C == c) for c in (0, 1))
# in silico experiment: the same model, masks assigned by a coin flip
C2, M2, Y2 = run(lambda c: rng.random(n) < 0.5)
rct = Y2[M2].mean() - Y2[~M2].mean()
print(f"naive difference {naive:+.4f} | back-door adjusted {adjusted:+.4f} | randomised in silico {rct:+.4f}")

The output is -0.159, -0.082 and -0.080.

Interacting systems add a difficulty: interference. My neighbour’s vaccination protects me, so the outcome of an agent depends on the treatments of others. Hudgens and Halloran (2008) separate the direct effect (an agent’s own treatment, at fixed coverage), the indirect effect (the coverage of others, on untreated agents) and their sum, the total effect. An epidemic ABM is a natural test bench for such designs (Exercise 17).

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • In an Ising model each spin is a logistic regression on the others; \ell_1-penalised neighbourhood selection recovers the graph from about d^3 \log p samples, while correlations confuse direct and indirect links.
  • An interaction kernel is learned by least squares over pairwise distances, at a rate that does not depend on the number of agents.
  • An ABM is a structural causal model: in silico randomisation gives true effects, against which adjustment and designs under interference can be tested.

9. Rare events: conspiracy versus catastrophe

A sum of many random contributions, a total loss, a total delay or an aggregate demand, takes an unusually large value. Either all contributions are a little larger than usual, or one of them is huge. Nair, Wierman and Zwart (2022) call these the conspiracy and the catastrophe principles; the tail of the contributions decides which one holds.

Light tails: conspiracy

Theorem 9.1 — Chernoff bound and Cramér’s theorem Let X_1, X_2, \dots be i.i.d. with mean \mu and \Lambda(\theta) = \ln \mathbb{E}\, e^{\theta X} finite near 0, and S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n. For a > \mu, \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq n a) \leq e^{-n I(a)}, \qquad I(a) = \sup_{\theta \geq 0} \big(\theta a - \Lambda(\theta)\big), and Cramér’s theorem adds that \frac1n \ln \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq n a) \to -I(a).

Proof. For \theta \geq 0, Markov’s inequality applied to e^{\theta S_n} gives \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq na) \leq e^{-\theta n a}\, \mathbb{E}\, e^{\theta S_n} = e^{-n(\theta a - \Lambda(\theta))} . Optimise over \theta. For the lower bound, the tilted law e^{\theta x - \Lambda(\theta)} \mathbb{P}(dx) with \Lambda'(\theta) = a has mean a, so the event is typical under it; changing back to \mathbb{P} costs e^{-n I(a) + o(n)} (Dembo and Zeitouni, 1998). ∎

Conditioned on the rare event, each X_i is distributed approximately as the tilted law of mean a: all summands conspire (the Gibbs conditioning principle). For exponential variables this holds exactly.

Proposition 9.2 — Conspiracy of exponentials Let the X_i be i.i.d. \mathrm{Exp}(1). Given S_n = s, the vector X/s is uniform on the simplex, whatever s.

With H_n = 1 + \frac12 + \dots + \frac1n, for every level x,

\mathbb{E}\Big[ \frac{\max_i X_i}{S_n} \;\Big|\; S_n > x \Big] = \frac{H_n}{n} .

Proof. The density e^{-(x_1 + \dots + x_n)} is constant on each simplex \{x_1 + \dots + x_n = s\}, so X/S_n is uniform and independent of S_n.

By Rényi’s representation, the largest of n exponentials has mean H_n. Independence gives \mathbb{E} \max_i X_i = \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\max_i X_i}{S_n}\Big]\, \mathbb{E}\, S_n, \qquad \mathbb{E}\, S_n = n. ∎

Heavy tails: catastrophe

Definition 9.3 — Regular variation A random variable is regularly varying with index \alpha > 0 if \bar F(x) = \mathbb{P}(X > x) = x^{-\alpha}\, \ell(x), \qquad \frac{\ell(tx)}{\ell(x)} \to 1 \ \text{ for every } t > 0 . The Pareto law \bar F(x) = x^{-\alpha}, x \geq 1, is the basic example.

Theorem 9.4 — Catastrophe principle Let X_1, \dots, X_n \geq 0 be i.i.d. and regularly varying. As x \to \infty,

\mathbb{P}(S_n > x) \sim n\, \mathbb{P}(X > x) \sim \mathbb{P}\big(\max_i X_i > x\big),

and, given S_n > x, the largest summand exceeds (1 - \delta)x with probability tending to 1, for every \delta > 0.

Proof. For n = 2. Lower bound: the event contains \{X_1 > x\} \cup \{X_2 > x\}, of probability 2 \bar F(x) - \bar F(x)^2.

Upper bound: for \delta \in (0, \frac12), if X_1 + X_2 > x then either one summand exceeds (1 - \delta)x, or both exceed \delta x. Hence \frac{\mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 > x)}{\bar F(x)} \leq \frac{2 \bar F((1 - \delta)x) + \bar F(\delta x)^2}{\bar F(x)} \to 2 (1 - \delta)^{-\alpha}, because \bar F(\delta x)^2 / \bar F(x) is equivalent to \delta^{-2\alpha} \bar F(x), which tends to 0. Let \delta \to 0.

The same ratio shows that the scenario “both summands exceed \delta x” has vanishing conditional probability. The case of n summands follows by induction. ∎

The simulation compares the two worlds for n = 10, at the level that S_n exceeds with probability 2 \times 10^{-4}:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
n, reps = 10, 500_000
laws = {
    "exponential, mean 1": (rng.exponential(1.0, (reps, n)), lambda x: np.exp(-x)),
    "Pareto alpha = 1.5": (rng.pareto(1.5, (reps, n)) + 1, lambda x: x ** -1.5),  # P(X > x) = x^-1.5
}
for name, (X, tail) in laws.items():
    S, Mx = X.sum(1), X.max(1)
    x = np.quantile(S, 1 - 2e-4)                    # a level exceeded with probability 2e-4
    big = S > x
    print(f"{name:20s}: x = {x:6.1f}, P(S>x)/(n P(X>x)) = {np.mean(big) / (n * tail(x)):10.3g}, "
          f"E[max/S | S > x] = {np.mean(Mx[big] / S[big]):.2f}")

For exponentials (x = 24.8), the single-big-jump estimate n \mathbb{P}(X > x) is wrong by a factor of 10^6, and the largest summand carries 28% of the sum (H_{10}/10 = 0.293). For the Pareto law (x = 1397) the ratio is 1.04 and the largest summand carries 99% of the sum.

Where heavy tails come from

Proposition 9.5 — Exponential growth stopped at a random time If a quantity grows as e^{\mu t} and is observed at an independent time T \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\nu), then X = e^{\mu T} is Pareto with index \alpha = \nu / \mu.

Proof. For x \geq 1, \mathbb{P}(X > x) = \mathbb{P}(T > \ln x / \mu) = x^{-\nu/\mu}. ∎

The course has met the other mechanisms: critical branching and percolation clusters (ch. 4 and ch. 6), multiplicative recursions (ch. 11) and extremes (ch. 10). Interactions produce heavy tails by coordinating units, as in herding, or by multiplying fluctuations.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Light tails: \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq na) \approx e^{-n I(a)}, and on the rare event all summands are shifted together (conspiracy).
  • Heavy tails: \mathbb{P}(S_n > x) \sim n\, \mathbb{P}(X > x), and the rare event is one huge summand (catastrophe).
  • Random stopping of exponential growth, critical branching and multiplicative noise all produce power laws.

10. Higher-dimensional extrema: convex hulls and combinatorial lemmas

The maximum of a random walk is a one-dimensional extreme. In the plane, the natural extreme object is the convex hull of the trajectory, whose extent in every direction is a maximum. A combinatorial lemma computes its mean perimeter exactly, whatever the law of the steps (Majumdar, Comtet and Randon-Furling, 2010).

A combinatorial lemma and Kac’s formula

Let x_1, \dots, x_n be real numbers with partial sums S_k (S_0 = 0) and running maxima M_k = \max(0, S_1, \dots, S_k).

The j-th cyclic shift is the sequence x_{j+1}, \dots, x_n, x_1, \dots, x_j. Write M^{(j)}_{n-1} for its running maximum after n - 1 steps.

Lemma 10.1 — A cyclic identity for maxima For all real x_1, \dots, x_n, \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \big( S_n - M^{(j)}_{n-1} \big)^+ = S_n^+ .

Proof. If S_n \leq 0, every term vanishes because M^{(j)}_{n-1} \geq 0. Let S_n > 0, and extend the sequence periodically in both directions, so that S_{m + n} = S_m + S_n.

The shifted walk has partial sums S_{j+i} - S_j, so the j-th term equals \Big( S_{j+n} - \max_{j \leq m < j+n} S_m \Big)^+ . Since S_{m-n} < S_m, the maximum over this window is the maximum R_{j+n-1} of all S_m with m < j + n.

The term is therefore R_{j+n} - R_{j+n-1}, the increase of the running maximum. The sum telescopes to R_{2n-1} - R_{n-1} = S_n. ∎

Theorem 10.2 — Kac’s formula (Spitzer, 1956) For a random walk whose increments are exchangeable and integrable,

\mathbb{E}\, \max(0, S_1, \dots, S_n) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\mathbb{E}\, S_k^+}{k} .

Proof. The running maximum increases only when the walk exceeds it: M_n - M_{n-1} = (S_n - M_{n-1})^+. Exchangeability gives all cyclic shifts the same law, so by Lemma 10.1 \mathbb{E}\, (S_n - M_{n-1})^+ = \frac1n \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}\, \big(S_n - M^{(j)}_{n-1}\big)^+ = \frac{\mathbb{E}\, S_n^+}{n} . Sum over n. ∎

Convex hulls of planar random walks

Theorem 10.3 — Spitzer and Widom (1961) Let S_0 = 0, S_1, \dots, S_n be a planar random walk with exchangeable integrable increments, and L_n the perimeter of the convex hull of \{S_0, \dots, S_n\}. Then

\mathbb{E}\, L_n = 2 \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\mathbb{E}\, \|S_k\|}{k} .

Proof. By Cauchy’s formula, the perimeter of a compact convex set C in the plane is \int_0^{2\pi} h_C(\theta)\, d\theta, where h_C(\theta) = \max_{y \in C} \langle y, u_\theta \rangle is its support function and u_\theta the unit vector at angle \theta.

For the hull, h(\theta) = \max_{k \leq n} \langle S_k, u_\theta \rangle is the maximum of a one-dimensional walk with exchangeable increments. Theorem 10.2 gives \mathbb{E}\, h(\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{\mathbb{E}\, \langle S_k, u_\theta\rangle^+}{k} . Integrate over \theta (Fubini) and use \int_0^{2\pi} (r \cos(\theta - \varphi))^+\, d\theta = 2r. ∎

For standard Gaussian steps, \mathbb{E}\, \|S_k\| = \sqrt{\pi k / 2} and \mathbb{E}\, L_n \approx \sqrt{8 \pi n}. For planar Brownian motion on [0, T], each h(\theta) is the maximum of a one-dimensional Brownian motion, of mean \sqrt{2T/\pi}, so the mean perimeter is \sqrt{8 \pi T}.

Theorem 10.4 — Mean area (Barndorff-Nielsen and Baxter, 1963) With S' an independent copy of the walk, the area A_n of the hull satisfies \mathbb{E}\, A_n = \frac12 \sum_{i + j \leq n} \frac{\mathbb{E}\, |\det(S_i, S'_j)|}{i\, j} . For standard Gaussian steps \mathbb{E}\, |\det(S_i, S'_j)| = \sqrt{ij}, and the sum tends to the Brownian value \pi n / 2.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import ConvexHull

rng = np.random.default_rng(13)
n, reps = 1000, 2000
per, area = [], []
for _ in range(reps):
    S = np.vstack([[0, 0], np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal((n, 2)), axis=0)])   # planar walk from 0
    h = ConvexHull(S)
    per.append(h.area); area.append(h.volume)    # in 2D, scipy's "area" is the perimeter
k = np.arange(1, n + 1)
spitzer_widom = 2 * np.sum(np.sqrt(np.pi * k / 2) / k)    # 2 sum E|S_k| / k, E|S_k| = sqrt(pi k / 2)
print(f"mean perimeter: simulated {np.mean(per):.1f} +- {np.std(per) / np.sqrt(reps):.1f}, "
      f"Spitzer-Widom {spitzer_widom:.1f}, Brownian sqrt(8 pi n) = {np.sqrt(8 * np.pi * n):.1f}")
print(f"mean area     : simulated {np.mean(area):.0f} +- {np.std(area) / np.sqrt(reps):.0f}, "
      f"Brownian pi n / 2 = {np.pi * n / 2:.0f}")
bnb = 0.5 * sum(np.sum(1 / np.sqrt(i * np.arange(1, n - i + 1))) for i in range(1, n))
print(f"discrete mean-area formula (1/2) sum_(i+j<=n) 1/sqrt(ij) = {bnb:.0f}")

For n = 1000, the simulated mean perimeter is 155.2 \pm 0.7 against 154.9 from Theorem 10.3, and the mean area is 1486 \pm 12 against 1480 from Theorem 10.4. The Brownian values, 158.5 and 1571, are reached only slowly.

Lévy processes

Proposition 10.5 — Hulls of Lévy processes Let X be a planar Lévy process with \mathbb{E}\, \|X_t\| < \infty. Then the mean perimeter of its convex hull on [0, T] is \mathbb{E}\, L(T) = 2 \int_0^T \frac{\mathbb{E}\, \|X_t\|}{t}\, dt . For an isotropic \alpha-stable process with 1 < \alpha \leq 2, \mathbb{E}\, \|X_t\| = c_\alpha t^{1/\alpha} and \mathbb{E}\, L(T) = 2 \alpha\, c_\alpha T^{1/\alpha}.

Sketch. Apply Theorem 10.3 to the skeleton (X_{kT/n})_{k \leq n}, whose increments are i.i.d. The hulls of the skeletons increase to the closed hull of the path, and the sums converge to the integral.

The hull of a Lévy flight grows like T^{1/\alpha}, faster than the diffusive \sqrt T: the rare long jumps dominate the extent, the catastrophe principle in two dimensions. The same tools give the mean home range of n independent foragers, the hull of n Brownian paths (Randon-Furling, Majumdar and Comtet, 2009).

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • A cyclic identity turns the mean maximum of a walk into \sum_k \mathbb{E} S_k^+ / k (Kac’s formula), for any exchangeable increments.
  • Cauchy’s formula writes the perimeter of a hull as the integral of its support function, a maximum; hence \mathbb{E} L_n = 2 \sum_k \mathbb{E}\|S_k\|/k.
  • The mean area has a similar double sum; Lévy flights have hulls of size T^{1/\alpha}.

11. Heavy tails in stochastic gradient descent

Stochastic gradient descent is itself a stochastic process in which the iterate interacts with random data, multiplicatively. Even with Gaussian data, its iterates can have power-law tails (Gurbuzbalaban, Simsekli and Zhu, 2021). This chapter explains why with random linear recursions.

Random linear recursions

Consider X_{k+1} = A_k X_k + B_k with i.i.d. pairs (A_k, B_k) of real numbers. If \mathbb{E} \ln |A| < 0, the products A_1 \cdots A_k decay exponentially and the series X = \sum_{k \geq 0} A_1 \cdots A_k\, B_{k+1} converges almost surely; its law is the unique stationary law, the solution of X \overset{d}{=} A X + B with (A, B) independent of X.

Theorem 11.1 — Kesten (1973), Goldie (1991) Assume that \mathbb{E} \ln|A| < 0 and that some \alpha > 0 satisfies \mathbb{E}\, |A|^\alpha = 1, \qquad \mathbb{E}\, |A|^\alpha \ln^+ |A| < \infty, \qquad \mathbb{E}\, |B|^\alpha < \infty . Assume also that \ln |A| is non-arithmetic, and that Ax + B = x fails with positive probability for every x. Then, for constants with c_+ + c_- > 0,

\mathbb{P}(X > x) \sim c_+ x^{-\alpha}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(X < -x) \sim c_- x^{-\alpha} .

Idea. |X| is large when some product A_1 \cdots A_k is large. Its logarithm is a random walk with negative drift, whose maximum exceeds \ln x with probability of order x^{-\alpha}, where \alpha solves \mathbb{E}\, e^{\alpha \ln |A|} = 1 (the Cramér–Lundberg estimate). Goldie’s implicit renewal theorem makes this rigorous.

Proposition 11.2 — Which moments are finite If \mathbb{E}\, |A|^p < 1 and \mathbb{E}\, |B|^p < \infty for some p > 0, then \mathbb{E}\, |X|^p < \infty.

Proof. For p \leq 1, |x + y|^p \leq |x|^p + |y|^p, so, by independence, \mathbb{E}\, |X|^p \leq \sum_{k \geq 0} \big(\mathbb{E}\, |A|^p\big)^k\, \mathbb{E}\, |B|^p < \infty . For p \geq 1, Minkowski’s inequality gives the same bound for \|X\|_p with \|A\|_p^k \|B\|_p. ∎

The function p \mapsto \mathbb{E}\, |A|^p is convex, equal to 1 at p = 0 and at p = \alpha, and below 1 in between. Moments of order p < \alpha are finite and, by Theorem 11.1, those of order p \geq \alpha are not.

SGD on least squares

Take one-dimensional data y = a w^\ast + \varepsilon with a, \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), and SGD with step \eta and batches of size b. The error X_k = w_k - w^\ast follows a Kesten recursion: X_{k+1} = \Big(1 - \eta\, \frac{1}{b} \sum_{j \leq b} a_j^2\Big) X_k + \eta\, \frac1b \sum_{j \leq b} a_j \varepsilon_j .

Key result — the tail index of SGD \mathbb{E}\, \Big| 1 - \eta\, \frac{\chi^2_b}{b} \Big|^{\alpha} = 1

For b = 1, \mathbb{E}(1 - \eta a^2)^2 = 1 - 2\eta + 3\eta^2, which equals 1 at \eta = 2/3. The stationary variance of SGD is infinite for \eta \geq 2/3, although the data are Gaussian and the iteration is stable. Larger steps and smaller batches give heavier tails; Gurbuzbalaban, Simsekli and Zhu (2021) prove this monotonicity for Gaussian linear regression in any dimension.

The tail index is estimated from the iterates with the Hill estimator (Hill, 1975): with |X|_{(1)} \geq |X|_{(2)} \geq \dots the ordered values, \hat\alpha_k = \Big( \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^{k} \ln \frac{|X|_{(i)}}{|X|_{(k+1)}} \Big)^{-1} . 

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

rng = np.random.default_rng(14)

def tail_index(eta, b):
    """Solve E|1 - eta * mean of b squared Gaussians|^alpha = 1 (Kesten-Goldie exponent)."""
    A = np.abs(1 - eta * rng.chisquare(b, 400_000) / b)
    assert np.mean(np.log(A)) < 0, "unstable: E log|A| >= 0"
    return brentq(lambda al: np.mean(A ** al) - 1, 1e-3, 50)

def hill(x, k):
    x = np.sort(np.abs(x))[::-1]
    return 1 / np.mean(np.log(x[:k] / x[k]))

for eta, b in [(0.5, 1), (0.9, 1), (1.2, 1), (1.2, 10)]:
    alpha = tail_index(eta, b)
    # 1-D least squares, y = a * 1 + noise: SGD iterate x_{k+1} = (1 - eta a^2) x_k + eta a y
    x, xs = 0.0, []
    a = rng.standard_normal((200_000, b)); noise = rng.standard_normal((200_000, b))
    for t in range(200_000):
        g = np.mean(a[t] * (a[t] * x - (a[t] + noise[t])))   # minibatch gradient
        x -= eta * g
        if t > 1000 and t % 10 == 0:
            xs.append(x - 1)
    print(f"eta = {eta}, b = {b:2d}: Kesten exponent alpha = {alpha:5.2f}, "
          f"Hill estimate on the iterates {hill(np.array(xs), 200):5.2f}")

With b = 1, the exponent drops from 2.88 to 1.30 and 0.78 as \eta grows from 0.5 to 0.9 and 1.2; batches of 10 bring it back to 5.07 at \eta = 1.2. The Hill estimates (3.32, 1.14, 0.82, 4.34) follow the same order; they are noisy, since the iterates are correlated and 200 order statistics are few.

In dimension d, A_k = I - \eta H_k is a random matrix and the exponent solves a matrix version of the same equation (Kesten, 1973). In decentralised SGD, agents on a network average their iterates with their neighbours and take local steps. Gurbuzbalaban and coauthors (2022) study how the network topology, the step size and the batch size then shape the tail index.

Key takeaways — chapter 11

  • A random linear recursion with \mathbb{E} \ln|A| < 0 and \mathbb{E}|A|^\alpha = 1 has power-law tails of index \alpha (Kesten–Goldie): multiplicative noise creates heavy tails from light-tailed inputs.
  • SGD on least squares is such a recursion; its tail index solves \mathbb{E}|1 - \eta \chi^2_b / b|^\alpha = 1 and decreases with the step size and increases with the batch size.
  • The Hill estimator measures the index from the iterates.

Constructing infinite systems (complement to ch. 1)

The graphical construction of ch. 1 runs every site with two Poisson clocks of rate \bar c. In infinite volume, the state of a site at time t could in principle depend on infinitely many clock rings. The fix is to work on short time windows.

Proposition 12.1 — Finite influence clusters On a graph of maximum degree \Delta, call a site active on [0, t_0] if one of its clocks rings in that window, which happens with probability q = 1 - e^{-2 \bar c t_0}. If q (\Delta - 1) < 1, every cluster of active sites is finite almost surely.

Proof. A cluster containing x and reaching graph distance n contains a self-avoiding path of n active sites from a neighbour of x. There are at most \Delta (\Delta - 1)^{n-1} such paths, each active with probability q^n, so the probability is at most \Delta (\Delta - 1)^{n-1} q^n \to 0 . ∎

On [0, t_0], a site interacts only with its finite cluster, so its evolution is a finite computation. Repeating on [t_0, 2t_0], [2t_0, 3t_0], and so on, defines the process for all times, with the same clocks coupling all initial conditions.

The voter model on lattices (complement to ch. 2)

Theorem 12.2 — Clustering or coexistence (Holley and Liggett, 1975) Start the voter model on \mathbb{Z}^d from independent Bernoulli(\theta) opinions.

If d \leq 2, \mathbb{P}(\eta_t(x) \neq \eta_t(y)) \to 0 for all sites: large clusters of agreement form. If d \geq 3, \eta_t converges in law to an invariant measure under which both opinions coexist with densities \theta and 1 - \theta.

Proof sketch. By duality (Proposition 2.5), \eta_t(x) \neq \eta_t(y) requires that the lineages of x and y have not met by time t, and that their ancestors disagree. The difference of the two lineages is a random walk, recurrent if and only if d \leq 2 (Pólya). In d \geq 3 lineages escape each other with positive probability, and the limit law is read off the coalescing walks.

Disordered systems (complement to ch. 3)

If the couplings J_{xy} of the Ising model are themselves random, of both signs, some loops cannot satisfy all their bonds: the system is frustrated. These spin glasses, the Edwards–Anderson model on a lattice and the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model on the complete graph, have the same Metropolis and heat-bath dynamics as in ch. 3. They have many metastable states and relax very slowly (Mézard and Montanari, 2009).

The statistical physics of disordered systems studies their typical behaviour over the randomness of the couplings. Learning the couplings from samples, the inverse problem, is the subject of ch. 8.

Scale invariance and power laws (complement to ch. 4)

Near a critical point, as in the Ising model at T_c, fluctuations have no characteristic size. The distributions of cluster or event sizes then follow power laws, for a simple reason.

Proposition 12.3 — Scale invariance forces a power law Let p be a positive measurable density on (0, \infty) such that p(\lambda s) = g(\lambda)\, p(s) for all \lambda, s > 0. Then, for some exponent \tau,

p(s) = p(1)\, s^{-\tau} .

Proof. Taking s = 1 gives g(\lambda) = p(\lambda)/p(1), hence p(\lambda s)\, p(1) = p(\lambda)\, p(s). The function u(x) = \ln\big(p(e^x)/p(1)\big) is then measurable and additive, u(x + y) = u(x) + u(y), so it is linear: u(x) = -\tau x. ∎

An exponential law e^{-s/s_0} has the scale s_0; a power law has none. A straight line on a log-log plot is weak evidence: Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (2009) give maximum-likelihood estimators and goodness-of-fit tests.

Mean-field limits and propagation of chaos (complement to ch. 2, 3 and 8)

The mean-field equations of ch. 2 (contact process) and ch. 3 (Curie–Weiss) are instances of a general limit. Consider N exchangeable particles in \mathbb{R}^d, driven by independent Brownian motions W_i: dX_i = \frac1N \sum_{j=1}^N b(X_i, X_j)\, dt + \sigma\, dW_i .

Theorem 12.4 — Propagation of chaos (Sznitman, 1991) Let b be bounded and Lipschitz and the initial positions i.i.d. with law \mu_0. As N \to \infty, the empirical measure of the particles converges to the solution \mu_t of the McKean–Vlasov equation \partial_t \mu_t = -\nabla \cdot \Big( \mu_t \int b(\cdot, y)\, \mu_t(dy) \Big) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \Delta \mu_t , and any fixed number of particles become asymptotically independent, each with law \mu_t.

Proof sketch. Couple each X_i with the nonlinear process \bar X_i, driven by the same W_i with drift \int b(\bar X_i, y)\, \mu_t(dy). The difference of drifts splits into a Lipschitz term in |X_i - \bar X_i| and a law-of-large-numbers term of order N^{-1/2}.

Gronwall’s lemma then gives \mathbb{E}\, |X_i - \bar X_i| \leq C_T N^{-1/2} on [0, T].

The pairwise kernels learned in ch. 8 are the drifts b(x, y) = \phi(\|y - x\|)(y - x) of this framework. Gradient descent on a wide two-layer neural network is an interacting particle system of the same type, with one particle per neuron (Mei, Montanari and Nguyen, 2018; Chizat and Bach, 2018).

Hydrodynamics of the exclusion process (complement to ch. 2)

In the totally asymmetric exclusion process (TASEP) on \mathbb{Z}, each particle jumps one step to the right at rate 1 if the site is empty. Under the invariant measure \nu_\rho of ch. 2, the current of particles across an edge is j(\rho) = \rho(1 - \rho).

At large scales, local equilibrium holds: around position x = \xi t, the configuration looks like \nu_{\rho} with a density \rho(t, x) that solves the Burgers equation \partial_t \rho + \partial_x \big( \rho (1 - \rho) \big) = 0 .

Theorem 12.5 — Rarefaction fan (Rost, 1981) Start TASEP with every site x \leq 0 occupied and every site x > 0 empty. The density at position \xi t at time t converges to \rho(\xi) = \frac{1 - \xi}{2} \quad \text{for } |\xi| \leq 1, \qquad \rho = 1 \text{ for } \xi < -1, \qquad \rho = 0 \text{ for } \xi > 1 .

Why this profile. Density disturbances travel at the characteristic speed j'(\rho) = 1 - 2\rho. In a self-similar solution \rho(\xi), the density present at position \xi t is the one whose speed is \xi, so \xi = 1 - 2\rho. The front of the jam advances at speed 1 into empty space, and the jam melts backwards at speed 1.

Interacting particles, each moving at speed at most 1, produce a deterministic macroscopic flow with shocks and rarefaction fans: the prototype of traffic models.

Stochastic resetting and first passage (complement to ch. 10)

A diffusing searcher looking for a target can do better by restarting from scratch from time to time (Evans and Majumdar, 2011).

Let X be a Brownian motion with diffusion coefficient D, that is with generator D\, \partial_x^2, started at x_0 > 0.

It is reset to x_0 at the times of a Poisson process of rate r, and T is its first passage time at 0.

Proposition 12.6 — Mean first-passage time under resetting With z = x_0 \sqrt{r / D}, \mathbb{E}\, T = \frac{e^{z} - 1}{r} . It is finite for every r > 0, although \mathbb{E}\, T = \infty without resetting. It is minimal when z/2 = 1 - e^{-z}, that is z^\ast = 1.5936, with the value

\min_r \mathbb{E}\, T = 1.544\, \frac{x_0^2}{D} .

Proof. Let \tau(x) be the mean first-passage time from x, so that \tau(x_0) = \mathbb{E}\, T. During a time dt, the process diffuses, or is reset with probability r\, dt. This gives the backward equation D \tau''(x) - r\, \tau(x) + r\, \tau(x_0) = -1, \qquad \tau(0) = 0, with \tau bounded at infinity. Its solution is \tau(x) = \frac{1 + r\, \tau(x_0)}{r} \big( 1 - e^{-x \sqrt{r/D}} \big). Setting x = x_0 and solving for \tau(x_0) gives the formula. Writing \mathbb{E}\, T = (x_0^2 / D)(e^z - 1)/z^2 and differentiating in z gives the optimality condition. ∎

The simulation is exact: each excursion is a free first-passage time x_0^2 / (2 D Z^2), with Z standard Gaussian, unless the next reset comes first.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
D, x0 = 1.0, 1.0
z_star = brentq(lambda z: z / 2 - 1 + np.exp(-z), 0.5, 3)        # optimality condition
r_star = D * z_star**2 / x0**2
mfpt = lambda r: (np.exp(x0 * np.sqrt(r / D)) - 1) / r

def first_passage(r):
    """Exact simulation: free first-passage times x0^2 / (2 D Z^2) cut by Exp(r) resets."""
    total = 0.0
    while True:
        T = x0**2 / (2 * D * rng.standard_normal() ** 2)          # free first-passage time
        R = rng.exponential(1 / r)                                 # next reset
        if T < R:
            return total + T
        total += R

for r in [0.5, r_star, 8.0]:
    sim = np.mean([first_passage(r) for _ in range(40000)])
    print(f"r = {r:5.3f}: simulated MFPT {sim:.3f}, formula (e^z - 1)/r = {mfpt(r):.3f}")
print(f"optimal z* = {z_star:.4f}, r* = {r_star:.4f}, minimal MFPT = {mfpt(r_star):.4f} x0^2/D")

The simulated means are 2.052, 1.542 and 2.018 against 2.056, 1.544 and 1.990. In first-passage resetting, the reset is triggered by the process itself, when it reaches a threshold, rather than by a clock; de Bruyne, Randon-Furling and Redner (2020) study optimisation problems for such processes. Evans, Majumdar and Schehr (2020) review the field.

Rare-event simulation (complement to ch. 9)

Crude Monte Carlo needs about 100/p samples to estimate a probability p with 10% relative error. The two principles of ch. 9 lead to two different remedies.

  • Light tails: exponential tilting. Sample the X_i from the tilted law of Theorem 9.1, under which the rare event is typical, and reweight by the likelihood ratio e^{-\theta S_n + n \Lambda(\theta)}. With \Lambda'(\theta) = a, the relative error grows only polynomially in n.
  • Heavy tails: conditioning on the big jump. Tilting fails, since \Lambda = \infty for \theta > 0. The Asmussen–Kroese estimator uses the catastrophe principle instead: with M_{n-1} and S_{n-1} the maximum and sum of n - 1 summands, \mathbb{P}(S_n > x) = n\, \mathbb{E}\, \bar F\big( \max(M_{n-1},\ x - S_{n-1}) \big). 
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(16)
R = 20000                                         # samples per estimator

# Light tails: P(S_n > n a) for Gaussian steps, by crude Monte Carlo and by exponential tilting
n, a = 50, 0.5
exact = norm.sf(n * a / np.sqrt(n))
crude = (rng.standard_normal((R, n)).sum(1) > n * a)
Xt = rng.standard_normal((R, n)) + a              # tilted law: N(a, 1), theta = a
St = Xt.sum(1)
w = np.exp(-a * St + n * a**2 / 2) * (St > n * a)  # likelihood ratio dP / dP_theta on the event
print(f"Gaussian, exact {exact:.3e}: crude {crude.mean():.3e} (rel. s.e. {crude.std() / crude.mean() / np.sqrt(R):.2f}), "
      f"tilted {w.mean():.3e} (rel. s.e. {w.std() / w.mean() / np.sqrt(R):.3f})")

# Heavy tails: P(S_n > x) for Pareto(1.5) steps, by crude MC and by Asmussen-Kroese conditioning
alpha, n, x = 1.5, 10, 1000.0
tail = lambda y: np.where(y > 1, np.maximum(y, 1) ** -alpha, 1.0)
X = rng.pareto(alpha, (R, n)) + 1
crude = X.sum(1) > x
Y = X[:, :-1]
ak = n * tail(np.maximum(Y.max(1), x - Y.sum(1)))  # n * P(X_n > max(M_{n-1}, x - S_{n-1}))
print(f"Pareto, n P(X > x) = {n * x**-alpha:.3e}: crude {crude.mean():.3e} (rel. s.e. "
      f"{crude.std() / crude.mean() / np.sqrt(R):.2f}), conditional {ak.mean():.3e} "
      f"(rel. s.e. {ak.std() / ak.mean() / np.sqrt(R):.1e})")

With 20000 samples, tilting reduces the relative error from 50% to 1.4% on a probability of 2.0 \times 10^{-4}. The conditional estimator gives 3.295 \times 10^{-4} with a relative error below 10^{-3}, where crude Monte Carlo has 38%. The single-big-jump approximation n \bar F(x) = 3.16 \times 10^{-4} is 4% too low at this level.

Universality of persistence (complement to ch. 10)

The combinatorial lemmas of ch. 10 have a striking consequence for the probability that a walk stays positive.

Theorem 12.7 — Sparre Andersen (1954) If the increments are i.i.d. with a symmetric continuous law, then for every n, \mathbb{P}(S_1 > 0, \dots, S_n > 0) = \binom{2n}{n} 4^{-n} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}, whatever the law: Gaussian, uniform or Cauchy.

Proof sketch. Sparre Andersen’s combinatorial lemma, a relative of Lemma 10.1, gives the generating-function identity \sum_{n \geq 0} \mathbb{P}(S_1 > 0, \dots, S_n > 0)\, s^n = \exp\Big( \sum_{k \geq 1} \frac{s^k}{k}\, \mathbb{P}(S_k > 0) \Big). By symmetry and continuity \mathbb{P}(S_k > 0) = 1/2, so the right-hand side is (1 - s)^{-1/2}, whose coefficients are \binom{2n}{n} 4^{-n}.

import numpy as np
from math import comb

rng = np.random.default_rng(17)
n, R = 10, 200_000
steps = {"Gaussian": rng.standard_normal((R, n)),
         "Cauchy": rng.standard_cauchy((R, n)),
         "uniform": rng.uniform(-1, 1, (R, n))}
for name, X in steps.items():
    stays_positive = (np.cumsum(X, axis=1) > 0).all(axis=1)
    print(f"{name:8s}: P(S_1 > 0, ..., S_10 > 0) = {stays_positive.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Sparre Andersen: binom(20, 10) / 4^10 = {comb(2 * n, n) / 4**n:.4f}")

The three estimates, 0.1769, 0.1764 and 0.1768, agree with 0.1762 within Monte Carlo error. Majumdar (2010) surveys these universal first-passage properties and their use in physics.

Key takeaways — chapter 12

  • Infinite systems are built on short time windows, on which influence clusters are finite.
  • Mean-field limits replace N interacting particles by one nonlinear process (propagation of chaos); TASEP has the Burgers equation as its hydrodynamic limit.
  • Poissonian resetting makes the mean first-passage time finite, with an optimal rate.
  • Rare events of light-tailed sums are simulated by tilting, those of heavy-tailed sums by conditioning on the big jump; persistence probabilities of symmetric walks are universal.

13. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is a self-study tour of active questions that extend the course. It cites only work whose content it describes; the references give the entry points.

Inference for agent-based models

  • Simulation-based inference. The likelihood of an ABM is almost never computable, but the model can be simulated. Approximate Bayesian computation (Beaumont, Zhang and Balding, 2002) accepts parameters whose simulations resemble the data. Neural methods learn posteriors or likelihood ratios from simulations (Cranmer, Brehmer and Louppe, 2020). Open questions: which parameters of an ABM are identifiable from macro-data, and how to detect misspecification.
  • Surrogates. When one run is expensive, a statistical emulator of the simulator is fitted on a design of runs and used for calibration and sensitivity analysis; see Learning Physical Phenomena. Building surrogates that keep the rare collective events of ch. 6 and ch. 9 is open.
  • Learning the rules. Lu, Maggioni and Tang (2021) extend the learning of interaction kernels to heterogeneous systems of agents observed along multiple trajectories. Sparse regression on a library of candidate terms identifies governing equations from data (Brunton, Proctor and Kutz, 2016). Learning rules of stochastic agents, on unknown networks, from partial observations, is largely open; Krakauer (2023) frames the general programme of unifying complexity science and machine learning.

Interaction structure and causality

  • Sample-optimal inverse Ising. Vuffray, Misra, Lokhov and Chertkov (2016) introduce the interaction screening estimator and show that it recovers Ising graphs with a near-optimal number of samples; Nguyen, Zecchina and Berg (2017) review inverse statistical problems from physics to data science. Learning with hidden units, or from dynamics instead of equilibrium samples, raises new identifiability questions.
  • Interference. Aronow and Samii (2017) estimate average causal effects under general interference, extending Hudgens and Halloran (2008). How to design experiments on networks with spillovers, and how to validate the designs on ABMs with a known truth (Manzo, 2022), are active topics.

Heavy tails, learning and optimisation

  • Heavy tails in deep learning. Simsekli, Sagun and Gurbuzbalaban (2019) report heavy-tailed gradient noise in deep networks. Hodgkinson and Mahoney (2021) show that multiplicative noise produces heavy tails in stochastic optimisation, the mechanism of ch. 11. Simsekli, Sener, Deligiannidis and Erdogdu (2020) relate heavy tails to the Hausdorff dimension of the trajectories and to generalisation bounds. Whether heavy tails help generalisation in practice is debated.
  • Decentralised optimisation. Gurbuzbalaban and coauthors (2022) study the tail index of decentralised SGD; how the communication graph should be chosen when tails matter is open.
  • Training as an interacting system. The mean-field analyses of Mei, Montanari and Nguyen (2018) and Chizat and Bach (2018) treat neurons as interacting particles (Theorem 12.4). Quantitative propagation of chaos along training, and the corrections at finite width, remain active questions.

Extrema, criticality and resetting

  • Convex hulls beyond the mean. Majumdar, Comtet and Randon-Furling (2010) review the mean perimeter and area of hulls of random walks, Brownian paths and Lévy processes. Kabluchko, Vysotsky and Zaporozhets (2017) prove distribution-free formulas for the faces of convex hulls of random walks in any dimension. Full distributions of the perimeter and area, and hulls of interacting walkers, are open.
  • Timeliness criticality. Moran and coauthors (2024) find a critical buffer in networks of agents with delays, with avalanches near it. The universality class of delay avalanches on structured networks, finite-size corrections like those of ch. 6, and the control of buffers in real systems are open.
  • Resetting and restarts. Evans, Majumdar and Schehr (2020) review stochastic resetting; de Bruyne, Randon-Furling and Redner (2020) study optimisation in first-passage resetting. In computer science, Luby, Sinclair and Zuckerman (1993) give optimal restart strategies for randomised algorithms. Resetting in systems of interacting searchers is a natural next step.

Key takeaways — chapter 13

  • Inference for ABMs relies on simulation: likelihood-free methods, surrogates and rule learning, with identifiability as the central open question.
  • Network inference and causal inference under interference are both questions about the structure of interactions.
  • Heavy tails, convex hulls, critical buffers and resetting connect the extreme-value part of the course with learning algorithms and socio-technical systems.

Exercises

The exercises are sorted by level: ★ applies the course, ★★ combines results or proves a short statement, ★★★ is open-ended. The session plan says which ones to do after each session.

  1. ★ The two-state chain. A site switches from 0 to 1 at rate a and from 1 to 0 at rate b. Write the rate matrix Q, compute P_t = e^{tQ}, and find the stationary law. Check detailed balance.

    Solution: Q has rows (-a, a) and (b, -b), with eigenvalues 0 and -(a+b). Hence P_t(0, 1) = \frac{a}{a + b} \big(1 - e^{-(a+b)t}\big), \qquad P_t(1, 0) = \frac{b}{a + b} \big(1 - e^{-(a+b)t}\big). The stationary law is \pi = (b, a)/(a + b), and \pi(0)\, a = \pi(1)\, b = ab/(a+b) is detailed balance.

  2. ★ Extinction. A Galton–Watson process has offspring law \mathbb{P}(\xi = 0) = \mathbb{P}(\xi = 1) = 1/4, \mathbb{P}(\xi = 2) = 1/2. Compute its mean and its extinction probability from one and from three ancestors.

    Solution: m = 1/4 + 1 = 1.25 > 1. The equation G(s) = \frac14 + \frac{s}{4} + \frac{s^2}{2} = s reads 2s^2 - 3s + 1 = 0, with roots 1/2 and 1. Hence q = 1/2 from one ancestor and q^3 = 1/8 from three (independent subtrees).

  3. ★ Independent flips. Each site of a finite set S flips from 0 to 1 at rate a and from 1 to 0 at rate b, independently. Write the generator and show that the product measure \nu = \mathrm{Bernoulli}(a/(a+b))^{\otimes S} is reversible.

    Solution: c(x, \eta) = a (1 - \eta(x)) + b\, \eta(x) and L f(\eta) = \sum_x c(x, \eta) [f(\eta^x) - f(\eta)]. For \eta(x) = 0, \nu(\eta^x)/\nu(\eta) = a/b, which equals c(x, \eta)/c(x, \eta^x) = a/b. This is detailed balance for every flip.

  4. ★ The voter model on a star. A star has a centre and n leaves. Compute the probability of consensus on 1 when only the centre holds opinion 1, and when only one leaf does.

    Solution: the degrees are n for the centre and 1 for each leaf, with 2|E| = 2n. By Proposition 2.4 the probabilities are n/(2n) = 1/2 and 1/(2n), far from the unweighted fractions 1/(n+1) and 1/(n+1).

  5. ★ Metropolis or heat-bath? For \beta \Delta = 2, compute the Metropolis and heat-bath rates of a flip and of its reverse. Show that the Metropolis rate is always at least the heat-bath rate.

    Solution: Metropolis gives 0.135 and 1; heat-bath gives 1/(1 + e^2) = 0.119 and 0.881. Both ratios equal e^{-2}.

    For u \geq 0, e^{-u} \geq 1/(1 + e^u) because 1 + e^u \geq e^u; for u < 0, the Metropolis rate is 1. By Peskun’s (1973) ordering, Metropolis therefore has a smaller asymptotic variance for every observable.

  6. ★ The Borel law. For critical Poisson branching (\mu = 1), compute \mathbb{P}(S = s) for s = 1, 2, 3, and check the first value directly. Compute \mathbb{E}\, S for \mu = 1/2.

    Solution: e^{-1} = 0.368, e^{-2} = 0.135 and \frac32 e^{-3} = 0.0747. Directly, S = 1 means no child, of probability e^{-1}.

    For \mu = 1/2, \mathbb{E}\, S = 1/(1 - \mu) = 2.

  7. ★ Final size and vaccination. For R_0 = 2, compute the final size, the herd-immunity threshold and the overshoot. Then vaccinate 30% of the population beforehand and compute the fraction infected.

    Solution: z = 1 - e^{-2z} gives z = 0.797; the threshold is 0.5, so the overshoot is 0.297.

    With 30% immune, the susceptibles are 70% and the effective reproduction number is 1.4. The final fraction y of the susceptibles solves y = 1 - e^{-1.4 y}, so y = 0.511, and 0.7\, y = 0.358 of the population is infected.

  8. ★ Kirman’s model at a = 1 (Python). Simulate Kirman’s model with N = 50 for a = 0.5, 1 and 2, and compare the time fractions with the beta-binomial law. Show that the stationary law is uniform on \{0, \dots, N\} when a = 1.

    Solution: with a = 1, \binom{N}{k} B(k + 1, N - k + 1) = 1/(N + 1), so \pi(k) = 1/(N + 1): the transition between unimodal and bimodal laws is a flat law. The simulation below gives total variations of a few percent:

    import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import betabinom

rng = np.random.default_rng(18)
N, h = 50, 1.0
for a in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    eps = a * h / (N - 1)
    k, time_in = N // 2, np.zeros(N + 1)
    for _ in range(300000):
        up = (N - k) * (eps + h * k / (N - 1))
        down = k * (eps + h * (N - k) / (N - 1))
        time_in[k] += rng.exponential(1 / (up + down))
        k += 1 if rng.random() < up / (up + down) else -1
    emp = time_in / time_in.sum()
    exact = betabinom.pmf(np.arange(N + 1), N, a, a)
    print(f"a = {a}: total variation to beta-binomial {0.5 * np.abs(emp - exact).sum():.3f}, "
          f"max |pi(k) - 1/(N+1)| = {np.abs(exact - 1 / (N + 1)).max():.4f}")

    The output gives total variations 0.033, 0.023 and 0.037, and a deviation from uniform of 0.0000 for a = 1.

  9. ★★ Mean field for the contact process. On the complete graph with N sites and infection rate \lambda/N per infected–healthy pair, compute L f for f(\eta) = |\eta|/N. Derive the mean-field equation and study the stability of its fixed points.

    Solution: with \rho = |\eta|/N, L f = \frac{\lambda}{N} \cdot \frac{|\eta| (N - |\eta|)}{N} - \frac{|\eta|}{N} = \lambda \rho (1 - \rho) - \rho . Neglecting fluctuations, which are of order N^{-1/2}, gives \dot\rho = \lambda\rho(1 - \rho) - \rho.

    The derivative of the right-hand side is \lambda - 1 at 0, and 1 - \lambda at \rho^\ast = 1 - 1/\lambda. The endemic state exists and is stable exactly when \lambda > 1, and 0 is then unstable.

  10. ★★ Curie–Weiss dynamics lumps. Show that heat-bath dynamics on the complete graph lumps to the number k of + spins. Write its birth and death rates, and check detailed balance for \pi(k) \propto \binom{N}{k} e^{\beta J M^2 / (2N)} with M = 2k - N.

    Solution: the dynamics is invariant under permutations of the sites, so Proposition 7.3 applies. Let u = 2\beta J (M + 1)/N.

    A - spin sees the field (J/N)(M + 1), so k \to k + 1 at rate (N - k)/(1 + e^{-u}).

    In the state k + 1, a + spin sees the same field, so k + 1 \to k at rate (k + 1)/(1 + e^{u}).

    The ratio of the rates is \frac{N - k}{k + 1} e^{u}, and so is \pi(k + 1)/\pi(k), since (M + 2)^2 - M^2 = 4(M + 1).

  11. ★★ Critical buffers (Python). Compute v_K of Theorem 6.3 for K = 2, 3, 5 and compare it with the mean of the largest of K independent \mathrm{Exp}(1) delays, H_K = 1 + \frac12 + \dots + \frac1K. Explain the gap.

    Hints and expected result: minimising (\ln K - \ln(1 - \theta))/\theta numerically gives 2.678, 3.289 and 3.994 for K = 2, 3 and 5, against H_K = 1.5, 1.833 and 2.283.

    A buffer equal to the mean largest input delay is far from enough: delays accumulate along the paths of the dependency tree, and the critical buffer is set by the best of K^s paths, a large-deviation quantity.

  12. ★★ Lumping the path. For the voter model on the path 1 - 2 - 3, use the reflection 1 \leftrightarrow 3 to build a lumpable partition, and compute the probability of consensus on 1 from (0, 1, 0) and from (1, 0, 0).

    Solution: the orbits are {000}, {111}, {100, 001}, {010}, {110, 011} and {101}, lumpable by Proposition 7.3.

    With degrees (1, 2, 1) and 2|E| = 4, Proposition 2.4 gives 2/4 = 1/2 from 010 and 1/4 from 100.

  13. ★★ Chernoff and its prefactor. For i.i.d. \mathrm{Exp}(1) variables, compute I(a) and compare the Chernoff bound with the exact value of \mathbb{P}(S_{20} \geq 40). Explain the remaining factor.

    Solution: \Lambda(\theta) = -\ln(1 - \theta), so I(a) = a - 1 - \ln a and I(2) = 0.307. The bound is e^{-20 \times 0.307} = 2.16 \times 10^{-3}, while S_{20} is Gamma(20, 1) with \mathbb{P}(S_{20} \geq 40) = 1.76 \times 10^{-4}.

    The Bahadur–Rao refinement multiplies the bound by 1/(\theta \sigma \sqrt{2\pi n}), with \theta = 1/2 and tilted standard deviation \sigma = a = 2. This factor is 0.089, giving 1.93 \times 10^{-4}.

  14. ★★ Kac’s formula in two steps (Python). For standard Gaussian steps, compute \mathbb{E} \max(0, S_1, S_2) with Theorem 10.2 and check it by simulation.

    Solution: \mathbb{E}\, S_1^+ = 1/\sqrt{2\pi} and \mathbb{E}\, S_2^+ / 2 = \sqrt 2 / (2\sqrt{2\pi}), so \mathbb{E} \max(0, S_1, S_2) = \frac{1 + 1/\sqrt 2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} = 0.6810 .

  15. ★★ When is the variance of SGD infinite? In the model of ch. 11 with batch size b, find the step size at which \alpha = 2. Evaluate it for b = 1 and b = 10.

    Solution: \chi^2_b / b has mean 1 and variance 2/b, so \mathbb{E}\Big(1 - \eta\, \frac{\chi^2_b}{b}\Big)^2 = 1 - 2\eta + \eta^2 \Big(1 + \frac2b\Big). It equals 1 at \eta = 2b/(b + 2): 2/3 for b = 1 and 5/3 for b = 10. Beyond this step size, by Proposition 11.2 and Theorem 11.1, the stationary variance is infinite.

  16. ★★ Optimal resetting. Prove the optimality condition of Proposition 12.6 and the value of the minimum.

    Solution: \mathbb{E}\, T = (x_0^2/D)\, g(z) with g(z) = (e^z - 1)/z^2. Then g'(z) = 0 gives z e^z = 2(e^z - 1), that is z/2 = 1 - e^{-z}.

  18. ★★★ The learning rate of interaction kernels. Repeat the experiment of ch. 8 with the smooth kernel \phi(r) = e^{-r^2}, for M = 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 trajectories, with a number of bins proportional to M^{1/3}.   19. ★★★ Avalanches of delays. In the model of Theorem 6.3 with N = 5000 and K = 2, choose B slightly above the finite-N threshold. Written exam (proposal)

Format: 2 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic device; four independent exercises, 20 points.

  1. Course question (4 points). Define a stochastic Ising model. Show that the heat-bath rate satisfies the definition, and that Kawasaki dynamics conserves the magnetisation.

    Expected: Definition 3.2; the ratio of the heat-bath rates of a flip and its reverse is e^{-\beta\Delta}, using \Delta_x(\sigma^x) = -\Delta_x(\sigma); an exchange of two unequal neighbouring spins keeps the number of + spins, and the dynamics is reversible for the measure conditioned on the magnetisation.

  2. Computation (6 points). A stochastic SIR epidemic has R_0 = 3. (a) Give the probability of a major outbreak from one and from two initial cases.     Expected: (a) 1 - 1/3 = 2/3 and 1 - 1/9 = 8/9; (b) z = 1 - e^{-3z}, z = 0.940, threshold 2/3; (c) more than 1 - 1/R_0 = 2/3, so that the effective reproduction number falls below 1. Two points per part.

  3. Short proof (5 points). Let X_1, X_2 be i.i.d. Pareto with index \alpha. Prove that \mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 > x) \sim 2\, \mathbb{P}(X_1 > x). For i.i.d. \mathrm{Exp}(1) variables, compute the same ratio.

  4. Problem (5 points). For the voter model on a finite connected graph, prove that the degree-weighted magnetisation is a martingale. On the path with 4 vertices, compute the probability of consensus on 1 when the two middle vertices hold opinion 1.

    Expected: the generator computation of Proposition 2.4 and optional stopping (3 points). Degrees (1, 2, 2, 1), total 6, so the probability is 4/6 = 2/3 (2 points).
