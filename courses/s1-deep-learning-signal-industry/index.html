0. Review: Fourier bases, Gaussian noise and the chain rule

Every later chapter projects signals on Fourier-type bases, models noise as Gaussian and differentiates compositions of functions. This review fixes the notation and proves the three facts used most often: the discrete Fourier basis is orthonormal, Gaussian vectors stay Gaussian under linear maps, and the energy of Gaussian noise follows a chi-square law.

Definition 0.1 — Inner product, orthonormal basis On \mathbb{C}^N the inner product and the norm are \langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, \overline{y[n]}, \qquad \|x\|^2 = \langle x, x \rangle .

A family (e_k) is an orthonormal basis when \langle e_k, e_l \rangle = \delta_{kl} and it spans \mathbb{C}^N. Every signal then expands on it, and its energy is the sum of the squared coefficients (Parseval): x = \sum_k \langle x, e_k \rangle\, e_k, \qquad \|x\|^2 = \sum_k |\langle x, e_k \rangle|^2 .

Proposition 0.2 — The discrete Fourier basis The vectors e_k[n] = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\, e^{2i\pi k n / N}, \qquad k = 0, \dots, N-1, form an orthonormal basis of \mathbb{C}^N.

Proof. For k \neq l, put q = e^{2i\pi (k - l)/N}. Then q \neq 1 and q^N = 1, so the geometric sum vanishes: \langle e_k, e_l \rangle = \frac1N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} q^n = \frac1N\, \frac{1 - q^N}{1 - q} = 0 .

For k = l, every term equals 1/N and the sum is 1. Finally, N orthonormal vectors are linearly independent, hence a basis of a space of dimension N. ∎

The numbers \sqrt N \langle x, e_k \rangle are the DFT of ch. 1.3, and Parseval says that the DFT preserves energy up to the factor N.

Definition 0.3 — Gaussian vectors and complex white noise A random vector x \in \mathbb{R}^d is Gaussian, written x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma), when every linear form a^\top x is a real Gaussian variable. For an invertible covariance \Sigma its density is p(x) = (2\pi)^{-d/2} \det(\Sigma)^{-1/2} \exp\Big(-\frac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\Big).

A complex variable w = u + iv is circular Gaussian, w \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma^2), when u and v are independent with law \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2/2); then \mathbb E|w|^2 = \sigma^2.

In this course, white Gaussian noise of power \sigma^2 is a sequence of independent \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma^2) samples (or \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) for real signals). Its expected power is the same at every frequency.

Proposition 0.4 — Linear maps and the energy of Gaussian noise

  1. If x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma), A \in \mathbb R^{m \times d} and b \in \mathbb R^m, then Ax + b \sim \mathcal N(A\mu + b,\ A \Sigma A^\top).
  2. If z_1, \dots, z_k are independent \mathcal N(0, 1), then \sum_i z_i^2 follows the chi-square law \chi^2_k, with mean k and variance 2k.
  3. For N samples of complex white noise of power \sigma^2: \frac{2}{\sigma^2} \sum_{n=1}^{N} |w[n]|^2 \sim \chi^2_{2N}.

Proof.

  1. A linear form of Ax + b is (A^\top a)^\top x + a^\top b: a linear form of x plus a constant, hence Gaussian. The mean and the covariance follow from the linearity of expectation.
  2. The mean is k\, \mathbb E z^2 = k. Since \mathbb E z^4 = 3, each term has variance 3 - 1 = 2, and independence adds the variances.
  3. Write |w[n]|^2 = u^2 + v^2 with u, v independent \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2/2). Then 2|w[n]|^2 / \sigma^2 is a sum of two squared standard Gaussians, and the N independent samples give 2N squares. ∎

The chi-square law sets the threshold of the energy detector (ch. 5.3) and of the Mahalanobis alarm (ch. 9.4).

Definition 0.5 — Jacobian, gradient and the chain rule For a differentiable f : \mathbb R^d \to \mathbb R^m, the Jacobian J_f(x) \in \mathbb R^{m \times d} collects the partial derivatives \partial f_i / \partial x_j. For m = 1 the gradient is \nabla f = J_f^\top. For a composition h = g \circ f, J_h(x) = J_g(f(x))\, J_f(x).

Two gradients are used repeatedly. For a symmetric matrix A: \nabla_x \Big(\frac12 x^\top A x - b^\top x\Big) = Ax - b, \qquad \nabla_x\, \frac12 \|y - Hx\|^2 = H^\top (Hx - y).

Example 0.6 — Power ratios in decibels Signal-processing results are quoted in decibels: a power ratio r is 10 \log_{10} r dB.

  • A signal of power 0.1 in noise of power 1 has an SNR of -10 dB; at -20 dB the ratio is 0.01.
  • Halving an amplitude divides the power by 4, that is -6.02 dB.
  • Coherently integrating N samples of a known signal multiplies the SNR by N (ch. 1.6); with N = 1000: \text{gain} = 10 \log_{10} 1000 = 30 \text{ dB}.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The discrete Fourier vectors form an orthonormal basis; energy is preserved (Parseval).
  • Gaussian vectors stay Gaussian under affine maps; the energy of white Gaussian noise is a scaled chi-square variable.
  • Gradients of compositions are products of Jacobians — the whole of backpropagation (ch. 4.2).
  • Decibels turn power ratios into sums; integration over N samples buys 10 \log_{10} N dB.

1. Signals, sampling and filtering

A sensor delivers a continuous waveform; a computer stores a finite list of numbers. This chapter states when the list loses nothing (sampling), how to read its frequency content (DFT), and how to transform it linearly (filters), up to the matched filter that every radar and receiver uses.

1.1 Signals and complex baseband

Radio, radar and many vibration signals are narrowband: their energy sits in a band of width B around a carrier f_c with B \ll f_c. Sampling them directly would require more than 2 f_c samples per second; one samples instead the slowly varying envelope.

Definition 1.1 — Complex baseband (complex envelope) A real bandpass signal of carrier f_c is written x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big\{ s(t)\, e^{2i\pi f_c t} \big\}, \qquad s(t) = I(t) + i\, Q(t), where the complex envelope s has its spectrum in [-B/2, B/2].

The receiver mixes x with a cosine and a sine at the carrier and low-pass filters the products. This yields the in-phase and quadrature components I and Q: the IQ samples.

Example 1.2 — Why IQ samples An X-band radar signal at f_c = 10 GHz with B = 20 MHz would need more than 2(f_c + B/2) \approx 20.02 GHz if sampled as a real signal. Its complex envelope, of bandwidth 20 MHz, needs only 20 million complex samples per second.

\text{rate reduction} \approx \frac{20\,020 \text{ MHz}}{2 \times 20 \text{ MHz}} \approx 500 .

1.2 Sampling and aliasing

The Fourier transform of a finite-energy signal is X(f) = \int x(t)\, e^{-2i\pi f t}\, dt. A sampling period T_s defines the rate f_s = 1/T_s and the samples x[n] = x(n T_s).

Theorem 1.3 — Sampling theorem (Shannon–Nyquist) Let x be integrable and square-integrable, with X(f) = 0 for |f| \geq f_s/2. Then x is determined by its samples, through the interpolation formula x(t) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb Z} x(nT_s)\, \mathrm{sinc}\Big(\frac{t - nT_s}{T_s}\Big), \qquad \mathrm{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin \pi u}{\pi u}.

Proof. Sketch. The Poisson summation formula gives the spectrum of the sampled sequence as the periodisation of X: \sum_{n} x(nT_s)\, e^{-2i\pi f n T_s} = f_s \sum_{k \in \mathbb Z} X(f - k f_s).

When X vanishes outside (-f_s/2, f_s/2), the copies do not overlap. Multiplying by the ideal low-pass window, equal to 1/f_s on [-f_s/2, f_s/2] and 0 elsewhere, therefore returns exactly X.

In time, multiplication by this window is convolution with its inverse transform, the sinc kernel scaled by T_s, which is the interpolation formula. ∎

When the hypothesis fails, the copies overlap and a component at f reappears at f - k f_s: this is aliasing. No processing after sampling can undo it; an analogue anti-aliasing filter must act before the converter.

1.3 The discrete Fourier transform

Definition 1.4 — DFT and frequency resolution The DFT of x \in \mathbb C^N and its inverse are X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-2i\pi k n / N}, \qquad x[n] = \frac1N \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\, e^{2i\pi k n / N}.

Bin k corresponds to the frequency k f_s / N (bins above N/2 to negative frequencies), so the bin spacing is f_s / N: resolution is bought with observation time N / f_s. The fast Fourier transform (Cooley and Tukey, 1965) computes the DFT in \mathcal O(N \log N) operations.

Theorem 1.5 — Circular convolution theorem Define the circular convolution (x \circledast h)[n] = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} x[m]\, h[(n - m) \bmod N]. Then its DFT is the product of the DFTs: \mathrm{DFT}(x \circledast h)[k] = X[k]\, H[k].

Proof. Substitute and exchange the sums, with \omega = e^{-2i\pi/N}: \sum_n \sum_m x[m]\, h[(n - m) \bmod N]\, \omega^{kn} = \sum_m x[m]\, \omega^{km} \sum_n h[(n - m) \bmod N]\, \omega^{k(n - m)}.

Since \omega^{kN} = 1, the inner sum runs over a full period of h whatever m is, and equals H[k]. The outer sum is X[k]. ∎

Linear (non-circular) convolution is obtained by zero-padding both signals to length at least N_x + N_h - 1: this is how long filters are applied by FFT.

A tone whose frequency falls between two bins spreads its energy over all bins: this is spectral leakage, caused by the implicit rectangular window of the finite observation. Tapering windows trade a wider main lobe for lower side lobes. The highest side lobe sits at about -13 dB for the rectangular window, -31 dB for Hann and -43 dB for Hamming.

The code shows aliasing and designs a low-pass filter (ch. 1.5).

# Code 1.6 — aliasing, and a windowed-sinc low-pass filter
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

fs = 10_000                          # sampling rate (Hz)
t = np.arange(0, 0.1, 1 / fs)        # 1000 samples
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 7_000 * t)    # a 7 kHz tone, above fs / 2
X = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x))
f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x), 1 / fs)
print("apparent frequency:", f[np.argmax(X)], "Hz")

# 101-tap windowed-sinc low-pass filter, cut-off 1 kHz
h = signal.firwin(101, 1_000, fs=fs, window="hamming")
_, H = signal.freqz(h, worN=[500, 1_000, 2_000], fs=fs)
print("gain (dB) at 0.5, 1, 2 kHz:", np.round(20 * np.log10(np.abs(H)), 1))

The output is an apparent frequency of 3000 Hz, and gains of 0, -6 and -65 dB at 0.5, 1 and 2 kHz.

1.4 Linear time-invariant filters

Definition 1.7 — LTI filter, impulse and frequency response A linear time-invariant (LTI) filter acts by convolution with its impulse response h: y[n] = (h * x)[n] = \sum_{m \in \mathbb Z} h[m]\, x[n - m].

Complex exponentials are its eigenvectors: the input e^{i\omega n} produces H(e^{i\omega})\, e^{i\omega n}, where the frequency response is H(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_m h[m]\, e^{-i\omega m}.

A filter is FIR (finite impulse response) when h has finitely many non-zero taps. It is IIR when it is defined by a recursion whose transfer function H(z) = B(z)/A(z) has a non-trivial denominator.

Proposition 1.8 — BIBO stability An LTI filter maps every bounded input to a bounded output if and only if its impulse response is absolutely summable: \sum_m |h[m]| < \infty .

Proof. Sufficiency. If |x[n]| \leq M for all n, then |y[n]| \leq M \sum_m |h[m]|.

Necessity. Take the bounded input x[n] = \overline{h[-n]} / |h[-n]| where h[-n] \neq 0, and 0 elsewhere. Then y[0] = \sum_m h[m]\, x[-m] = \sum_m |h[m]|, which must be finite. ∎

FIR filters are therefore always stable. A causal IIR filter is stable if and only if all the poles of H(z) lie strictly inside the unit circle.

1.5 Designing a FIR filter

The ideal low-pass filter of cut-off f_c has the impulse response h_d[n] = (2 f_c / f_s)\, \mathrm{sinc}(2 f_c n / f_s): infinite and non-causal. The window method keeps M central taps, multiplies them by a tapering window and delays the result by (M-1)/2 samples.

  • Symmetric taps give a linear phase: every frequency is delayed by the same (M-1)/2 samples, so pulse shapes are preserved — essential for radar and for transients.
  • The transition width shrinks like 1/M; the window sets the stop-band attenuation (about -53 dB for Hamming), as Code 1.6 shows.

1.6 The matched filter and pulse compression

A receiver looks for a known waveform s (a radar pulse, a pilot sequence) in white noise. Which linear filter makes the waveform stand out most?

Theorem 1.9 — Matched filter Let y = s + w with s \in \mathbb C^N known and w white noise of power \sigma^2. Among linear filters h, the output SNR at the decision time, \mathrm{SNR}(h) = \frac{|\langle s, h \rangle|^2}{\sigma^2\, \|h\|^2}, is maximised by h = s (up to a constant), that is, by correlating y with the waveform. The maximum is the signal energy over the noise power:

Key result — the matched filter \mathrm{SNR}_{\max} = \frac{\|s\|^2}{\sigma^2}

Proof. The filter output is the sum of a signal term and a noise term: \langle y, h \rangle = \langle s, h \rangle + \langle w, h \rangle .

The noise term has variance \sigma^2 \|h\|^2, since the samples of w are uncorrelated with power \sigma^2.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, |\langle s, h \rangle|^2 \leq \|s\|^2 \|h\|^2, with equality if and only if h is proportional to s. Dividing by \sigma^2 \|h\|^2 gives the bound and the maximiser. ∎

As a convolution, correlating with s is filtering with the time-reversed conjugate h[m] = \overline{s[N - 1 - m]}, hence the name. The SNR gain over one sample of power |s[n]|^2 = 1 is N.

A radar wants both a long pulse (energy, hence range) and a fine range resolution (a short pulse). Pulse compression reconciles them: transmit a long chirp of duration T sweeping a band B, then apply the matched filter. The compressed pulse has a width of about 1/B.

  • Sampled at its bandwidth, the chirp has N = BT samples, so the matched filter gains 10 \log_{10}(BT) dB.
  • The range resolution is c / (2B) instead of cT/2.
  • With T = 50 µs and B = 4 MHz: BT = 200, a gain of 23 dB, and a resolution of 37.5 m instead of 7.5 km.
# Code 1.10 — pulse compression of a linear FM chirp by the matched filter
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
B, T = 4e6, 50e-6                     # 4 MHz sweep, 50 us pulse
fs = B                                # complex baseband sampled at the bandwidth
N = int(round(B * T))                 # N = BT = 200 samples
t = np.arange(N) / fs
s = np.exp(1j * np.pi * (B / T) * (t - T / 2) ** 2)   # linear FM chirp, |s[n]| = 1
y = (rng.normal(size=2000) + 1j * rng.normal(size=2000)) / np.sqrt(2)  # noise, power 1
y[700:700 + N] += 0.25 * s            # echo at -12 dB per sample, delay 700
z = np.abs(np.correlate(y, s, mode="valid"))   # matched filter (s is conjugated)
print("estimated delay:", np.argmax(z))
print("output SNR (dB): %.1f" % (10 * np.log10(z.max() ** 2 / np.mean(z[:600] ** 2))))
print("predicted: -12 + 10 log10(BT) = %.1f dB" % (-12 + 10 * np.log10(B * T)))

The echo, invisible sample by sample, is found at delay 700 with an output SNR of about 10 dB, close to the predicted 11 dB.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Narrowband signals are processed as complex IQ samples of their envelope, at a rate set by the bandwidth, not the carrier.
  • Sampling at f_s is lossless for spectra inside (-f_s/2, f_s/2); outside, aliasing is irreversible.
  • The DFT has a bin spacing of f_s/N; windows trade main-lobe width for side-lobe level.
  • LTI filters are convolutions, diagonalised by complex exponentials; FIR filters are stable and can have linear phase.
  • The matched filter maximises the SNR by Cauchy–Schwarz; pulse compression turns BT into a 10 \log_{10}(BT) dB gain.

2. Time–frequency and time–scale representations

The DFT of a whole recording says which frequencies are present, not when. Speech, chirps, radar pulses and bearing impacts are non-stationary, so we need representations indexed by time and frequency. This chapter builds them, proves the limit on their resolution, and derives the speech features used in chapters 7 and 8.

2.1 Short-time Fourier transform and spectrogram

Definition 2.1 — STFT and spectrogram Let g be a window of length L (Hann, Hamming…), H a hop size and N \geq L a DFT size. The short-time Fourier transform of x is S_x[m, k] = \sum_{n} x[n]\, g[n - mH]\, e^{-2i\pi k (n - mH)/N}, the DFT of the m-th windowed frame. The spectrogram is |S_x[m, k]|^2, an energy density on the time–frequency plane.

The STFT is redundant when H < L, and it can be inverted.

Proposition 2.2 — Overlap-add reconstruction Suppose the shifted windows sum to a constant (the COLA condition): \sum_{m} g[n - mH] = C \neq 0 \quad \text{for all } n . Then x is recovered from its STFT by inverting each frame’s DFT and adding the frames: x[n] = \frac1C \sum_m x[n]\, g[n - mH].

Proof. Since N \geq L, the inverse DFT of S_x[m, \cdot] returns the windowed frame x[n]\, g[n - mH] exactly. Summing over m and using the COLA condition gives C\, x[n]. ∎

The periodic Hann window with H = L/2 satisfies COLA with C = 1, which is why “Hann, 50 % overlap” is the default setting.

2.2 The uncertainty principle

Short windows localise events in time but blur frequencies; long windows do the opposite. The trade-off is a theorem, not a weakness of the STFT.

Theorem 2.3 — Heisenberg–Gabor uncertainty Let g \in L^2(\mathbb R) with \|g\| = 1, centred in time and frequency. Define its spreads \sigma_t^2 = \int t^2 |g(t)|^2\, dt, \qquad \sigma_f^2 = \int f^2 |\hat g(f)|^2\, df . Then their product is bounded below, with equality exactly for Gaussian windows (up to translation and modulation):

Key result — time–frequency uncertainty \sigma_t\, \sigma_f \geq \frac{1}{4\pi}

Proof. Sketch for smooth, fast-decaying g. Integrate by parts: 1 = \int |g|^2\, dt = -\int t\, \frac{d}{dt}|g(t)|^2\, dt = -2\, \mathrm{Re} \int t\, g(t)\, \overline{g'(t)}\, dt .

By Cauchy–Schwarz, the right-hand side is at most 2\, \|t g\|\, \|g'\|. By Plancherel, g' has transform 2i\pi f \hat g(f), so \|g'\| = 2\pi \sigma_f, while \|t g\| = \sigma_t. Hence 1 \leq 4\pi \sigma_t \sigma_f.

Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz forces g' = -c\, t\, g with c > 0, whose solutions are the Gaussians. ∎

A spectrogram cell therefore cannot be smaller than a fixed area. Gabor (1946) proposed to tile the plane with Gaussian-windowed atoms for this reason.

Example 2.4 — Choosing the window For speech at f_s = 16 kHz:

  • a window of 32 ms (512 samples) gives a bin spacing of 16000 / 512 = 31.25 Hz, enough to resolve the harmonics of a 120 Hz voice;
  • a window of 8 ms (128 samples) gives 125 Hz bins, which merge harmonics but localise a click to a few milliseconds.

Code 2.9 measures this trade-off.

2.3 Wavelets and the scalogram

The STFT analyses all frequencies with the same window. Physical signals often call for a constant relative resolution instead: a 50 Hz hum needs fine frequency resolution, a 5 kHz impact needs fine time resolution.

Definition 2.5 — Continuous wavelet transform, scalogram A wavelet \psi \in L^2(\mathbb R) is admissible when C_\psi = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{|\hat\psi(f)|^2}{|f|}\, df < \infty, which forces \hat \psi(0) = 0: a wavelet has zero mean. The continuous wavelet transform at scale a > 0 and time b is W_x(a, b) = \int x(t)\, \frac{1}{\sqrt a}\, \overline{\psi\Big(\frac{t - b}{a}\Big)}\, dt, and the scalogram is |W_x(a, b)|^2.

  • If \psi has centre frequency f_0 and bandwidth \Delta f, the wavelet dilated by a has centre f_0 / a and bandwidth \Delta f / a. The relative bandwidth is constant: a constant-Q analysis.
  • The Morlet wavelet, a Gaussian-windowed complex exponential, is the usual choice for time–frequency maps: \psi(t) = \pi^{-1/4}\, e^{i \omega_0 t}\, e^{-t^2/2}, \qquad \omega_0 \approx 6 .
  • When C_\psi < \infty, x can be reconstructed from W_x (Calderón’s formula; see Mallat’s book in the References).

2.4 Discrete wavelets and filter banks

Sampling scales on a dyadic grid a = 2^j leads to a fast, non-redundant transform implemented by a cascade of two-channel filter banks.

Proposition 2.6 — The Haar filter bank is orthogonal For x \in \mathbb R^{2K}, define the approximation and detail coefficients a_k = \frac{x_{2k} + x_{2k+1}}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad d_k = \frac{x_{2k} - x_{2k+1}}{\sqrt 2}. The map x \mapsto (a, d) is orthogonal: it preserves energy and is inverted by its transpose.

Proof. The map acts independently on each pair (x_{2k}, x_{2k+1}) through the matrix \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, whose rows are orthonormal. A block-diagonal matrix with orthogonal blocks is orthogonal. ∎

Iterating on the approximation a gives the discrete wavelet transform. Its cost is N + N/2 + N/4 + \dots < 2N operations, linear in N, better than the FFT.

Daubechies wavelets use longer filters, so that smooth pieces of a signal give small details; soft thresholding of the details at \sigma \sqrt{2 \log N} then denoises (Donoho and Johnstone, 1994).

2.5 Perceptual features: mel spectrum, cepstrum and MFCC

Speech and audio systems compress the spectrogram along a perceptual frequency axis and separate the source from the filter.

Definition 2.7 — Mel scale and MFCC The mel scale maps a frequency in hertz to a perceived pitch: m(f) = 2595 \log_{10}\Big(1 + \frac{f}{700}\Big).

The MFCC of a frame (Davis and Mermelstein, 1980) are computed in four steps:

  1. the power spectrum of the windowed frame;
  2. energies in 20–40 triangular filters equally spaced in mel;
  3. the logarithm of each energy;
  4. a discrete cosine transform, keeping the first 12 or 13 coefficients.

The logarithm in step 3 is not cosmetic.

Proposition 2.8 — The cepstrum turns convolution into a sum If s = e * h, then at every frequency where the spectra do not vanish: \log |S(f)| = \log |E(f)| + \log |H(f)|. Any linear map of the log-spectrum, such as the DCT of the MFCC or the inverse Fourier transform of the real cepstrum, separates the two contributions additively.

Proof. The Fourier transform of a convolution is the product S = E \cdot H, and the logarithm of a product of moduli is the sum of the logarithms. ∎

For voiced speech, e is a pulse train at the pitch F_0 and h the smooth vocal-tract response (ch. 7.2). In the cepstrum, h occupies the low quefrencies and e a peak at the pitch period. A pitch of 125 Hz gives a peak at 8 ms, that is 128 samples at 16 kHz.

Example 2.9 — Spectrogram versus scalogram on a chirp with a click The code compares two Hann windows on a linear chirp from 100 Hz to 3 kHz with a 1 ms click at t = 1 s, then computes a Morlet scalogram in the Fourier domain.

# Code 2.9 — the time-frequency trade-off, and a Morlet scalogram
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal

fs = 8_000
t = np.arange(2 * fs) / fs                       # 2 s
x = signal.chirp(t, f0=100, t1=2, f1=3_000)      # linear chirp 100 Hz -> 3 kHz
x[8_000:8_008] += 5.0                            # a 1 ms click at t = 1 s

for L in (64, 1024):                             # short and long Hann windows
    f, tt, S = signal.stft(x, fs, window="hann", nperseg=L, noverlap=L // 2)
    P = np.abs(S) ** 2
    k = np.argmin(np.abs(tt - 0.5))              # frame closest to t = 0.5 s
    width = np.sum(P[:, k] > 0.5 * P[:, k].max()) * fs / L
    hf = P[f > 3_500].sum(axis=0)                # energy above the chirp band
    spread = np.sum(hf > 0.1 * hf.max()) * (L / 2) / fs * 1e3
    print(f"L={L:4d}: ridge {f[np.argmax(P[:, k])]:6.1f} Hz, -3 dB width {width:5.1f} Hz,"
          f" click spread over {spread:5.1f} ms")

def morlet_cwt(x, fs, freqs, w0=6.0):
    """CWT with an analytic Morlet wavelet, computed in the Fourier domain."""
    X = np.fft.fft(x)
    om = 2 * np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(len(x), 1 / fs)    # angular frequencies
    W = np.empty((len(freqs), len(x)), complex)
    for i, fc in enumerate(freqs):
        a = w0 / (2 * np.pi * fc)                # scale whose centre frequency is fc
        psi_hat = np.pi ** -0.25 * np.exp(-0.5 * (a * om - w0) ** 2) * (om > 0)
        W[i] = np.fft.ifft(X * np.sqrt(a) * psi_hat)
    return W

freqs = np.geomspace(100, 3_500, 48)             # constant-Q grid of centre frequencies
W = np.abs(morlet_cwt(x, fs, freqs)) ** 2
print("scalogram ridge at t = 0.5 s: %.0f Hz (true 825 Hz)" % freqs[np.argmax(W[:, 4_000])])

With 64 samples, the ridge is 250 Hz wide and the click spreads over 4 ms. With 1 024 samples, the ridge is 70 Hz wide — the chirp moves by 185 Hz within one window — and the click spreads over 128 ms. The scalogram ridge lies at 832 Hz for a true 825 Hz.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The spectrogram is the squared modulus of windowed DFTs; with a COLA window the STFT is exactly invertible.
  • The uncertainty principle bounds the product of time and frequency spreads by 1/(4\pi), reached by Gaussian windows.
  • Wavelets give a constant-Q analysis: fine time resolution at high frequency, fine frequency resolution at low frequency.
  • The discrete wavelet transform is an orthogonal filter bank computed in linear time.
  • MFCC = mel energies, logarithm, DCT; the logarithm turns the source–filter convolution into a sum.

3. The machine-learning toolbox: classification, clustering, optimisation

Before deep networks, a signal is usually summarised by a few hand-crafted features — energy, kurtosis, spectral centroid — and fed to a standard learner. This chapter states what such a learner estimates, reviews the main families of classifiers and clustering algorithms, and proves the convergence rate of gradient descent, the engine of everything in chapter 4.

3.1 Supervised learning and the Bayes rule

Data are pairs (x_i, y_i), drawn independently from an unknown distribution P on features x and labels y \in \{1, \dots, K\}. A classifier f is judged by its risk R(f) = P(f(x) \neq y), and learning minimises an empirical estimate of it over a class of functions.

Theorem 3.1 — The Bayes classifier The classifier that picks the most probable label given the observation, f^\star(x) = \arg\max_{k}\ P(y = k \mid x), has the smallest risk among all classifiers.

Proof. For any f, condition on x: the probability of error given x is 1 - P(y = f(x) \mid x).

It is minimised, for each observation separately, by choosing the label with the largest conditional probability. Integrating over x preserves the inequality. ∎

With two classes, f^\star compares the likelihood ratio p(x \mid 1) / p(x \mid 0) with the threshold P(y = 0) / P(y = 1): the detection tests of ch. 5 are Bayes rules with a threshold set differently.

Unsupervised learning has no labels: it looks for structure — clusters, low-dimensional subspaces, densities — in the x_i alone. Industrial data are often unlabelled or labelled only for rare events, so both settings appear in every use case.

Note 3.2 — Splitting signal data without leakage Consecutive windows of one recording are strongly correlated. If windows of the same recording, speaker, device or machine land in both the training and the test set, the test score measures memorisation of that source, not generalisation.

  • Split by source: recording, session, speaker, emitter, machine or day.
  • Fit normalisations (means, variances, PCA) on the training set only.
  • Keep a final test set untouched until the end.

3.2 The main families of classifiers

Definition 3.3 — Linear classifiers: logistic regression and SVM Both predict with the sign of a score w^\top x + b (one score per class for K > 2) and differ by their loss.

  • Logistic regression models P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b), with \sigma(u) = 1/(1 + e^{-u}), and minimises the convex cross-entropy loss.
  • The support vector machine minimises the hinge loss plus \lambda \|w\|^2, which maximises the margin between classes. With a kernel k(x, x'), such as the Gaussian kernel, it separates classes with non-linear boundaries.
Family Strength Weakness
Linear models fast, calibrated, interpretable linear boundaries only
Kernel SVM strong on small data sets quadratic cost in the sample size
k-nearest neighbours no training; asymptotic error at most twice the Bayes error (Cover and Hart, 1967) needs a good distance
Random forests, gradient boosting robust to scales and heterogeneous indicators blind to time and frequency structure

3.3 Unsupervised learning: clustering

Clustering partitions n points into groups. Three algorithms cover most industrial needs.

Proposition 3.4 — Lloyd’s algorithm for k-means The k-means objective of an assignment c and centres \mu_1, \dots, \mu_K is J(c, \mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - \mu_{c(i)}\|^2 . Lloyd’s algorithm alternates two steps: assign each point to its nearest centre, then move each centre to the mean of its points. J never increases, and the algorithm stops after finitely many iterations.

Proof. The assignment step minimises J over c for fixed \mu, since each term is minimised separately. The update step minimises J over the centres for a fixed assignment, since the mean minimises a sum of squared distances. Each step can only lower the objective.

There are finitely many assignments, and an assignment cannot repeat once J strictly decreases (ties broken consistently), so the algorithm terminates. ∎

The limit is a local minimum only: practice restarts from several initialisations (k-means++). k-means assumes round clusters of similar sizes and a known K.

Definition 3.5 — Gaussian mixtures and the EM algorithm A Gaussian mixture has density p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\, \mathcal N(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k).

EM (Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977) alternates an E-step, computing the responsibilities r_{ik} = P(k \mid x_i), and an M-step, re-estimating the weights, means and covariances with these responsibilities. No iteration decreases the log-likelihood: the E-step makes a lower bound (Jensen’s inequality) tight and the M-step maximises it. Mixtures model MFCC frames in ch. 7.3.

Definition 3.6 — DBSCAN Given a radius \varepsilon and an integer m, a point is a core point if at least m points lie within distance \varepsilon of it. Clusters are the sets of points reachable from one another through chains of core points; points reachable from no core point are labelled noise.

DBSCAN (Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu, 1996) needs no number of clusters, finds any shape and isolates outliers, as radar deinterleaving requires (ch. 6.3). Standardise the features first: it has a single density scale.

Hierarchical clustering (Ward’s criterion) and principal component analysis complete the toolbox.

3.4 Evaluation

Definition 3.7 — Detection and clustering metrics

  • For a binary detector with a threshold, the probability of false alarm P_{FA} is the rate of positives on negative examples, and the probability of detection P_D (recall) is the rate of positives on positive examples.
  • The ROC curve traces P_D against P_{FA} as the threshold varies; its area (AUC) is the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative.
  • The equal error rate (EER) is the error where false acceptances and false rejections are equal, the standard metric of speaker verification (ch. 7.1).
  • Precision is the fraction of true positives among detections; F1 is its harmonic mean with recall.
  • For clustering, the adjusted Rand index (ARI) measures the agreement of two partitions, corrected for chance: 1 for identical partitions, about 0 for random ones.

Example 3.8 — Accuracy under class imbalance A fleet of pumps produces 10 000 windows, of which 100 precede a failure.

  • The classifier “always healthy” has an accuracy of 99 % and a recall of 0.
  • A detector that flags 150 windows, 80 of them before failures, has a recall of 0.80 and a precision of 0.53.
  • Its F1 score is F_1 = \frac{2 \times 0.53 \times 0.80}{0.53 + 0.80} \approx 0.64 .

Predictive maintenance (ch. 9) is always judged on recall, precision and the cost of each error, never on accuracy.

3.5 Optimisation and gradient descent

Training minimises an empirical loss F(\theta). Gradient descent iterates \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \eta\, \nabla F(\theta_k) with a step size \eta.

Theorem 3.9 — Rate of gradient descent on smooth convex functions Let F be convex with an L-Lipschitz gradient, and let \theta^\star be a minimiser. With the step \eta = 1/L, the iterates satisfy, for every k \geq 1: F(\theta_k) - F(\theta^\star) \leq \frac{L\, \|\theta_0 - \theta^\star\|^2}{2k}.

Proof. Write g_k = \nabla F(\theta_k).

  1. Descent lemma. The Lipschitz gradient gives F(\theta_{k+1}) \leq F(\theta_k) - \|g_k\|^2 / (2L): the values decrease.
  2. Convexity. The gap is bounded by a linear term: F(\theta_k) - F(\theta^\star) \leq \langle g_k, \theta_k - \theta^\star \rangle .
  3. Expanding the square of \|\theta_{k+1} - \theta^\star\|, where \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - g_k / L: \langle g_k, \theta_k - \theta^\star \rangle - \frac{\|g_k\|^2}{2L} = \frac{L}{2}\big(\|\theta_k - \theta^\star\|^2 - \|\theta_{k+1} - \theta^\star\|^2\big).
  4. Combining 1–3, F(\theta_{k+1}) - F(\theta^\star) is bounded by the right-hand side of step 3. Summing from 0 to k - 1, the sum telescopes and is at most L \|\theta_0 - \theta^\star\|^2 / 2.
  5. The values decrease (step 1), so k times the last gap is at most this sum. ∎

The rate 1/k is slow; strong convexity gives a geometric rate, and Nesterov’s acceleration gives 1/k^2 (see Convex Optimization and Applications in Machine Learning).

Stochastic gradient descent replaces \nabla F by an unbiased estimate on a random mini-batch; momentum and Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015) rescale the steps. On the non-convex losses of deep networks, guarantees only concern stationary points.

The code runs the classical chain on three kinds of noisy synthetic signals: tones, square waves and sparse impulses.

# Code 3.10 — hand-crafted features, two classifiers and k-means
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, adjusted_rand_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fs, n = 1_000, 512

def make(label):
    t = np.arange(n) / fs
    f0 = rng.uniform(20, 120)
    if label == 0:                                   # tone
        x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f0 * t + rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi))
    elif label == 1:                                 # square wave (odd harmonics)
        x = np.sign(np.sin(2 * np.pi * f0 * t))
    else:                                            # sparse impulses (a "fault")
        x = (rng.random(n) < 0.02) * rng.normal(0, 3, n)
    return x + rng.normal(0, 1.5, n)

def features(x):
    P = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x)) ** 2
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1 / fs)
    return [np.sqrt(np.mean(x ** 2)),               # RMS
            stats.kurtosis(x, fisher=False),        # kurtosis (3 for a Gaussian)
            (f * P).sum() / P.sum(),                # spectral centroid
            np.mean(np.diff(np.sign(x)) != 0)]      # zero-crossing rate

y = rng.integers(0, 3, 900)
F = np.array([features(make(c)) for c in y])
Ftr, Fte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(F, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)
scaler = StandardScaler().fit(Ftr)                  # fitted on the training set only
Ftr, Fte = scaler.transform(Ftr), scaler.transform(Fte)
for clf in (LogisticRegression(max_iter=1_000), RandomForestClassifier(random_state=0)):
    acc = accuracy_score(yte, clf.fit(Ftr, ytr).predict(Fte))
    print(f"{type(clf).__name__:24s} test accuracy {acc:.3f}")
km = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(scaler.transform(F))
print("k-means ARI (no labels used):", round(adjusted_rand_score(y, km.labels_), 3))

Expected output: test accuracies of 0.96 (logistic regression) and 0.93 (random forest), and an ARI of 0.75 for k-means. Chapter 4 asks whether a network finds such features alone.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • The Bayes classifier maximises the posterior probability; with two classes it is a likelihood-ratio test.
  • Split signal data by source (recording, speaker, device, machine), never by window.
  • Linear models, kernels, nearest neighbours and tree ensembles are strong baselines on hand-crafted features.
  • k-means decreases its objective monotonically; EM does the same for the likelihood of a mixture; DBSCAN finds an unknown number of clusters and flags noise.
  • Judge detectors with ROC, EER, precision and recall, not accuracy.
  • Gradient descent with step 1/L converges at rate 1/k on smooth convex losses.

4. Deep learning: basics and main architectures

A deep network learns the features and the classifier together, from raw samples or spectrograms. This chapter defines the building blocks, shows that a convolutional layer is a learnable filter bank, explains why recurrent networks forget, and ends with a first, proven answer to the course’s question: what does depth add?

4.1 Multilayer perceptrons

Definition 4.1 — Multilayer perceptron A multilayer perceptron (MLP) of depth L alternates affine maps and a pointwise non-linearity \sigma (ReLU \sigma(u) = \max(u, 0), tanh…): h_0 = x, \qquad z_\ell = W_\ell\, h_{\ell - 1} + b_\ell, \qquad h_\ell = \sigma(z_\ell) \quad (\ell < L), and the last pre-activation z_L gives the output. For classification, a softmax turns z_L into probabilities p_k = e^{z_{L,k}} / \sum_j e^{z_{L,j}}, trained with the cross-entropy loss -\log p_y.

Theorem 4.2 — Universal approximation Let \sigma be continuous and not a polynomial. For every continuous function f on a compact set K \subset \mathbb R^d and every \varepsilon > 0, there is a network with one hidden layer such that \sup_{x \in K} |f(x) - g(x)| \leq \varepsilon .

Proofs: Cybenko (1989) for sigmoids, Hornik (1991), and Leshno, Lin, Pinkus and Schocken (1993) for every non-polynomial activation.

The theorem says nothing about the number of hidden units needed. Section 4.7 shows that this number can be exponentially larger for a shallow network than for a deep one.

4.2 Backpropagation

Proposition 4.3 — Backpropagation for an MLP For one example with label y and loss \ell = -\log p_y, define \delta_\ell = \partial \ell / \partial z_\ell. Then, with e_y the one-hot vector of y: \delta_L = p - e_y, \qquad \delta_\ell = \big(W_{\ell+1}^\top\, \delta_{\ell+1}\big) \odot \sigma'(z_\ell), \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial W_\ell} = \delta_\ell\, h_{\ell-1}^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b_\ell} = \delta_\ell . All gradients cost about twice the forward pass.

Proof. Output. Since \ell = -z_{L,y} + \log \sum_j e^{z_{L,j}}, its derivative in z_{L,j} is -\mathbb 1[j = y] + p_j.

Recursion. z_{\ell+1} depends on z_\ell through W_{\ell+1} \sigma(z_\ell), whose Jacobian is W_{\ell+1}\, \mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(z_\ell)). The chain rule (Definition 0.5) transposes it.

Weights. z_\ell is linear in W_\ell with coefficient h_{\ell-1}.

Cost. Each backward step is one product by a transposed matrix and one pointwise product — the same sizes as the forward step. ∎

Automatic differentiation in PyTorch applies the same reverse-mode rule to any composition of differentiable operations (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986, popularised it for networks).

4.3 Convolutional networks: learnable filter banks

Definition 4.4 — One-dimensional convolutional layer A layer with C input channels, C' output channels, kernel size K and dilation d computes, for each output channel c: y_c[n] = \sigma\Big(b_c + \sum_{c'=1}^{C} \sum_{m=0}^{K-1} w_{c,c'}[m]\, x_{c'}[n + m d]\Big). Each output channel is a sum of FIR filters (Definition 1.7) followed by a non-linearity. The number of parameters, C C' K + C', does not depend on the length of the signal.

Proposition 4.5 — Equivariance, invariance and receptive field Let T_\tau shift a signal, (T_\tau x)[n] = x[n - \tau]. Ignoring borders:

  1. a stride-1 convolutional layer commutes with shifts: \mathrm{Layer}(T_\tau x) = T_\tau\, \mathrm{Layer}(x);
  2. a stack of such layers followed by a global average over time is shift-invariant;
  3. a stack of L layers with kernel size K and dilations d_1, \dots, d_L sees a window of input samples of length R = 1 + (K - 1) \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} d_\ell .

Proof.

  1. Replace x by T_\tau x in Definition 4.4: the inner sum becomes the original one evaluated at n - \tau. The pointwise non-linearity commutes with shifts.
  2. Compositions of shift-commuting maps commute with shifts, and the average over all n of a shifted sequence equals the average of the sequence.
  3. One layer makes each output depend on the inputs n, n + d, \dots, n + (K-1)d: the window grows by (K - 1) d_\ell samples per layer. ∎

Strides and pooling keep equivariance only for shifts that are multiples of the stride. Doubling dilations make the receptive field grow exponentially with depth (WaveNet, van den Oord et al., 2016): with K = 3 and dilations 1, 2, 4, \dots, 512 (10 layers), R = 1 + 2 \times 1023 = 2047 samples.

On spectrograms, 2D convolutions treat the time–frequency plane as an image.

4.4 Recurrent networks and long dependencies

A recurrent network reads a sequence x_1, \dots, x_T and updates a state h_t = \tanh(W h_{t-1} + U x_t + b). Training unrolls it in time and backpropagates through the unrolled graph.

Proposition 4.6 — Vanishing gradients If the spectral norm of the recurrent matrix satisfies \|W\|_2 \leq \gamma < 1, then the gradient of the state at time T with respect to the state at an earlier time t decays geometrically: \Big\| \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} \Big\|_2 \leq \gamma^{\,T - t}.

Proof. By the chain rule, the Jacobian is the product of T - t factors \mathrm{diag}(\tanh'(a_s))\, W, where a_s is the pre-activation. Each factor has norm at most \gamma because |\tanh'| \leq 1, and operator norms are sub-multiplicative. ∎

With \|W\|_2 > 1 the product can instead explode. The LSTM (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) adds a cell state updated additively, c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde c_t, whose Jacobian along the cell path is the learned forget gate \mathrm{diag}(f_t): the network decides how long to remember.

4.5 Autoencoders, attention and transformers

Definition 4.7 — Autoencoder and denoising autoencoder An autoencoder is an encoder E and a decoder D trained to minimise \|x - D(E(x))\|^2 through a narrow code E(x). A denoising autoencoder (Vincent, Larochelle, Bengio and Manzagol, 2008) is trained to output the clean x from a corrupted input, minimising \|x - D(E(\tilde x))\|^2.

A linear autoencoder spans the principal subspace of PCA (Baldi and Hornik, 1989). Autoencoders serve for restoration (ch. 8.4) and anomaly detection (ch. 9.4).

Definition 4.8 — Self-attention Let X \in \mathbb R^{T \times d} hold T tokens (audio frames, radar pulses) as rows. With learned matrices, set Q = X W_Q, K = X W_K and V = X W_V. Self-attention outputs \mathrm{Attn}(X) = \mathrm{softmax}\Big(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\, V, where the softmax acts on each row. A transformer block (Vaswani et al., 2017) chains several attention heads and an MLP, each wrapped in a residual connection and a normalisation.

Proposition 4.9 — Attention ignores order For every permutation matrix \Pi: \mathrm{Attn}(\Pi X) = \Pi\, \mathrm{Attn}(X).

Proof. Permuting the rows of X permutes the rows of the queries, keys and values, so the score matrix becomes \Pi Q K^\top \Pi^\top. A row-wise softmax commutes with a simultaneous permutation of rows and columns. Multiplying by the permuted values \Pi V cancels the factor \Pi^\top \Pi = I. ∎

Order must therefore be injected, through positional encodings or, for radar pulses, through the times of arrival themselves.

4.6 Training in practice

Note 4.10 — A training recipe for signals

  • Architecture: residual connections (He et al., 2016) and batch normalisation make deep stacks trainable.
  • Optimisation: Adam, a learning-rate schedule, early stopping on a validation set split by source (Note 3.2).
  • Augmentation that respects the physics: time shifts, gains, noise at random SNR, carrier offsets, time and frequency masking (SpecAugment, Park et al., 2019).
  • Diagnosis: always compare with the classical chain of the same problem.

The code below trains a small 1D CNN on the raw signals of Code 3.10, with no hand-crafted features.

# Code 4.11 — a 1D CNN learns its own filter bank on raw signals
import numpy as np
import torch
from torch import nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, fs = 512, 1_000

def batch(m):
    """Tone / square wave / sparse impulses in strong noise, random phase and pitch."""
    y = rng.integers(0, 3, m)
    t = np.arange(n) / fs
    f0 = rng.uniform(20, 120, (m, 1))
    ph = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, (m, 1))
    tone = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f0 * t + ph)
    square = np.sign(tone)
    imp = (rng.random((m, n)) < 0.02) * rng.normal(0, 3, (m, n))
    x = np.choose(y[:, None], [tone, square, imp]) + rng.normal(0, 1.5, (m, n))
    return torch.tensor(x[:, None, :], dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(y)

net = nn.Sequential(                       # a learnable filter bank, then invariance
    nn.Conv1d(1, 16, kernel_size=33, padding=16), nn.BatchNorm1d(16), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool1d(4),
    nn.Conv1d(16, 32, kernel_size=9, padding=4), nn.BatchNorm1d(32), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1), nn.Flatten(),  # global average pooling: shift invariance
    nn.Linear(32, 3))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for step in range(250):
    x, y = batch(64)
    loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(net(x), y)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
x, y = batch(1_000)
net.eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    acc = (net(x).argmax(1) == y).float().mean().item()
print(f"test accuracy {acc:.3f}, parameters {sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters())}")

With 5 400 parameters and 16 000 training signals, the network reaches a test accuracy of 0.91, below the 0.96 of the four features of Code 3.10: it had to discover its detectors from data alone. Exercise 8 asks whether longer training closes the gap.

4.7 What makes deep learning deep? A first answer

The universal approximation theorem allows one hidden layer; the question is how many units it needs. The following example, due to Telgarsky (2016), shows a function that a narrow deep network computes exactly and that every shallow network of sub-exponential width misses.

Lemma 4.12 — Shallow ReLU networks have few pieces A one-hidden-layer ReLU network with m units, from \mathbb R to \mathbb R, is continuous and piecewise affine with at most m + 1 pieces.

Proof. It is a constant plus a sum of terms a_j \max(w_j x + b_j, 0). Each term is affine except at the single point -b_j / w_j, so the sum has at most m breakpoints. ∎

Theorem 4.13 — Depth separation (after Telgarsky, 2016) Let \tau^k be the k-fold composition of the tent map on [0, 1], written with two ReLU units: \tau(x) = 2\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x) - 4\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - 1/2).

  1. \tau^k is computed exactly by a ReLU network of depth k with 2 units per layer, and it has 2^k affine pieces.
  2. Every continuous piecewise affine g with at most p pieces on [0, 1] satisfies \int_0^1 |\tau^k(x) - g(x)|\, dx \geq \frac18 - \frac{p + 1}{2^{k+2}} .
  3. Hence a one-hidden-layer ReLU network with at most 2^{k-2} - 2 units has an L^1 error of at least 1/16.

Proof. Sketch, in five steps.

  1. The tent map sends the unit interval onto itself with two affine pieces. By induction, each of the 2^{k-1} pieces of \tau^{k-1} maps its interval affinely onto the unit interval, and \tau splits it in two. This gives 2^k pieces, forming 2^{k-1} teeth.
  2. The graph of \tau^k crosses the level 1/2 twice per tooth. Between consecutive crossings lie 2^k - 1 triangles of base 2^{-k} and height 1/2, alternately above and below the level, each of area 2^{-k-2}.
  3. Since g is continuous with p affine pieces, g - 1/2 changes sign at most p times. At most p triangle bases contain a sign change. The others form at most p + 1 runs of consecutive triangles on which this sign is constant.
  4. Triangles alternate, so in a run of r triangles at least \lfloor r/2 \rfloor lie on the wrong side of 1/2 relative to g. There, |\tau^k - g| \geq |\tau^k - 1/2|, which integrates to the triangle’s area.
  5. Summing over runs, with \lfloor r/2 \rfloor \geq (r - 1)/2, at least 2^{k-1} - 1 - p triangles are wrong. Multiplying by 2^{-k-2} gives item 2; Lemma 4.12 with p \leq m + 1 gives item 3. ∎

Key result — depth buys exponentially many linear regions \text{depth } k,\ 2k \text{ units} \ \longrightarrow\ 2^k \text{ pieces}, \qquad \text{depth } 2 \ \longrightarrow\ \text{width} \geq 2^{k-2} - 1 \text{ to approximate} 

# Code 4.14 — counting the linear pieces of a deep, narrow ReLU network
import numpy as np

relu = lambda z: np.maximum(z, 0.0)
tent = lambda z: 2 * relu(z) - 4 * relu(z - 0.5)      # one layer with two ReLU units
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 2 ** 12 + 1)
y = x.copy()
for k in range(1, 7):
    y = tent(y)                                       # depth k, width 2
    slopes = np.diff(y) / np.diff(x)
    pieces = 1 + np.count_nonzero(np.abs(np.diff(slopes)) > 1e-6)
    print(f"depth {k}: {pieces:3d} linear pieces with {2 * k:2d} ReLU units;"
          f" an exact one-hidden-layer copy needs at least {pieces - 1} units")

At depth 6, twelve units produce 64 pieces; an exact shallow copy needs 63 units. Composition reuses the same folds at every scale. Chapter 10 completes this answer with invariance and stability.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • An MLP alternates affine maps and non-linearities; one hidden layer approximates any continuous function, at an unspecified width.
  • Backpropagation is the chain rule in reverse order; its cost is a small multiple of the forward pass.
  • A convolutional layer is a learnable FIR filter bank; it is shift-equivariant, and global pooling makes it shift-invariant.
  • Plain recurrent networks forget geometrically; LSTM gates create an additive memory path.
  • Attention is permutation-equivariant: order must be injected.
  • Depth can be exponentially more efficient than width: \tau^k needs 2k units deep and about 2^{k-2} units shallow.

5. Use case: signal detection for cognitive radio

A cognitive radio (Mitola and Maguire, 1999; Haykin, 2005) transmits in licensed bands when, and only when, their owners are silent. It must decide from a few milliseconds of samples whether a band is occupied — a detection problem at negative SNR. This chapter solves it optimally under a physical model, finds the limit of that solution, and asks what learning adds.

5.1 Operational context and physical model

A secondary user senses a licensed band, transmits in a spectrum hole, and leaves when the primary user returns; IEEE 802.22, for instance, standardises such access in television bands. Sensing must work below 0 dB, because the secondary receiver may sit in a fade while a nearby primary receiver still needs protection (the hidden-terminal problem).

Definition 5.1 — Spectrum sensing as a binary test From N complex baseband samples, decide between \mathcal H_0 : y[n] = w[n], \qquad \mathcal H_1 : y[n] = h\, s[n] + w[n], where w is white Gaussian noise of power \sigma^2, s the primary signal and h the channel gain. The per-sample SNR is \gamma = |h|^2 P_s / \sigma^2.

A detector is judged by its false-alarm probability P_{FA}, which measures lost opportunities, and its detection probability P_D, which measures the protection of the primary user.

5.2 The Neyman–Pearson test

Theorem 5.2 — Neyman–Pearson lemma Let p_0, p_1 be the densities of y under the two hypotheses. Consider the likelihood-ratio test (LRT) that decides \mathcal H_1 when p_1(y) > \eta\, p_0(y), with \eta chosen so that its false-alarm probability equals \alpha. Among all tests with P_{FA} \leq \alpha, the LRT has the largest P_D.

Proof. Let \phi^\star be the indicator of the LRT and \phi any test with P_{FA}(\phi) \leq \alpha. The integrand below is non-negative everywhere, because \phi^\star = 1 exactly where p_1 - \eta p_0 > 0: \int (\phi^\star - \phi)(p_1 - \eta\, p_0)\, dy \geq 0 .

Expanding the integral gives P_D(\phi^\star) - P_D(\phi) \geq \eta\, \big(\alpha - P_{FA}(\phi)\big) \geq 0, which is the claim. ∎

Two consequences shape the chapter. For a known signal, the log-ratio is linear in \mathrm{Re}\langle y, s \rangle: the LRT is the matched filter of Theorem 1.9. For an unknown Gaussian signal, s[n] \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, P_s), both densities depend on y only through \|y\|^2, and the ratio increases with it: the LRT is the energy detector.

5.3 The energy detector and its SNR wall

Proposition 5.3 — Energy detector (Urkowitz, 1967) Let T = \sum_{n=1}^N |y[n]|^2, with a Gaussian signal, a known noise power and many samples. By the central limit theorem, T is nearly Gaussian, with mean N\sigma^2 and standard deviation \sqrt N \sigma^2 under \mathcal H_0.

Under \mathcal H_1 both are multiplied by 1 + \gamma.

With Q the Gaussian tail function, the threshold and the detection probability are \lambda = \sigma^2 \big(N + \sqrt N\, Q^{-1}(P_{FA})\big), \qquad P_D = Q\Big(\frac{Q^{-1}(P_{FA}) - \sqrt N\, \gamma}{1 + \gamma}\Big). Reaching a target P_D requires a number of samples that grows like 1/\gamma^2:

Key result — sample size of the energy detector N = \Big(\frac{Q^{-1}(P_{FA}) - (1 + \gamma)\, Q^{-1}(P_D)}{\gamma}\Big)^2

Proof. Proposition 0.4 gives the exact law 2T/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{2N} under \mathcal H_0, with the moments above. Under \mathcal H_1 the samples are \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma^2(1 + \gamma)), so the same law holds with the power \sigma^2 (1 + \gamma).

Normalising T, solving P(T > \lambda) = P_{FA} under \mathcal H_0, then computing P(T > \lambda) under \mathcal H_1 gives the formulas. ∎

Example 5.4 — Sensing budget Target P_{FA} = 0.01 and P_D = 0.9, so that Q^{-1}(P_{FA}) = 2.326 and Q^{-1}(P_D) = -1.282.

  • At \gamma = -10 dB, that is 0.1: N = \Big(\frac{2.326 + 1.1 \times 1.282}{0.1}\Big)^2 \approx 1\,396 \text{ samples}.
  • At -20 dB the requirement is about 100 times larger, about 1.3 \times 10^5 samples.

Code 5.8 checks the first case by Monte Carlo.

The formula promises detection at any SNR if one waits long enough. Real receivers only know the noise power approximately, and this breaks the promise.

Proposition 5.5 — SNR wall (Tandra and Sahai, 2008) Suppose the noise power is only known to lie in [\sigma_n^2 / \rho,\ \rho\, \sigma_n^2] for some \rho > 1. Then no energy detector achieves given P_{FA} < 1/2 < P_D for all noise levels, however large N, when \gamma < \frac{\rho^2 - 1}{\rho}.

Proof. As N \to \infty, the average T/N tends to the received power (law of large numbers).

  1. To keep P_{FA} below 1/2 at the worst noise level \rho \sigma_n^2, the threshold on T/N must eventually exceed \rho \sigma_n^2.
  2. Under \mathcal H_1 with the most favourable noise level, T/N tends to the limit \frac{\sigma_n^2}{\rho} + \gamma\, \sigma_n^2 .
  3. Detection with P_D > 1/2 requires this limit to exceed the threshold, that is \gamma > \rho - 1/\rho. ∎

A noise uncertainty of only 1 dB gives \rho = 1.259 and a wall at \gamma = 0.465, that is -3.3 dB.

5.4 Feature detectors: exploiting the structure of the signal

Man-made signals are not Gaussian noise: they have carriers, symbol rates, pilots and cyclic prefixes. Cyclostationary detectors (Gardner, 1991) use the periodicity of their correlation.

Definition 5.6 — Cyclic autocorrelation For a cyclic frequency \alpha and a lag \ell, R_y^\alpha(\ell) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac1N \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} y[n + \ell]\, \overline{y[n]}\, e^{-2i\pi \alpha n}. Stationary noise has R_w^\alpha = 0 for every \alpha \neq 0, whereas a linearly modulated signal has non-zero values at multiples of its symbol rate. A test on R_y^\alpha at those \alpha is therefore insensitive to the noise level, and escapes the wall of Proposition 5.5.

The price is knowledge of the cyclic frequencies. Eigenvalue-based detectors (Zeng and Liang, 2009) use the sample covariance matrix instead.

5.5 Learned detectors

A learned detector is a classifier trained on labelled windows of IQ samples, spectra or spectrograms. O’Shea, Corgan and Clancy (2016) showed that a CNN on raw IQ samples recognises modulations at least as well as expert features; O’Shea, Roy and Clancy (2018) extended this to over-the-air recordings.

Proposition 5.7 — A calibrated classifier is a likelihood-ratio test If a classifier outputs the posterior probability q(y) = P(\mathcal H_1 \mid y) and the classes have priors \pi_0, \pi_1, then \frac{q(y)}{1 - q(y)} = \frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}\, \frac{p_1(y)}{p_0(y)} . Thresholding q is a likelihood-ratio test; the Neyman–Pearson threshold is set on noise-only validation data for the target P_{FA}.

Proof. Bayes’ formula gives q = \pi_1 p_1 / (\pi_0 p_0 + \pi_1 p_1), and the odds are an increasing function of the likelihood ratio. ∎

A network therefore cannot beat the LRT on the model it was trained on. It wins when no tractable model exists — unknown modulations, coloured or impulsive noise, hardware effects — and loses when the test distribution drifts away from training.

# Code 5.8 — energy detector: Monte Carlo against the Gaussian approximation
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, pfa, snr_db = 1_000, 0.01, -10.0
g = 10 ** (snr_db / 10)                              # SNR as a ratio, 0.1
lam = N + np.sqrt(N) * norm.isf(pfa)                 # threshold (noise power 1)
pd_theory = norm.sf((norm.isf(pfa) - np.sqrt(N) * g) / (1 + g))

def cn(shape, power=1.0):                            # circular complex Gaussian samples
    return np.sqrt(power / 2) * (rng.normal(size=shape) + 1j * rng.normal(size=shape))

T0 = np.sum(np.abs(cn((5_000, N))) ** 2, axis=1)                      # H0: noise only
T1 = np.sum(np.abs(cn((5_000, N), g) + cn((5_000, N))) ** 2, axis=1)  # H1: Gaussian signal
print(f"threshold {lam:.1f}; P_FA {np.mean(T0 > lam):.3f} (target {pfa})")
print(f"P_D Monte Carlo {np.mean(T1 > lam):.3f}, Gaussian approximation {pd_theory:.3f}")
n_req = ((norm.isf(pfa) - (1 + g) * norm.isf(0.9)) / g) ** 2
print(f"samples needed for P_D = 0.9 at -10 dB: {n_req:.0f}")

The simulated false-alarm rate is 0.009 for a target of 0.01, and the detection rate is 0.787 against 0.776 predicted; 1 396 samples reach a detection rate of 0.9.

The second experiment observes a narrowband primary user, a tone of unknown frequency at -15 dB, over 256 samples. It compares the energy detector, the periodogram maximum (the generalised likelihood-ratio test, GLRT, for an unknown frequency) and a logistic regression on the 32 largest periodogram values, a shift-invariant input.

# Code 5.9 — classical versus learned detection of a narrowband signal
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N, snr_db, m = 256, -15.0, 4_000
a = np.sqrt(10 ** (snr_db / 10))                     # tone amplitude, noise power 1

def draw(m):
    y = rng.integers(0, 2, m)
    n = np.arange(N)
    f = rng.uniform(0, 1, (m, 1))                    # unknown normalised frequency
    tone = a * np.exp(2j * np.pi * (f * n + rng.uniform(0, 1, (m, 1))))
    w = (rng.normal(size=(m, N)) + 1j * rng.normal(size=(m, N))) / np.sqrt(2)
    return w + y[:, None] * tone, y

def periodogram(x):
    P = np.abs(np.fft.fft(x, 4 * N, axis=1)) ** 2 / N   # zero-padded periodogram
    return np.sort(P, axis=1)[:, ::-1][:, :32]          # 32 largest values: shift-invariant

xtr, ytr = draw(m)
xte, yte = draw(m)
energy = np.sum(np.abs(xte) ** 2, axis=1)               # energy detector
glrt = np.max(np.abs(np.fft.fft(xte, 4 * N, axis=1)) ** 2, axis=1)   # max periodogram
clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2_000).fit(np.log(periodogram(xtr)), ytr)
learned = clf.decision_function(np.log(periodogram(xte)))
for name, s in [("energy", energy), ("max-periodogram", glrt), ("learned", learned)]:
    print(f"{name:16s} AUC {roc_auc_score(yte, s):.3f}")

The energy detector reaches an AUC of 0.64; the periodogram peak and the learned detector both reach 0.82. Physics (a model of the tone) and learning (with the right invariance) find the same structure; learning keeps its edge where no closed-form test exists.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Spectrum sensing is a binary test at negative SNR; the Neyman–Pearson LRT is optimal for a given false-alarm rate.
  • Known signal: matched filter. Unknown Gaussian signal: energy detector, whose sample size grows like 1/\gamma^2.
  • Noise-power uncertainty creates an SNR wall, -3.3 dB for 1 dB of uncertainty.
  • Feature detectors (cyclostationarity, covariance structure) exploit signal structure and escape the wall.
  • A calibrated classifier is a likelihood-ratio test on its training distribution; set its threshold on noise-only data.

6. Use case: radar emitter identification

An electronic-support receiver listens to the radars around it: it intercepts a stream of pulses from several emitters, interleaved in time, and must say how many radars there are, of which types, and possibly which individual transmitters. This chapter models the pulses, separates the trains with clustering and timing histograms, and discusses what learning adds.

6.1 Operational context and physics of pulsed radars

A pulsed radar emits pulses of width \tau every pulse repetition interval (PRI) T_r. The receiver measures each intercepted pulse and summarises it by a small vector.

Definition 6.1 — Pulse descriptor word A pulse descriptor word (PDW) gathers the measured parameters of one pulse: time of arrival (TOA), carrier frequency (RF), pulse width (PW), amplitude, angle of arrival (AOA), and possibly a description of the modulation inside the pulse. An interception produces a sequence of PDWs sorted by TOA, mixing all the emitters in view.

Example 6.2 — Orders of magnitude

  • A PRI of 1 ms gives an unambiguous range of c T_r / 2 = 150 km; a pulse of 1 µs gives a range resolution of c\tau/2 = 150 m without compression (ch. 1.6).
  • The radar sees its echo after a two-way path, with a power decaying like R^{-4}; the interceptor receives the direct pulse, decaying like R^{-2} (Friis formula).
  • This advantage lets an interceptor detect a radar well beyond the range at which the radar detects it, which is why electronic support exists.

Emitters are classified by their PRI pattern — constant, jittered, staggered, sliding or dwell-and-switch — by RF agility and by intrapulse modulation (Wiley, 2006).

6.2 Deinterleaving by timing: TOA-difference histograms

When several radars share a frequency band and a direction, the PDW parameters do not separate them; their timing does.

Definition 6.3 — Difference histogram For TOAs t_1 < t_2 < \dots, the difference histogram counts the differences t_j - t_i, j > i, falling in each bin of width b, up to a maximum lag. The difference histogram of order c keeps only the differences t_{i+c} - t_i.

Proposition 6.4 — Peaks reveal the PRIs

  1. A single train t_n = \varphi + n T, n = 0, \dots, M - 1, produces the differences kT only, with multiplicity M - k.
  2. For two interleaved trains with PRIs T_1, T_2 of irrational ratio, every difference between a pulse of one train and a pulse of the other occurs at most once.

The histogram therefore shows peaks at the multiples of each PRI over a flat floor of cross-differences.

Proof.

  1. A difference of two pulses n and n + k equals kT, and M - k pairs have index gap k.
  2. A cross-difference has the form \varphi_2 - \varphi_1 + k T_2 - l T_1 (or its negative). If two such values coincide, then (k - k') T_2 = (l - l') T_1; irrationality of the ratio forces k = k' and l = l'. ∎

Missed pulses create peaks at 2T, 3T, \dots that compete with T. Cumulative and sequential difference histograms (CDIF, Mardia, 1989; SDIF, Milojević and Popović, 1992) test orders c = 1, 2, \dots against thresholds to pick the fundamental.

The PRI transform (Nishiguchi and Kobayashi, 2000) suppresses the sub-harmonics by giving each pair a phase: D(\tau) = \sum_{j > i} \delta\big(\tau - (t_j - t_i)\big)\, \exp\Big(2i\pi\, \frac{t_j}{t_j - t_i}\Big). For pairs at distance T of a train, the phase is constant modulo 2\pi and the terms add up. For pairs at distance 2T, it alternates by \pi with the parity of the pulse index and the terms cancel.

6.3 Deinterleaving by clustering the PDWs

When emitters differ in RF, PW or AOA, clustering the PDWs separates them. The number of emitters is unknown and spurious pulses abound, so DBSCAN (Definition 3.6) fits where k-means does not. The practical chain has two stages: cluster the PDWs, then run a difference histogram in each cluster to split look-alike emitters and estimate their PRIs.

6.4 Learning to identify emitters

Learning enters at three places.

  • Emitter type from PDW sequences: a recurrent network or a transformer, with the TOAs as positions (Proposition 4.9), learns agile and staggered PRI patterns end to end.
  • Intrapulse modulation: a CNN on the spectrogram or IQ samples of a pulse, as in ch. 5.5.
  • Deinterleaving as sequence labelling, trained on simulated mixtures.

Operational data are scarce and often confidential, so training relies on simulators; robustness to emitters absent from the library is open (ch. 11).

6.5 Specific emitter identification

Two radars of the same model share their PRI and RF settings, yet their hardware differs.

Definition 6.5 — RF fingerprint Specific emitter identification recognises an individual transmitter from unintentional modulation: the non-linearity of its power amplifier, IQ imbalance, oscillator phase noise, or the shape of its turn-on transient. A simple model of the amplifier is a memoryless polynomial, y(t) = a_1\, x(t) + a_3\, x(t)\, |x(t)|^2, whose coefficients differ slightly between units.

CNNs on raw IQ samples learn the fingerprint directly (Merchant, Revay, Stantchev and Nousain, 2018). The pitfall is shortcut learning: a network may learn the session or the channel instead of the transmitter, so training and test captures must come from different days (Note 3.2).

The code below simulates three emitters observed for 100 ms with 10 % missed pulses. Emitters B and C share their RF and PW and differ by 2° in AOA, less than the AOA measurement noise.

# Code 6.6 — deinterleaving: DBSCAN on PDWs, then TOA-difference histograms
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# emitter: (PRI in us, RF in MHz, PW in us, AOA in deg); B and C look alike
emitters = {"A": (1_000, 9_400, 1.0, 30), "B": (1_300, 3_000, 5.0, 75), "C": (770, 3_000, 5.0, 77)}
toa, pdw, lab = [], [], []
for k, (pri, rf, pw, aoa) in enumerate(emitters.values()):
    t = rng.uniform(0, pri) + pri * np.arange(int(100_000 / pri))   # 100 ms of intercept
    t = t[rng.random(len(t)) > 0.1]                                   # 10 % missed pulses
    toa.append(t + rng.normal(0, 0.05, len(t)))                       # 50 ns TOA jitter
    pdw.append(np.c_[rf + rng.normal(0, 2, len(t)), pw + rng.normal(0, 0.05, len(t)),
                     aoa + rng.normal(0, 2, len(t))])
    lab.append(np.full(len(t), k))
toa, pdw, lab = np.concatenate(toa), np.vstack(pdw), np.concatenate(lab)

# step 1: cluster the pulse descriptor words on (RF, PW, AOA)
cl = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=10).fit_predict(StandardScaler().fit_transform(pdw))
print("clusters:", len(set(cl) - {-1}), " ARI:", round(adjusted_rand_score(lab, cl), 3))

# step 2: inside each cluster, histogram of all TOA differences below 3 ms
for c in sorted(set(cl) - {-1}):
    t = np.sort(toa[cl == c])
    d = (t[None, :] - t[:, None]).ravel()
    h, e = np.histogram(d[(d > 0) & (d < 3_000)], bins=np.arange(97.5, 3_000, 5))
    pri = []
    for p in e[:-1][h > 0.4 * h.max()] + 2.5:        # strong peaks, in increasing order
        if all(abs(p / q - round(p / q)) > 0.05 for q in pri):   # drop multiples
            pri.append(round(float(p)))
    print(f"cluster {c}: {len(t)} pulses, PRIs found (us): {pri}")

DBSCAN finds two clusters (ARI 0.58): emitter A alone, and B and C merged. The difference histogram of the merged cluster then shows two fundamental PRIs, 770 µs and 1 300 µs, and the one of A shows 1 000 µs. Exercise 7 does the same computation by hand.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • An interceptor turns each pulse into a PDW (TOA, RF, PW, amplitude, AOA); emitters are interleaved in the stream.
  • Timing separates what parameters cannot: TOA-difference histograms peak at the PRIs and their multiples, and the PRI transform removes the sub-harmonics.
  • DBSCAN suits PDW clustering: unknown number of emitters, noise pulses, elongated clusters.
  • Learning helps for agile PRI patterns, intrapulse modulations and RF fingerprints, provided splits avoid shortcut learning and simulators cover the real conditions.

7. Use case: speaker recognition and few-shot learning

A voice carries its speaker’s anatomy and habits. Speaker recognition turns them into a score, from a statistical model of short-term spectra (GMM-UBM) to embeddings learned by deep networks. Enrolling a new speaker from a few seconds of speech is also the textbook case of few-shot learning, the last theme of the syllabus.

7.1 Tasks and metrics

Definition 7.1 — Identification, verification, EER Identification assigns a test utterance to one of S enrolled speakers. Verification accepts or rejects a claimed identity by thresholding a score. Verification errors are false acceptances and false rejections; the threshold where their rates are equal defines the equal error rate (EER), and the DET curve plots both rates on a probit scale.

7.2 Physics: the source–filter model

Voiced speech is a glottal pulse train at the pitch F_0 (roughly 85–180 Hz for adult men and 165–255 Hz for adult women) filtered by the vocal tract, whose resonances are the formants. Speaker identity lives in both: pitch statistics, and formant positions set by the length and shape of the tract.

Example 7.2 — The vocal tract as a tube A uniform tube of length \ell, closed at the glottis and open at the lips, resonates at odd multiples of a quarter wavelength: F_k = \frac{(2k - 1)\, c}{4 \ell}, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3, \dots With \ell = 17.5 cm and c = 350 m/s: F_1 = 500 Hz, F_2 = 1500 Hz, F_3 = 2500 Hz. A tract 15 % shorter shifts every formant up by about 18 %, a speaker cue that survives any choice of words.

Linear prediction estimates the vocal-tract filter as an all-pole model, s[n] \approx \sum_{k=1}^p a_k\, s[n - k].

Proposition 7.3 — Normal equations of linear prediction For a wide-sense stationary signal with autocorrelation r, the coefficients minimising the mean squared prediction error solve the Toeplitz system \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\, r[|j - k|] = r[j], \qquad j = 1, \dots, p .

Proof. Set the derivative of \mathbb E\big(s[n] - \sum_k a_k s[n - k]\big)^2 with respect to a_j to zero. This says that the prediction error is orthogonal to each past sample s[n - j] (orthogonality principle), and expanding the expectation gives the system. ∎

The Levinson–Durbin recursion solves it in \mathcal O(p^2) operations; the same equations reappear for gap interpolation in ch. 8.3.

7.3 The classical chain: GMM-UBM

Definition 7.4 — GMM-UBM (Reynolds, Quatieri and Dunn, 2000) MFCC frames (Definition 2.7) of many speakers train a universal background model, a Gaussian mixture with means \mu_k. A speaker model adapts the means to the enrolment frames by maximum a posteriori estimation: \hat\mu_k = \alpha_k\, \bar x_k + (1 - \alpha_k)\, \mu_k, \qquad \alpha_k = \frac{n_k}{n_k + r}, where n_k is the soft count of frames in component k, \bar x_k their mean and r a relevance factor. The verification score of a test utterance is the average log-likelihood ratio between the speaker model and the UBM.

Components that saw little enrolment data stay close to the UBM, which is why the method works with short enrolments. i-vectors (Dehak, Kenny, Dehak, Dumouchel and Ouellet, 2011) later compressed the adapted means into a low-dimensional vector scored by cosine similarity or PLDA.

7.4 Deep speaker embeddings

Definition 7.5 — x-vector (Snyder, Garcia-Romero, Sell, Povey and Khudanpur, 2018) A time-delay network (a 1D CNN over frames) maps each frame to a feature vector u_t. Statistics pooling concatenates their mean and standard deviation over the utterance, and segment-level layers follow. The network is trained to classify thousands of training speakers; the embedding before the softmax is then used for any speaker, scored by cosine similarity or PLDA.

Statistics pooling ignores frame order and maps any length to a fixed size, like the global pooling of Proposition 4.5. ECAPA-TDNN (Desplanques, Thienpondt and Demuynck, 2020) adds channel attention and angular-margin training, benchmarked on VoxCeleb (Nagrani, Chung and Zisserman, 2017).

7.5 Few-shot learning

Definition 7.6 — Episodes and prototypes An N-way K-shot episode gives a support set of K labelled examples for each of N classes never seen in training, and asks to label query examples. Prototypical networks (Snell, Swersky and Zemel, 2017) embed examples with a network f_\theta, average each class into a prototype c_k, and classify a query by a softmax over its negative squared distances to the prototypes. The network is meta-trained on episodes drawn from the training classes.

Proposition 7.7 — Prototypes make a linear classifier With squared Euclidean distances, the prototype rule is a linear classifier in the embedding space, with weights and biases w_k = 2 c_k, \qquad b_k = -\|c_k\|^2 .

Proof. Expanding the distance, -\|f - c_k\|^2 = 2 c_k^\top f - \|c_k\|^2 - \|f\|^2. The last term is the same for every class, so it changes neither the softmax nor the decision. ∎

With K = 1 the rule is nearest-neighbour classification. Speaker enrolment is a few-shot problem: the mean embedding of the enrolment utterances is a prototype. Triplet-loss siamese networks (Schroff, Kalenichenko and Philbin, 2015) and MAML (Finn, Abbeel and Levine, 2017) are the other classic routes; the same tools enrol a new radar emitter.

The code synthesises ten speakers with a source–filter model, scaling average vowel formants (Peterson and Barney, 1952) by a speaker-specific tract factor. It compares one GMM per speaker, trained on five utterances, with K-shot prototypes of a fixed embedding: the mean and standard deviation of the MFCC frames.

# Code 7.8 — speaker identification: GMM per speaker versus few-shot prototypes
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal
from scipy.fft import dct
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fs, S = 8_000, 10                                    # 8 kHz audio, 10 speakers

def mel_filterbank(n_fft, n_mels=24, fmax=4_000):
    mel = lambda f: 2595 * np.log10(1 + f / 700)
    imel = lambda m: 700 * (10 ** (m / 2595) - 1)
    edges = imel(np.linspace(0, mel(fmax), n_mels + 2))
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(n_fft, 1 / fs)
    lo, c, hi = edges[:-2, None], edges[1:-1, None], edges[2:, None]
    return np.maximum(0, np.minimum((f - lo) / (c - lo), (hi - f) / (hi - c)))

FB = mel_filterbank(256)

def mfcc(x, n_ceps=13):
    _, _, Z = signal.stft(x, fs, nperseg=256, noverlap=128)      # 32 ms frames
    E = FB @ (np.abs(Z) ** 2) + 1e-10                             # mel energies
    return dct(np.log(E), axis=0, norm="ortho")[1:n_ceps].T      # frames x 12

# a speaker = mean pitch + vocal-tract length factor (scales all formants)
speakers = [(rng.uniform(90, 220), rng.uniform(0.85, 1.15)) for _ in range(S)]
vowels = [(730, 1090, 2440), (270, 2290, 3010), (300, 870, 2240), (530, 1840, 2480)]

def utterance(spk, dur=1.0):
    f0, alpha = speakers[spk]
    x = []
    for v in rng.permutation(4):                     # the four vowels, random order
        n = int(dur * fs / 4)
        f = f0 * (1 + 0.05 * rng.normal()) * (1 + 0.03 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3 * np.arange(n) / fs))
        e = np.diff(np.floor(np.cumsum(f) / fs), prepend=0)       # glottal pulse train
        for F in vowels[v]:                          # cascade of formant resonators
            r, th = np.exp(-np.pi * 80 / fs), 2 * np.pi * F * alpha / fs
            e = signal.lfilter([1 - r], [1, -2 * r * np.cos(th), r * r], e)
        x.append(e / e.std())
    x = np.concatenate(x)
    return x + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=x.size)

train = {s: np.vstack([mfcc(utterance(s)) for _ in range(5)]) for s in range(S)}
gmms = [GaussianMixture(8, covariance_type="diag", random_state=0).fit(train[s]) for s in range(S)]
tests = [(s, mfcc(utterance(s))) for s in range(S) for _ in range(10)]
acc = np.mean([s == np.argmax([g.score(X) for g in gmms]) for s, X in tests])
print(f"GMM identification accuracy: {acc:.2f}")

def embed(X):                                        # statistics pooling: mean and std
    return np.r_[X.mean(0), X.std(0)]

for K in (1, 5):                                     # K-shot prototypes (Definition 7.6)
    protos = np.array([np.mean([embed(mfcc(utterance(s))) for _ in range(K)], 0) for s in range(S)])
    pred = [np.argmin(((protos - embed(X)) ** 2).sum(1)) for _, X in tests]
    print(f"{K}-shot prototype accuracy: {np.mean([s == p for (s, _), p in zip(tests, pred)]):.2f}")

The GMMs reach an accuracy of 0.94, the 1-shot prototypes 0.94 and the 5-shot prototypes 0.99. Every utterance here contains the same four vowels; on real text-independent speech, fixed MFCC statistics lose to learned x-vectors.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Identification picks a speaker; verification thresholds a score and is measured by the EER.
  • The source–filter model explains the cues: pitch from the source, formants from the vocal tract; linear prediction estimates the tract by solving Toeplitz normal equations.
  • GMM-UBM adapts a background mixture to each speaker by MAP and scores a log-likelihood ratio.
  • x-vectors learn a fixed-size embedding with statistics pooling, trained on many speakers and reused for new ones.
  • Few-shot learning classifies new classes from K examples; prototypes are linear classifiers in the embedding space.

8. Use case: reconstruction of altered audio signals

Old recordings crackle, packets get lost in voice calls, loud passages clip, and microphones pick up noise. Restoration inverts a known degradation with prior knowledge of what audio looks like; the prior is either a model (Wiener, autoregressive, sparse) or learned from clean examples.

8.1 Degradation models

Definition 8.1 — Degradation models With x the clean signal and y the observation:

  • additive noise: y = x + n;
  • missing samples (clicks, packet loss): y = m \odot x, with a binary mask m;
  • clipping at level \theta: y = \mathrm{sign}(x)\, \min(|x|, \theta);
  • band limitation or reverberation: y = h * x + n.

Each is an ill-posed inverse problem: many signals explain y, and the prior chooses.

8.2 Denoising: the Wiener gain

Theorem 8.2 — Wiener gain Let X and N be zero-mean, uncorrelated, of variances S_x and S_n, and Y = X + N. Among the estimates G\, Y with a scalar gain, the mean squared error is minimised by

Key result — the Wiener gain G^\star = \frac{S_x}{S_x + S_n} = \frac{\xi}{1 + \xi}, \qquad \xi = \frac{S_x}{S_n}

Applied to each time–frequency bin of an STFT, with \xi the local SNR, this is the Wiener filter.

Proof. The error \mathbb E|X - G Y|^2 = |1 - G|^2 S_x + |G|^2 S_n is a convex quadratic in G. Setting its derivative to zero gives (G - 1) S_x + G\, S_n = 0. ∎

A bin at 10 dB of local SNR keeps a gain of 0.91, a bin at 0 dB is halved, a bin at -10 dB gets 0.09.

Spectral subtraction (Boll, 1979) estimates S_n on noise-only segments and S_x by \max(|Y|^2 - S_n, 0); it leaves “musical noise”, which the estimator of Ephraim and Malah (1984) suppresses by smoothing the SNR over time.

8.3 Filling gaps with an autoregressive model

Short gaps — a few milliseconds — are well predicted from both sides by linear prediction (Proposition 7.3).

Proposition 8.3 — Least-squares AR interpolation (Janssen, Veldhuis and Vries, 1986) Let b = (1, -a_1, \dots, -a_p) be the prediction-error filter of an AR(p) model, and e = B x the vector of prediction errors over the samples around the gap.

Split x into unknown samples x_U and known samples x_K, and the columns of B accordingly into B_U and B_K. The interpolation minimising the energy of the prediction errors is \hat x_U = -\big(B_U^\top B_U\big)^{-1} B_U^\top B_K\, x_K .

Proof. The residual e = B_U x_U + B_K x_K is affine in x_U; minimising its squared norm is a least-squares problem whose normal equations are B_U^\top (B_U x_U + B_K x_K) = 0. ∎

The method alternates estimating the AR coefficients and interpolating. It needs p larger than the gap’s complexity, roughly the number of spectral peaks times two.

For longer gaps and clipping, sparse priors take over: audio inpainting (Adler, Emiya, Jafari, Elad, Gribonval and Plumbley, 2012) seeks a few Gabor or DCT atoms matching the known samples; declipping also forces restored samples above the clipping level.

8.4 Learned restoration

A network learns the prior from clean audio: degrade clean examples with the model of Definition 8.1, and train it to undo the degradation, as a denoising autoencoder (Definition 4.7).

Proposition 8.4 — A mask trained with the squared error estimates a conditional Wiener gain If a network outputs a real mask M(Y) applied to the noisy bin Y and minimises \mathbb E|X - M(Y)\, Y|^2, its optimum is M^\star(Y) = \frac{\mathrm{Re}\, \mathbb E[X\, \overline{Y} \mid Y]}{|Y|^2} .

Proof. Conditionally on Y, the error is a quadratic function of the real number M; setting its derivative to zero gives the formula. ∎

With the true clean and noise spectra, the ideal ratio mask |X|^2 / (|X|^2 + |N|^2) is the oracle version of this gain, the upper bound of Code 8.5.

  • Architectures: U-Nets on magnitude spectrograms (Jansson et al., 2017) and on waveforms (Wave-U-Net, Stoller, Ewert and Dixon, 2018); Demucs for real-time speech enhancement in the waveform domain (Défossez, Synnaeve and Adi, 2020).
  • Phase: a mask on magnitudes keeps the noisy phase; Griffin and Lim (1984) estimate a consistent phase by alternating projections between the STFT and the signal domain.
  • Metrics: SNR and scale-invariant SDR (Le Roux, Wisdom, Erdogan and Hershey, 2019); perceptual scores such as PESQ (ITU-T P.862) and STOI (Taal, Hendriks, Heusdens and Jensen, 2011).
# Code 8.5 — a 5 ms gap: linear versus AR interpolation; denoising: oracle mask versus spectral subtraction
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal
from scipy.linalg import solve_toeplitz

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fs = 8_000
t = np.arange(4_000) / fs
x = (np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t) + 0.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 660 * t + 1)
     + 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1_250 * t)) + 0.02 * rng.normal(size=t.size)

# --- gap interpolation with an AR(p) model (Proposition 8.3) ---------------------
gap = np.arange(2_000, 2_040)                         # 40 missing samples = 5 ms
known = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(x.size), gap)
p = 40
ctx = np.r_[x[1_500:2_000], x[2_040:2_540]]           # context used to fit the model
r = np.correlate(ctx, ctx, "full")[ctx.size - 1:][: p + 1] / ctx.size
a = solve_toeplitz(r[:p], r[1:p + 1])                 # normal equations (Proposition 7.3)
b = np.r_[1, -a]                                      # prediction-error filter
seg = np.arange(gap[0] - p, gap[-1] + p + 1)          # residuals touching the gap
B = np.zeros((seg.size - p, seg.size))
for i in range(seg.size - p):
    B[i, i:i + p + 1] = b[::-1]                       # e[n] = sum_k b_k x[n-k]
U = np.isin(seg, gap)
xu = np.linalg.lstsq(B[:, U], -B[:, ~U] @ x[seg[~U]], rcond=None)[0]
lin = np.interp(gap, known, x[known])
snr = lambda est: 10 * np.log10(np.sum(x[gap] ** 2) / np.sum((x[gap] - est) ** 2))
print(f"gap of 5 ms: linear interpolation {snr(lin):5.1f} dB, AR({p}) {snr(xu):5.1f} dB")

# --- denoising: ideal ratio mask (oracle) versus spectral subtraction -------------
noise = 0.5 * rng.normal(size=x.size)
y = x + noise
_, _, X = signal.stft(x, fs, nperseg=256)
_, _, N = signal.stft(noise, fs, nperseg=256)
_, _, Y = signal.stft(y, fs, nperseg=256)
Sn = np.mean(np.abs(N) ** 2, axis=1, keepdims=True)  # noise PSD (from a noise-only recording)
gains = {"ideal ratio mask": np.abs(X) ** 2 / (np.abs(X) ** 2 + np.abs(N) ** 2),
         "spectral subtraction": np.maximum(1 - Sn / np.abs(Y) ** 2, 0)}
out = lambda z: 10 * np.log10(np.sum(x ** 2) / np.sum((x - z[: x.size]) ** 2))
print(f"input SNR {out(y):5.1f} dB")
for name, G in gains.items():
    print(f"{name:21s} {out(signal.istft(G * Y, fs, nperseg=256)[1]):5.1f} dB")

On the 5 ms gap, linear interpolation scores -1 dB and AR(40) interpolation 29 dB. For denoising from an input SNR of 4 dB, spectral subtraction reaches 10 dB and the oracle mask 17 dB: the gap between the two is the room left for a learned mask estimator.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Restoration inverts a known degradation (noise, mask, clipping, filtering) with a prior on audio.
  • The Wiener gain \xi / (1 + \xi) is the MMSE scalar gain per bin; spectral subtraction approximates it.
  • AR models fill gaps of a few milliseconds by least squares; sparse dictionaries handle longer gaps and clipping.
  • A mask network trained with the squared error estimates a conditional Wiener gain; the ideal ratio mask is its oracle bound.

9. Use case: predictive maintenance

A rotating machine announces its failure weeks in advance, in its vibrations. Predictive maintenance listens to them to repair just in time — not too early, which wastes parts, and not too late, which breaks the machine. This chapter models the most common fault, a damaged rolling bearing, and builds the detection chain from physics to anomaly detection and learned prognostics.

9.1 Operational context

Definition 9.1 — Maintenance strategies Corrective maintenance repairs after failure; preventive maintenance replaces parts on a fixed schedule; condition-based maintenance acts when a measured indicator crosses a threshold; predictive maintenance also estimates the remaining useful life (RUL), the time left before failure.

Failures are rare and machines differ, so the unsupervised setting — learn “healthy”, flag the rest — dominates.

9.2 Physics of a bearing fault

A rolling bearing has n balls of diameter d on a pitch circle of diameter D, with contact angle \varphi; the inner race turns at f_r and the outer race is fixed. A local defect produces an impact each time a ball rolls over it.

Proposition 9.2 — Characteristic fault frequencies With rolling without slipping, the cage turns at the frequency f_{\mathrm{cage}} = \frac{f_r}{2}\Big(1 - \frac dD \cos\varphi\Big), and the defect frequencies on the outer and inner races are

Key result — bearing fault frequencies \mathrm{BPFO} = \frac{n f_r}{2}\Big(1 - \frac dD \cos\varphi\Big), \qquad \mathrm{BPFI} = \frac{n f_r}{2}\Big(1 + \frac dD \cos\varphi\Big)

Proof. A ball’s centre moves at half the speed of the inner-race contact point, since the outer-race contact point is at rest. That contact point is at radius (D - d\cos\varphi)/2 and the ball’s centre at radius D/2, which gives the cage frequency.

A fixed outer-race defect is hit by n balls per cage revolution: \mathrm{BPFO} = n f_{\mathrm{cage}}. An inner-race defect turns at f_r - f_{\mathrm{cage}} relative to the cage: \mathrm{BPFI} = n (f_r - f_{\mathrm{cage}}). ∎

Example 9.3 — A bearing at 1 500 rpm With n = 9, f_r = 25 Hz, d/D = 0.2 and \varphi = 0: the cage turns at 10 Hz, and \mathrm{BPFO} = 9 \times 12.5 \times 0.8 = 90 \text{ Hz}, \qquad \mathrm{BPFI} = 9 \times 12.5 \times 1.2 = 135 \text{ Hz}.

Each impact rings a structural resonance, typically several kilohertz, so the fault frequency appears as an amplitude modulation of that resonance, not as a spectral line of its own. Envelope analysis band-passes the resonance, takes its envelope and looks at the envelope’s spectrum.

Proposition 9.4 — Envelope by the analytic signal (Bedrosian, 1963) Let x(t) = a(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t + \phi) with a \geq 0 whose spectrum lies in (-f_c, f_c). With \mathcal H the Hilbert transform, the analytic signal is x + i\,\mathcal H x = a(t)\, e^{i(2\pi f_c t + \phi)}, so its modulus returns the envelope a(t).

9.3 Condition indicators

Root mean square, peak value and crest factor (peak over RMS) track energy; kurtosis tracks impulsiveness.

Proposition 9.5 — Kurtosis of sparse impulses The kurtosis \mathbb E(x - \mu)^4 / \sigma^4 of a Gaussian variable is 3. For impulses x = b\, g, with b Bernoulli of parameter p and g \sim \mathcal N(0, s^2) independent, it equals 3/p.

Proof. \mathbb E x^2 = p\, s^2 and \mathbb E x^4 = 3 p\, s^4, since b^4 = b^2 = b; the mean is zero. The ratio is 3p\, s^4 / (p\, s^2)^2 = 3/p. ∎

Rare impacts (p = 0.02) give a kurtosis of 150. Background noise dilutes it, which is why kurtosis is computed in the resonance band — the idea behind the spectral kurtosis of Antoni (2006), which selects the band automatically.

# Code 9.6 — envelope analysis of a simulated outer-race fault
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal, stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
fs, dur = 20_000, 1.0
n_balls, fr, ratio = 9, 25.0, 0.2                    # 9 balls, 1500 rpm, d/D = 0.2
bpfo = n_balls * fr / 2 * (1 - ratio)                # 90 Hz (contact angle 0)
t = np.arange(int(fs * dur)) / fs

def vibration(faulty):
    x = 0.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * fr * t) + rng.normal(0, 1.0, t.size)   # shaft + noise
    if faulty:                                       # impacts at BPFO ringing at 3 kHz
        imp = np.zeros(t.size)
        times = np.arange(0, dur, 1 / bpfo) + rng.normal(0, 2e-4, int(np.ceil(dur * bpfo)))
        imp[(times[times < dur] * fs).astype(int)] = 3.0
        ring = np.exp(-800 * t[:200]) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3_000 * t[:200])
        x += np.convolve(imp, ring)[: t.size]
    return x

for faulty in (False, True):
    x = vibration(faulty)
    sos = signal.butter(4, [2_000, 4_000], btype="bandpass", fs=fs, output="sos")
    xb = signal.sosfiltfilt(sos, x)                  # the resonance band, 2-4 kHz
    env = np.abs(signal.hilbert(xb))                 # its envelope (Proposition 9.4)
    E = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(env - env.mean()))
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(env.size, 1 / fs)
    band = (f > 10) & (f < 500)
    k = f[band][np.argmax(E[band])]
    print(f"faulty={faulty!s:5}: kurtosis raw {stats.kurtosis(x, fisher=False):4.2f},"
          f" band {stats.kurtosis(xb, fisher=False):4.2f}; envelope peak {k:5.1f} Hz,"
          f" {E[band].max() / np.median(E[band]):4.1f} x the median")

On the healthy signal, both kurtoses stay at 3 and the envelope spectrum has no line (its maximum, at 206 Hz, is 3 times the median). On the faulty one, the raw kurtosis is only 3.3, the band kurtosis 5.5, and the envelope spectrum shows a line at exactly 90 Hz, 28 times the median: the BPFO of Example 9.3.

9.4 Anomaly detection with a controlled false-alarm rate

Learn the distribution of indicators on healthy data, and raise an alarm when a new window is unlikely under it.

Proposition 9.7 — Mahalanobis alarm If healthy feature vectors in \mathbb R^d follow \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma), the squared Mahalanobis distance follows a chi-square law with d degrees of freedom: D^2(z) = (z - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (z - \mu) \sim \chi^2_d . Raising an alarm when D^2 exceeds the (1 - \alpha)-quantile of \chi^2_d gives a false-alarm rate \alpha.

Proof. By Proposition 0.4, u = \Sigma^{-1/2}(z - \mu) \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d), and D^2 = \|u\|^2 is a sum of d independent squared standard Gaussians. ∎

For d = 3 and \alpha = 0.01 the threshold is 11.34. Indicators such as kurtosis are not Gaussian, so the realised rate drifts from \alpha: calibrate it on held-out healthy data.

On raw spectra, the reconstruction error of an autoencoder trained on healthy data (Definition 4.7) plays the role of D^2; one-class SVMs (Schölkopf et al., 2001) and isolation forests (Liu, Ting and Zhou, 2008) are alternatives.

# Code 9.8 — a Mahalanobis health indicator on a slowly growing fault
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal, stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fs, n, bpfo = 20_000, 4_096, 90.0
sos = signal.butter(4, [2_000, 4_000], btype="bandpass", fs=fs, output="sos")
ring = np.exp(-800 * np.arange(200) / fs) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 3_000 * np.arange(200) / fs)

def features(severity):
    """One 0.2 s window: noise + impacts of amplitude `severity` at BPFO."""
    x = rng.normal(size=n)
    imp = np.zeros(n)
    imp[(np.arange(0, n / fs, 1 / bpfo) * fs).astype(int)] = severity
    xb = signal.sosfiltfilt(sos, x + np.convolve(imp, ring)[:n])
    return [np.sqrt(np.mean(xb ** 2)), stats.kurtosis(xb, fisher=False),
            np.max(np.abs(xb)) / np.sqrt(np.mean(xb ** 2))]     # RMS, kurtosis, crest

H = np.array([features(0.0) for _ in range(300)])               # healthy reference
mu, P = H.mean(0), np.linalg.inv(np.cov(H.T))
d2 = lambda z: (z - mu) @ P @ (z - mu)                           # Mahalanobis distance
thr = stats.chi2.ppf(0.99, df=3)                                 # 1 % false alarms (Proposition 9.7)
print(f"threshold {thr:.2f}; false alarms on new healthy data:",
      np.mean([d2(features(0.0)) > thr for _ in range(300)]))
for day, sev in enumerate(np.linspace(0, 2, 9)):                 # a slowly growing fault
    print(f"day {day}: severity {sev:4.2f}, alarm rate",
          np.mean([d2(features(sev)) > thr for _ in range(50)]))

The false-alarm rate on new healthy windows is 0.02 for a target of 0.01, a symptom of non-Gaussian features. The alarm rate rises from 0.1 at severity 0.75 to 0.48 at 1.0 and 1.0 from severity 1.5: the indicator detects the fault before it dominates the noise.

9.5 Prognostics and learned models

Estimating the RUL is a regression on degradation trajectories. The C-MAPSS data set (Saxena, Goebel, Simon and Eklund, 2008) simulates run-to-failure trajectories of turbofan engines with 21 sensors. Its score penalises late predictions more than early ones, with d the predicted minus the true RUL: s(d) = e^{-d/13} - 1 \ \ (d < 0), \qquad s(d) = e^{d/10} - 1 \ \ (d \geq 0).

1D CNNs and LSTMs on sliding windows of the sensors are standard learned baselines. Deployment meets few run-to-failure examples, domain shift between machines and speeds, and the need to explain alarms. Physics helps: order tracking resamples the signal at constant shaft angle, and envelope features make good inputs. Lei and coauthors (2020) review the learned approaches.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Predictive maintenance detects degradation early and estimates the remaining useful life; failures are rare, so learning is mostly unsupervised.
  • Bearing defects repeat at BPFO and BPFI, set by the geometry; they modulate a structural resonance.
  • Envelope analysis (band-pass, analytic signal, spectrum) reveals the fault line hidden in a Gaussian-looking signal.
  • Kurtosis grows like 3/p for rare impacts; compute it in the resonance band.
  • A Mahalanobis or autoencoder score with a calibrated threshold controls the false-alarm rate.

10.2 A deep network without learning: the scattering transform

Why do spectrograms and mel features work so well as inputs? Because a small deformation of a signal — a slight change of speed or pitch — barely changes them, whereas it moves the high-frequency lines of a Fourier spectrum a lot. Mallat (2012) built a deep network with this stability by design: cascade wavelet filters, moduli and averaging, S x = \big(x * \phi,\ |x * \psi_{\lambda_1}| * \phi,\ \big||x * \psi_{\lambda_1}| * \psi_{\lambda_2}\big| * \phi,\ \dots\big).

Proposition 10.1 — Wavelet-modulus layers are non-expansive Suppose the filters satisfy the Littlewood–Paley condition, for every frequency f: |\hat\phi(f)|^2 + \sum_\lambda |\hat\psi_\lambda(f)|^2 \leq 1 . Then the layer U x = (x * \phi, |x * \psi_\lambda|)_\lambda satisfies \|Ux - Uy\| \leq \|x - y\|, and so does any cascade of such layers.

Proof. By the reverse triangle inequality, \big||a| - |b|\big| \leq |a - b| pointwise, so \|Ux - Uy\|^2 \leq \|(x - y) * \phi\|^2 + \sum_\lambda \|(x - y) * \psi_\lambda\|^2 . By Plancherel, the right-hand side is the integral of |\widehat{x - y}|^2 against the Littlewood–Paley sum, which is at most \|x - y\|^2. A composition of non-expansive maps is non-expansive. ∎

Mallat (2012) proves more: the scattering transform is Lipschitz-stable to small deformations, which the Fourier modulus is not. Andén and Mallat (2014) obtained strong audio classification results with it, without any training of the filters.

# Code 10.2 — a 3 % dilation: Fourier modulus versus constant-Q averages
import numpy as np

fs, n = 16_000, 16_000
t = np.arange(n) / fs
harmonic = lambda f0: sum(np.sin(2 * np.pi * k * f0 * t) / k for k in range(1, 31))
x, x_warp = harmonic(200.0), harmonic(206.0)          # a 3 % dilation of the frequency axis

f = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1 / fs)
centres = np.geomspace(100, 7_000, 40)                # constant-Q bands (about 11 % wide)
bank = np.exp(-0.5 * (np.log(f[None, 1:] / centres[:, None]) / 0.05) ** 2)

def fourier(x):                                       # Fourier modulus: shift-invariant only
    return np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x))[1:]

def averaged(x):                                      # band energies = first-order scattering
    return np.sqrt(bank @ fourier(x) ** 2)

for name, phi in [("Fourier modulus", fourier), ("constant-Q averages", averaged)]:
    a, b = phi(x), phi(x_warp)
    print(f"{name:20s} relative change {np.linalg.norm(a - b) / np.linalg.norm(a):.2f}")

The Fourier modulus changes by 1.41 — its lines no longer overlap — while the constant-Q averages change by 0.30. Learned front-ends such as SincNet (Ravanelli and Bengio, 2018) and LEAF (Zeghidour, Teboul, de Chaumont Quitry and Tagliasacchi, 2021) start from such filter banks and let training adjust them. Algorithm unrolling goes the other way: it turns the iterations of a classical algorithm into network layers, as LISTA did for sparse coding (Gregor and LeCun, 2010).

10.3 How research is organised in industry

The syllabus announces a discussion of industrial research; the following points frame it.

  • From use case to product. A need from an operational team becomes a research question, then a prototype on recorded data, then a demonstrator in real conditions, then an industrialised function. Technology readiness levels (TRL 1–9) name these stages.
  • Data. Field data are costly, sometimes confidential, often unlabelled; simulators, data-collection campaigns and annotation are research tasks in their own right.
  • Constraints. Embedded hardware limits memory, power and latency; safety-critical functions need guarantees, explanations and qualification; deployed models must be monitored for drift.
  • Collaboration. Companies work with academic laboratories through joint projects, industrial PhDs (the French CIFRE scheme), internships and shared publications, while protecting know-how through patents.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • Learning pays where physics leaves a gap and data cover the operating conditions; classical chains remain the baseline and the source of good inputs.
  • Depth adds exponentially many regions (Theorem 4.13) and composes invariances and stability (scattering).
  • Wavelet-modulus cascades are non-expansive; averaging makes them stable to deformations.
  • Industrial research runs from use case to deployment under data, embedding and certification constraints.

11. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It surveys where research on learning for signals is heading, around the five use cases of the course, and lists open problems suitable for a project or an internship. The works cited are entry points, not a complete bibliography.

11.1 Self-supervised and foundation models for signals

Labels are the bottleneck of every use case. Self-supervised learning trains on unlabelled signals with a pretext task, then adapts to the target task with few labels.

  • In speech, wav2vec 2.0 (Baevski, Zhou, Mohamed and Auli, 2020) masks latent frames and solves a contrastive task over quantised targets; HuBERT (Hsu and coauthors, 2021) predicts cluster labels of masked frames; WavLM (Chen and coauthors, 2022) adds denoising and reaches strong results on speaker verification among other tasks.
  • For radio, radar and vibration, comparable pretrained models are much less mature. Which pretext tasks respect the physics — carrier offsets, multipath, operating speeds — is an open question.

11.2 Open-set recognition and novelty

An emitter library, a speaker list or a set of fault types is never complete. A closed-set classifier assigns every input to a known class, often with high confidence.

  • Scheirer, de Rezende Rocha, Sapkota and Boult (2013) formalised open-set recognition; Bendale and Boult (2016) proposed OpenMax to reject unknown inputs from deep features.
  • Calibration (Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger, 2017) and conformal prediction (Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023) give confidence statements with explicit guarantees, the kind that safety-critical users ask for.

Open problem: open-set emitter identification with guarantees on the rate of unknown emitters wrongly accepted.

11.3 Domain shift and simulation-to-real transfer

Most industrial training data come from simulators or from a few machines, and deployment happens elsewhere. Domain-adversarial training (Ganin and coauthors, 2016) learns features that a discriminator cannot attribute to a domain. For maintenance, transfer across machines, loads and speeds remains largely unsolved; benchmarks often split windows of the same recordings, which overstates performance (Note 3.2).

11.4 Model-based deep learning

Between hand-crafted chains and black boxes lies a spectrum of hybrids.

  • Algorithm unrolling turns the iterations of a classical solver into trainable layers (Monga, Li and Eldar, 2021).
  • Model-based deep learning (Shlezinger, Whang, Eldar and Dimakis, 2023) replaces only the parts of a classical algorithm that depend on an unknown model, keeping its structure and interpretability.
  • Physics-informed networks (Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis, 2019) add residuals of differential equations to the loss.

Open problem: when does a hybrid provably need fewer samples than a generic network for the same accuracy?

11.5 Generative priors for restoration

Diffusion models learn a prior over clean audio. DiffWave (Kong, Ping, Huang, Zhao and Catanzaro, 2021) generates waveforms with a diffusion model; diffusion posterior sampling (Chung, Kim, McCann, Klasky and Ye, 2023) combines such a prior with a measurement model to solve inverse problems, including inpainting. The Wiener and AR priors of ch. 8 become special cases of a learned prior plus a likelihood.

Open problem: restoration with guarantees of consistency with the observed samples and controlled hallucination for archival audio.

11.6 Theory of depth

Theorem 4.13 is a one-dimensional example. Eldan and Shamir (2016) proved a separation between depth 2 and depth 3 for a radial function in high dimension; Poggio and coauthors (2017) showed that deep networks avoid the curse of dimensionality for compositional functions whose constituents depend on few variables. Mallat’s scattering theory (ch. 10.2) explains stability to deformations. Why trained networks find such structured solutions by gradient descent remains open (see Theoretical Foundations of Deep Learning).

11.7 Frugal and embedded learning

Sensors run on small processors and batteries. Integer quantisation (Jacob and coauthors, 2018) and pruning cut memory and energy, while certification asks for models whose behaviour can be bounded. Designing networks that are small, robust and certifiable at once is an active question for every use case of the course.

Key takeaways — chapter 11

  • Self-supervised pretraining reduces the need for labels in speech; its counterparts for radio, radar and vibration are still open.
  • Open-set recognition, calibration and conformal prediction address inputs outside the training classes.
  • Domain shift between simulators, machines and sites is the main obstacle to deployment.
  • Hybrid, model-based networks and generative priors connect the classical chains of the course with learning.
  • Depth separation, compositionality and scattering explain parts of the success of depth; trainability remains open.

  1. ★ Aliasing and complex baseband. (a) A 9 kHz tone is sampled at 8 kHz. At which frequency does it appear? (b) A receiver captures a band of 20 MHz around 2.4 GHz. Compare the minimum rate of complex IQ sampling with the minimum rate of real sampling of the raw band-pass signal.

    Solution: (a) the tone is folded by one sampling rate, 9 - 8 = 1 kHz. (b) Complex sampling needs 20 million complex samples per second (the bandwidth). Real sampling of the raw signal must exceed twice its highest frequency, 2 \times 2.41 GHz = 4.82 GHz: about 240 times more samples per second, or 120 times more real numbers, since an IQ sample holds two.

  2. ★ A resolution budget. Speech is sampled at 16 kHz. A Hann window has a main lobe four bins wide. Which window length separates two harmonics 100 Hz apart, and what hop gives 50 % overlap?

    Solution: two lines stay separated when they are at least half a main lobe apart, two bins. Hence 2 f_s / N \leq 100 Hz, so N \geq 320 samples (20 ms). With N = 512 (32 ms, a power of two), the hop is 256 samples, 16 ms; the bin spacing is 31.25 Hz.

  3. ★ Pulse compression. A radar transmits a linear chirp of T = 20 µs sweeping B = 10 MHz. Compute the compression gain, the compressed range resolution, and the resolution without compression.

    Solution: BT = 200, a gain of 10 \log_{10} 200 = 23 dB (Theorem 1.9, with sampling at the bandwidth). Resolution with compression: c / (2B) = 15 m; without: c T / 2 = 3 km.

(b) R = 1 + 4 \times (1 + 2 + 4 + 8) = 61 samples (Proposition 4.5). (c) The gap to the hand-crafted features narrows with more steps; report both accuracies with the same test set size.

(a) Prove that 1-shot prototype classification is nearest-neighbour classification, and that K-shot prototypes with squared Euclidean distance give a linear classifier (Proposition 7.7). (b) In Code 7.8, compute the accuracy for K = 1, \dots, 5, then replace the Euclidean distance by the cosine distance.

  10. ★★ The Wiener gain in numbers. (a) Show that the minimal mean squared error of Theorem 8.2 equals S_x S_n / (S_x + S_n), so that the output SNR is 1 + \xi. (b) Compute the gain and the SNR improvement for bins at 10 dB and at -5 dB.

    Solution: (a) Substituting G^\star in the error gives S_x S_n / (S_x + S_n); dividing S_x by it gives 1 + \xi.

    For (b): at 10 dB, the gain is 0.909, with an output SNR of 10 \log_{10} 11 = 10.4 dB, an improvement of 0.4 dB. At -5 dB, where \xi = 0.316: gain 0.240, output SNR 1.2 dB, an improvement of 6.2 dB. The Wiener gain helps most where the SNR is low, at the price of attenuating the signal there.

  11. ★★ An inner-race fault (computational). A bearing has n = 8 balls, d/D = 0.25, contact angle 0, and turns at f_r = 30 Hz. (a) Compute BPFO and BPFI. (b) For impacts b\, g plus Gaussian noise w of variance \sigma^2, compute the kurtosis. (c) Modify Code 9.6 to simulate an inner-race fault whose impact amplitude is modulated by the shaft rotation, and identify the lines of the envelope spectrum.

    Solution: (a) \mathrm{BPFO} = 8 \times 15 \times 0.75 = 90 Hz and \mathrm{BPFI} = 8 \times 15 \times 1.25 = 150 Hz. (b) With b Bernoulli(p), g \sim \mathcal N(0, s^2), independent of w: K = \frac{3 p s^4 + 6 p s^2 \sigma^2 + 3 \sigma^4}{(p s^2 + \sigma^2)^2}, which is 3 for p = 1 and tends to 3/p when \sigma \to 0. (c) Multiply the impulse train at 150 Hz by 1 + 0.5 \cos(2\pi \cdot 30\, t): the envelope spectrum shows a line at 150 Hz with sidebands at 120 and 180 Hz, the signature of an inner-race defect passing through the load zone.

  12. ★★ Gradient descent on least squares. Let F(\theta) = \frac12 \|A\theta - y\|^2, where the eigenvalues of A^\top A lie in [\mu, L] with \mu > 0. (a) Give the smoothness constant and the step of Theorem 3.9. (b) Show that the error of gradient descent obeys \theta_k - \theta^\star = \big(I - A^\top A / L\big)^k (\theta_0 - \theta^\star), and deduce a geometric rate. (c) With \mu = 1 and L = 100, how many iterations divide the error by 1 000?

    Solution: (a) The gradient A^\top(A\theta - y) is L-Lipschitz, so the step is 1/L. (b) Using A^\top A \theta^\star = A^\top y, the error obeys the linear recursion. The matrix has eigenvalues in [0, 1 - \mu/L], so the error contracts by 1 - \mu/L per step.

    For (c): one needs 0.99^k \leq 10^{-3}, that is k \geq \ln(1000) / (-\ln 0.99) \approx 688 iterations: the condition number L/\mu sets the speed.

  13. ★★★ Depth separation in practice (computational). Fit \tau^5 (Theorem 4.13) on [0, 1] with (i) one-hidden-layer ReLU networks of widths 4, 8, 16, 64, and (ii) networks of depth 5 and width 4, trained with Adam on a fine grid. Report the L^1 errors and compare with the bound of Theorem 4.13.

    Hints and expected result: with k = 5, the theorem guarantees an error of at least 1/16 for widths up to 2^3 - 2 = 6, and wide networks need about 31 units to fit exactly. The deep network can represent \tau^5 exactly, but gradient descent often fails to find that solution from a random start: expressivity and trainability are different questions. Discuss initialisation near the exact weights.

  14. ★★★ Can a learned detector beat the SNR wall? Train a classifier (logistic regression on the periodogram, or a small CNN) to detect a white Gaussian signal when the noise power varies by ±1 dB between examples. Study its false-alarm rate on noise-only data at the worst noise level, as the SNR decreases towards and below -3.3 dB.

    Hints and expected result: below the wall, choose the noise level \sigma_n^2 = \rho\sigma^2 - P under \mathcal H_1; it lies in the uncertainty interval, and the samples then have exactly the law of noise of power \rho\sigma^2 under \mathcal H_0. No test, learned or not, can then have P_D > P_{FA} for both configurations. With a BPSK signal the two laws differ, and a detector exploiting cyclostationarity (Definition 5.6) escapes the wall.

Two hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no calculator needed beyond a table of the Gaussian tail and of chi-square quantiles; four exercises.

  1. Question (4 points). Explain the time–frequency trade-off of the STFT, state the uncertainty principle, and choose a representation for (a) bearing impacts ringing at 3 kHz and (b) the formants of speech. Expected: windowed DFTs and the bound \sigma_t \sigma_f \geq 1/(4\pi) (Theorem 2.3); (a) short windows or wavelets, which are fine in time at high frequency; (b) windows of 20–30 ms, or mel spectra.
  2. Question (6 points). An energy detector uses N = 2000 samples with P_{FA} = 0.01, so that Q^{-1}(P_{FA}) = 2.326, at an SNR of -12 dB. Compute the threshold and P_D, then the SNR wall for a noise uncertainty of 0.5 dB.
    • Expected: the threshold is about 2104\, \sigma^2, since \sqrt{2000} = 44.7; with \gamma = 0.063, P_D = Q(-0.47) \approx 0.68 (Proposition 5.3).
    • With \rho = 1.122, the wall is (\rho^2 - 1)/\rho = 0.231, that is -6.4 dB (Proposition 5.5).
  3. Question (5 points). Prove that among linear filters the matched filter maximises the output SNR, and give the SNR gain of a chirp with BT = 500 sampled at its bandwidth. Expected: output decomposition, noise variance \sigma^2 \|h\|^2, Cauchy–Schwarz with its equality case (Theorem 1.9); gain 10 \log_{10} 500 \approx 27 dB.
  4. Question (5 points). A bearing has 10 balls, d/D = 0.15, contact angle 0, and turns at 20 Hz. Compute BPFO and BPFI, describe the envelope-analysis chain, and set a 1 % false-alarm threshold on a Mahalanobis score built from four Gaussian indicators. Expected: \mathrm{BPFO} = 10 \times 10 \times 0.85 = 85 Hz and \mathrm{BPFI} = 115 Hz (Proposition 9.2); band-pass around the resonance, analytic-signal envelope, spectrum; threshold at the 99 % quantile of \chi^2_4, 13.28 (Proposition 9.7).
