0. Review: filtrations and Brownian motion

Every sampler and every generative model in the later chapters is a discrete skeleton of a diffusion. The driving noise is Brownian motion. This chapter fixes that process and the conditional calculus used to prove it is a martingale.

The official prerequisite is a first course in statistics and probability. We use three facts from that course. A square-integrable random variable has a conditional expectation given a sub-sigma-algebra. That conditional expectation is a projection, so the tower property holds. A standard Gaussian has mean 0, variance 1, and fourth moment 3.

Definition 0.1 — Filtration

A filtration is a family of sigma-algebras indexed by t\ge 0, increasing in t. A process is adapted when its value at time t is measurable for the sigma-algebra at time t.

We take the filtration generated by the Brownian motion, completed and made right-continuous, as in Revuz and Yor (1999). Statements below that only use independent increments do not need the completion step.

Definition 0.2 — Standard Brownian motion

A real process W is a standard Brownian motion when the four properties below hold.

  • W_0=0.
  • Every path t\mapsto W_t is continuous.
  • Increments over disjoint intervals are independent.
  • The increment over an interval of length t is centred Gaussian with variance t.

Theorem 0.1 — Covariance

For nonnegative s and t,

\mathbb{E}[W_s W_t]=\min(s,t).

Proof. Take s\le t. Split the later value into the earlier value plus the increment. The product then has two terms. The square of W_s has expectation s, because the increment from 0 to s has variance s.

The cross term is the product of W_s and a later increment. Condition on the past at time s. The conditional expectation of the increment is 0, so the cross term vanishes. The product formula follows, and the case t<s is the same with the labels swapped. ∎

The fourth moment of a centred Gaussian is three times the square of its variance. Over an interval of length h, the fourth moment of the increment is 3h^2. Kolmogorov’s continuity criterion, applied to these moments and to higher even moments, yields a Hölder modification. Paths are almost surely Hölder of every order strictly less than 1/2. They are almost surely nowhere differentiable. The proof of the chaining argument is in Revuz and Yor (1999). The moment computation is the part we need, because it feeds the quadratic variation.

Proposition 0.2 — Quadratic variation in L^2

Fix a time t>0 and the uniform partition of that interval into n pieces. Let S_n be the sum of the squares of the increments. Then S_n converges to t in L^2.

Proof. Each increment is centred Gaussian with variance t/n, so each square has mean t/n and S_n has mean t.

The fourth moment of a standard Gaussian is 3, so the variance of the square of one increment is 2(t/n)^2. Increments on disjoint intervals are independent, and the variance of S_n is therefore 2t^2/n.

The mean squared error \mathbb{E}[(S_n-t)^2] tends to 0. That is L^2 convergence. ∎

Example 0.3 — Two numerical checks

Take s=2 and t=5. Theorem 0.1 gives the product moment 2. The increment from time 1 to time 3 has variance 2.

On [0,1] with n=1000 equal steps, the variance of S_n-1 is 2/1000. The standard deviation is about 0.045. Exercise 2 draws one such path. A value near 0.96 sits about one standard deviation below the mean, which is the fluctuation Proposition 0.2 predicts, not a bias in the limit.

The integral in ch. 2 is a limit of sums \sum H_{t_i}(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}). Path continuity makes the sums well defined along uniform grids. The quadratic variation is what replaces (dW)^2 by dt inside Itô’s formula in ch. 3.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Conditional expectation is an L^2 projection, and the tower property lets us condition in two steps.
  • Brownian motion starts at 0, has continuous paths and independent Gaussian increments.
  • The covariance at times s and t equals the smaller time.
  • Sums of squared increments converge to t in L^2, with variance 2t^2/n on the uniform grid.
  • Paths are Hölder of every order below 1/2, and nowhere differentiable.

1. Continuous martingales

A diffusion sampler is useful when averages along the path converge. Martingale theorems are the convergence machine. This chapter defines the continuous martingale, identifies the two martingales built from Brownian motion, and proves L^2 convergence under a bounded second moment.

Definition 1.1 — Martingale

An adapted integrable process M is a martingale when, for s\le t,

\mathbb{E}[M_t\mid\mathcal{F}_s]=M_s.

It is a submartingale when the conditional expectation is at least M_s. All processes in this chapter have continuous paths.

Proposition 1.2 — Two martingales from Brownian motion

W is a martingale. The process W_t^2-t is a martingale.

Proof. For the first claim, write W_t as W_s plus the increment. The increment is independent of the past and has mean 0, so its conditional expectation vanishes.

For the second claim expand the square of W_s plus the increment. The cross term has conditional expectation 0. The square of the increment has conditional expectation t-s. Subtracting t leaves W_s^2-s. ∎

Proposition 1.2 is the model case of a decomposition. The process W_t^2 tends to rise. Subtracting t removes exactly that rise and leaves a martingale. The subtracted process is continuous, starts at 0, and increases.

Theorem 1.3 — Uniqueness in the Doob-Meyer decomposition

Suppose a continuous process admits two writings M+A and M'+A'. Here M and M' are continuous local martingales, and A and A' are continuous, adapted and of finite variation. All four processes start at 0.

Then M=M' and A=A'. In particular, W_t^2 equals a martingale plus the process t, and that writing is the only one.

Proof. The difference M-M' equals A'-A. The left side is a continuous local martingale. The right side is continuous and of finite variation, so its quadratic variation is 0.

A continuous local martingale with quadratic variation 0 is constant. Both sides start at 0, so both sides are 0. Existence of some decomposition for a general continuous submartingale is the existence half of the Doob-Meyer theorem in Revuz and Yor (1999). Uniqueness is the argument above. ∎

The finite-variation process in the decomposition is the quadratic variation when one starts from M^2 and M is a continuous local martingale. For Brownian motion that process is t, often written \langle W\rangle_t=t. For the Itô integral the same role is played by the integral of the square of the integrand.

Theorem 1.4 — L^2 convergence

Let M be a martingale with \sup_t\mathbb{E}[M_t^2]<\infty. Then there exists a square-integrable random variable M_\infty such that M_t converges to M_\infty in L^2.

Proof. Take s<t. The increment M_t-M_s is orthogonal to every square-integrable random variable that is measurable at time s. In particular it is orthogonal to M_s. Expanding the square gives

\mathbb{E}[M_t^2]=\mathbb{E}[M_s^2]+\mathbb{E}[(M_t-M_s)^2].

The map t\mapsto\mathbb{E}[M_t^2] is increasing and bounded, hence convergent. The displayed identity shows that M_t is Cauchy in L^2 as s and t grow. Completeness of L^2 gives the limit. ∎

L^2 convergence implies convergence in probability. Almost sure convergence of a right-continuous martingale bounded in L^1 is Doob’s theorem. Its proof controls upcrossings. We use the statement and leave the upcrossing inequality to Revuz and Yor (1999). Theorem 1.4 is the version used to pass to the limit in the Itô isometry.

Example 1.5 — A bounded time horizon

On the finite interval [0,1], \mathbb{E}[W_1^2]=1, so Theorem 1.4 is not about the limit t\to\infty. It is about Cauchy behaviour when a family of times tends to infinity.

The random variable W_1^2-1 has mean 0 by Proposition 1.2. Its variance equals \mathbb{E}[W_1^4]-1=3-1=2. That second-moment check is the same Gaussian computation as in the variance of S_n in ch. 0.

Optional stopping needs a hypothesis. If \tau is a stopping time bounded by a constant T, then the expectation of W at time \tau is 0.

Approximate \tau by dyadic stopping times and apply Theorem 1.4. The stopped martingale stays bounded in L^2 on the interval up to T.

Unbounded stopping times can fail. The first time W hits level 1 is not integrable, while the process equals 1 there. A naive identity would force that expectation to be both 0 and 1.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • W and W_t^2-t are martingales. The second identity is checked by expanding the increment.
  • A Doob-Meyer writing, when it exists, is unique, because a finite-variation continuous local martingale is constant.
  • The quadratic variation of Brownian motion is \langle W\rangle_t=t.
  • A martingale with bounded second moment converges in L^2. The proof is orthogonality of increments.
  • Stopping at a bounded stopping time preserves the mean. An unbounded hitting time need not.

2. The Itô integral

Ordinary calculus integrates a velocity against dt. Here the integrator has nonzero quadratic variation and unbounded variation, so Riemann-Stieltjes sums depend on where the integrand is sampled inside each interval. The Itô integral samples it at the left endpoint. That choice makes the integral a martingale. This chapter builds the integral for simple integrands and extends it by the isometry.

Definition 2.1 — Simple integrand

Fix a horizon T and times 0=t_0<\cdots<t_n=T. A simple integrand has the form

H_t=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\xi_i\,1_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}(t),

where \xi_i is square-integrable and measurable at time t_i. The Itô integral is the left-endpoint sum below.

I(H)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\xi_i\,(W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}).

The value of H at a single time does not affect the integral.

Theorem 2.2 — Itô isometry for simple integrands

The integral I(H) has mean 0, and

\mathbb{E}[I(H)^2]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt\right].

Proof. Condition each increment on the sigma-algebra at the left endpoint. The increment has conditional mean 0 and is multiplied by a value already known, so each summand has mean 0.

For the square, expand the double sum. A cross term with an earlier interval and a later interval contains a later increment. Condition on the left endpoint of that later interval. The conditional mean of the increment is 0, so the cross term vanishes.

The diagonal term with index i has conditional second moment \xi_i^2 times the length t_{i+1}-t_i. Taking expectations gives the integral of H^2. ∎

Proposition 2.3 — Martingale and quadratic variation

The process I(H)_{\cdot}, stopped at times inside the grid and extended as a constant between grid times in the natural way, is a martingale. Its quadratic variation at time t equals the integral of H_s^2 up to t.

The martingale property is Theorem 2.2 applied on [0,t] conditionally on the past, which kills the future summands. The quadratic variation claim is the Doob-Meyer writing of I(H)^2. The increasing process has to match the right-hand side of the isometry, because the expectation of I(H)_t^2 equals the expectation of that integral.

Simple integrands are dense. Let \mathcal{H} be the set of progressive processes on [0,T] with \mathbb{E}\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt finite. For every element of \mathcal{H} there is a sequence of simple integrands with the same integral of squares converging to 0 in expectation of the L^2 distance. This density is proved by approximating in time and then in the values, in Karatzas and Shreve (1991).

Theorem 2.4 — Extension

The map H\mapsto I(H) extends by continuity to an isometry from \mathcal{H} into L^2. The extension is a martingale up to time T, and its quadratic variation process is the integral of H^2.

Proof. Theorem 2.2 says that the integral is an isometry on the simple integrands, and those integrands are dense. A Cauchy sequence for the energy norm is therefore Cauchy in L^2 after integration. Define the integral of H as that L^2 limit.

The limit does not depend on the approximating sequence, because the isometry controls the difference.

The martingale property passes to the limit by Theorem 1.4 along a fixed sequence of times. The quadratic variation passes to the limit because the isometry identifies \mathbb{E}[I(H)_t^2] with \mathbb{E}\int_0^t H^2. ∎

Left endpoints matter. The same sums with the integrand evaluated at the right endpoint differ from I(H) by a sum of \xi increments times \Delta W, and that difference converges to half the quadratic covariation. For H=W the difference is visible in closed form in ch. 3. The Itô choice is the one that keeps conditional mean 0.

Example 2.5 — A deterministic integrand

Take H_t=t on [0,1]. The integrand is continuous and adapted. Theorem 2.4 gives

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\int_0^1 t\,dW_t\right)^2\right]=\int_0^1 t^2\,dt=\frac13.

The mean of the integral is 0. Exercise 5 asks for this computation from the simple case, by approximating t with left-endpoint step functions. The Riemann sums for \int t^2\,dt converge to 1/3, and the isometry passes to the limit.

Two warnings close the construction. First, the isometry controls L^2, not pathwise size. A single path of the integral can be large while the root mean square stays moderate. Second, if \mathbb{E}\int H^2 is infinite, the object may still exist as a local martingale, by stopping when the integral of H^2 reaches level n. Local martingales are not always martingales. Girsanov’s exponential in ch. 4 is the place where that distinction becomes a hypothesis.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The Itô integral of a simple process is the left-endpoint sum against Brownian increments.
  • Cross terms vanish by conditioning, which is the proof of the isometry.
  • The integral is a martingale. Its quadratic variation is the integral of H^2.
  • Progressive integrands with finite energy \mathbb{E}\int H^2 are limits of simple integrands.
  • Right-endpoint sums differ by a quadratic covariation term. ch. 3 computes it for H=W.

3. Itô’s formula and diffusion equations

The integral of ch. 2 is a martingale when there is no drift. A smooth function of Brownian motion is not a martingale. The second-order term in Taylor’s formula survives, because squared increments sum to time rather than to 0. Itô’s formula is that correction. Stochastic differential equations, generators and the Kolmogorov equations are the same correction written as a drift.

Formula 3.1 — Itô for Brownian motion

For f twice continuously differentiable,

f(W_t)=f(0)+\int_0^t f'(W_s)\,dW_s+\frac12\int_0^t f''(W_s)\,ds.

The first integral is the Itô integral. The second is an ordinary integral.

Theorem 3.2 — Itô’s formula

Formula 3.1 holds for every f\in C^2(\mathbb{R}). If f also depends smoothly on time, add the integral of the time derivative.

Proof. On a uniform partition, Taylor expansion with a Lagrange remainder gives a first-order piece, a second-order piece and a remainder.

The first-order piece is a left-endpoint sum. It converges to the Itô integral of f'(W) by Theorem 2.4, once f' is continuous and the path is continuous, so the integrand is in \mathcal{H} on a bounded time interval after a standard localisation.

The second-order piece is a sum of f''(W_{t_i}) times the squared increment. Proposition 0.2 replaces the squared increments by the mesh t/n when the coefficient is constant. For a continuous coefficient the same replacement holds, because f''(W) is uniformly continuous on the range of the path up to the first exit from a large ball. That is the key step.

The sum of remainders tends to 0 in probability because the cubic variation of Brownian motion is 0. Localisation removes the exit from the ball, and the general case is in Revuz and Yor (1999). ∎

Example 3.3 — The integral of W against W

Apply Formula 3.1 to f(x)=x^2. Then f'=2x and f''=2, so

W_t^2=2\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s+t.

Rearrangement gives the left-endpoint integral of W in closed form,

\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s=\frac{W_t^2-t}{2}.

The right-hand side has mean 0, in agreement with Proposition 1.2. The isometry gives a second check. The variance equals \mathbb{E}\int_0^t W_s^2\,ds=\int_0^t s\,ds=t^2/2.

A stochastic differential equation specifies the drift and the diffusion coefficient of X. In one dimension,

dX_t=b(t,X_t)\,dt+\sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t,

with a deterministic square-integrable initial condition. A strong solution is an adapted continuous process satisfying the integral form of this equation on the given probability space, with the given W.

Definition 3.4 — Strong solution

Fix a Brownian motion W on a given filtered space and a square-integrable initial condition. A continuous adapted process X is a strong solution of the one-dimensional equation when, for every t,

X_t=X_0+\int_0^t b(s,X_s)\,ds+\int_0^t \sigma(s,X_s)\,dW_s.

The integrands have to satisfy the square-integrability used in ch. 2, on each finite horizon.

Theorem 3.5 — Lipschitz strong solutions

Suppose b and \sigma are Lipschitz in the space variable, uniformly in time, and of linear growth. Then the equation has a unique strong solution on [0,\infty).

The proof is Picard iteration in the Banach space of adapted processes with finite \sup_{t\le T}\mathbb{E}[X_t^2], on successive horizons T. The Lipschitz constant makes the iteration a contraction for a small horizon, and linear growth prevents explosion. The estimates are written in Karatzas and Shreve (1991) and in Øksendal (2003). Pathwise uniqueness is the part ch. 5 reuses.

Example 3.5 — Ornstein-Uhlenbeck

Fix \gamma>0 and \sigma\in\mathbb{R}, and consider dX=-\gamma X\,dt+\sigma\,dW with X_0=x. Set Z_t=e^{\gamma t}X_t. Itô’s formula for the product, which is Formula 3.1 applied to (t,x)\mapsto e^{\gamma t}x, cancels the drift and leaves

Z_t=x+\sigma\int_0^t e^{\gamma s}\,dW_s.

Therefore

X_t=e^{-\gamma t}x+\sigma\int_0^t e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\,dW_s.

The stochastic integral is Gaussian with mean 0. Its variance equals \sigma^2\int_0^t e^{-2\gamma(t-s)}\,ds by the isometry. The mean of X_t is e^{-\gamma t}x, and the variance is \sigma^2(1-e^{-2\gamma t})/(2\gamma).

With \gamma=1, \sigma=1 and x=1, the mean at time 1 is e^{-1}\approx 0.367879. Exercise 6 compares an Euler scheme with this value.

The generator of the diffusion is the second-order operator

Lf=b\,\partial_x f+\frac12\sigma^2\,\partial_{xx}f.

Itô’s formula says that f(X_t)-f(X_0)-\int_0^t Lf(X_s)\,ds is a local martingale. For the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion, b(x)=-\gamma x and the diffusion coefficient is the constant \sigma.

Proposition 3.6 — Kolmogorov backward equation

Let \varphi be bounded and continuous. Suppose u(t,x) is bounded and of class C^{1,2} on [0,T)\times\mathbb{R}, with u(T,\cdot)=\varphi, and

\partial_t u+Lu=0.

Then u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_T)\mid X_t=x].

Proof. Apply Itô’s formula to u(s,X_s) from t to T. The time derivative and the generator cancel by the partial differential equation, so the drift integral vanishes. The remaining Itô integral is a martingale because u and its derivatives are bounded in this statement.

Taking the conditional expectation at time t kills the martingale and leaves u(t,X_t)=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X_T)\mid\mathcal{F}_t]. The Markov property of the strong solution replaces the conditioning on \mathcal{F}_t by conditioning on X_t. ∎

The forward equation describes the density p_t of X_t, when it exists. With enough smoothness,

\partial_t p=-\partial_x(bp)+\frac12\partial_{xx}(\sigma^2 p).

This is the Fokker-Planck equation. It is the adjoint of \partial_t+L acting on densities. For the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process the density is the Gaussian law computed in Example 3.5, and it satisfies the equation by direct differentiation. ch. 7 uses the same adjoint computation to see which drift leaves a prescribed density invariant.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Itô’s formula adds one half of f''(W_s) because squared increments sum to time.
  • The identity \int W\,dW=(W_t^2-t)/2 matches both the martingale W_t^2-t and the isometry.
  • Lipschitz drift and diffusion coefficients give a unique strong solution by Picard iteration.
  • The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean decays as e^{-\gamma t}, and the variance has the closed form above.
  • A solution of \partial_t u+Lu=0 is the conditional expectation of a terminal cost.

4. Girsanov and change of measure

Two diffusions with the same diffusion coefficient and different drifts are often absolutely continuous on a finite horizon. The Radon-Nikodym derivative is an exponential martingale. Changing measure removes the drift from the Brownian motion, or puts a drift in. Likelihood ratios and importance sampling are this change of measure applied to path space.

Definition 4.1 — Exponential martingale

For a constant \theta\in\mathbb{R} and a horizon T, set

Z_t=\exp\!\left(\theta W_t-\frac12\theta^2 t\right),\qquad 0\le t\le T.

Itô’s formula applied to the exponential shows that dZ=\theta Z\,dW. There is no dt term. So Z is a local martingale. For a constant \theta the Novikov condition in Revuz and Yor (1999) holds, and Z is a true martingale. In particular \mathbb{E}[Z_T]=1.

Theorem 4.2 — Girsanov, constant drift

Define a probability Q by dQ=Z_T\,dP. Under Q, the process

\widetilde W_t=W_t-\theta t

is a standard Brownian motion on [0,T].

Proof. The exponential is a martingale of mean 1, so the changed measure is a probability. The product rule shows that this exponential, times the shifted process, has no drift under the original measure.

The shifted process is therefore a local martingale under the new measure.

The quadratic variation of \widetilde W is still t, because a finite-variation drift does not change quadratic variation. Lévy’s characterisation, in Revuz and Yor (1999), says that a continuous local martingale with quadratic variation t is a Brownian motion. ∎

The same statement holds for a progressive process \theta_t when the exponential

Z_T=\exp\!\left(\int_0^T \theta_s\,dW_s-\frac12\int_0^T \theta_s^2\,ds\right)

is a martingale. A sufficient condition is Novikov’s integrability condition on \theta^2. The proof is the one above, with \theta W_t replaced by the Itô integral of \theta.

Read the theorem as a drift removal. Under the original measure, W has drift 0. Under the new measure it has drift \theta, because one adds \theta t back to a Brownian motion. The density of the new measure is Z_T.

Proposition 4.3 — Likelihood for a constant drift

Let P_\mu be the law of \mu t+W_t on [0,T], and let P_0 be Wiener measure. Then

\frac{dP_\mu}{dP_0}=\exp\!\left(\mu W_T-\frac12\mu^2 T\right),

where the right-hand side is evaluated on the coordinate process. For \mu=1 and T=1, a path that ends at 0 has likelihood e^{-1/2}\approx 0.606531.

For two diffusion coefficients that coincide and two drifts b and b+\sigma\theta, the same exponential, written with the Itô integral against the driving noise, is the Radon-Nikodym derivative on path space up to time T, provided it is a martingale. This is the likelihood ratio between two diffusion laws. It is the observation model used when a path is seen continuously and the unknown is the drift.

Example 4.4 — Importance sampling

Suppose the goal is \mathbb{E}_{P_\mu}[\varphi] for a bounded functional \varphi of the path, and paths are cheap to draw under P_0. Proposition 4.3 rearranges into

\mathbb{E}_{P_\mu}[\varphi]=\mathbb{E}_{P_0}[\varphi\, Z_T].

The weight is the likelihood. The identity is useful when \varphi is supported on paths that are rare under P_0 and typical under P_\mu. The variance of \varphi Z_T is the figure of merit. A bad drift makes Z_T huge on a few draws and the estimator unstable, even though the identity stays unbiased.

The symmetric form estimates a Wiener expectation by draws from P_\mu, with weight 1/Z_T. Both forms are the definition of absolutely continuous probabilities. Nothing here changes the diffusion coefficient. A change of \sigma usually destroys absolute continuity on path space.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • The exponential Z has differential \theta Z\,dW and mean 1 when \theta is constant.
  • Under dQ=Z_T\,dP, Brownian motion with drift \theta becomes driftless after subtracting \theta t.
  • The likelihood of a constant drift \mu against Wiener measure is the same exponential.
  • A path with W_1=0 and \mu=1 has likelihood about 0.606531.
  • Importance sampling reweights paths by that likelihood. Absolute continuity fails if the diffusion coefficient changes.

5. Martingale problems and weak solutions

Theorem 3.5 builds a strong solution from a given Brownian motion. Several equations have a solution only after the probability space is chosen together with the noise. The martingale problem describes the law of X without naming W. Weak and strong solutions are the two ways of naming it.

Definition 5.1 — Martingale problem

Let L be a second-order operator with continuous coefficients, and fix a starting point. A probability on continuous paths solves the martingale problem for L when the path starts at that point and, for every test function f of class C^2 with compact support,

f(X_t)-f(x)-\int_0^t Lf(X_s)\,ds

is a martingale under P. The coordinate process is X.

Proposition 5.2 — Weak solutions solve the martingale problem

Suppose the pair (X,W) is a weak solution of the equation with drift b(X) and diffusion coefficient \sigma(X), started at x. Assume those coefficients are continuous and of linear growth.

Let L be the generator from ch. 3. Then the law of X solves the martingale problem for L started at x.

Proof. Itô’s formula applies pathwise to f(X) for f\in C_c^2. The drift that appears is exactly Lf(X). Compact support makes the stochastic integral a true martingale, not only a local one. The law of X therefore satisfies Definition 5.1. ∎

The converse uses martingale representation. When \sigma does not vanish, one can rebuild a Brownian motion from the local martingales f(X)-\int Lf and obtain a weak solution on an extension of the space. Under the conditions of Stroock and Varadhan (1979), bounded continuous coefficients and uniform ellipticity, the martingale problem is well posed. Existence and uniqueness in law hold, and they are equivalent to weak existence and uniqueness in law for the stochastic differential equation.

Definition 5.3 — Weak and strong solutions

A strong solution is adapted to the completed filtration of the given Brownian motion and the initial condition. A weak solution is a pair (X,W) on some probability space satisfying the integral equation, with W a Brownian motion and X adapted to a filtration for which W is still a Brownian motion.

Pathwise uniqueness means that two strong solutions driven by the same W, on the same space, are indistinguishable.

Theorem 5.4 — Yamada-Watanabe

If a weak solution exists and pathwise uniqueness holds, then a strong solution exists and the law of the solution is unique.

The theorem is proved by realising two copies on one space with the same noise and using pathwise uniqueness to force them to coincide. Uniqueness in law is the consequence. The reference proof is Yamada and Watanabe (1971), also given in Karatzas and Shreve (1991). Lipschitz coefficients satisfy pathwise uniqueness, which is why Theorem 3.5 produces a strong solution.

Example 5.5 — Tanaka’s equation

Consider dX=\mathrm{sgn}(X)\,dW with the convention \mathrm{sgn}(0)=1. The square of the diffusion coefficient is identically 1, so the generator is half the second derivative, the same generator as Brownian motion.

Proposition 5.2 says that any weak solution is a solution of the martingale problem for that generator. By Lévy’s characterisation it is a Brownian motion in law. A weak solution exists. Take a Brownian motion B and set the driving noise to be the integral of \mathrm{sgn}(B) against B. That noise is a Brownian motion, again by Lévy, and B solves the equation in the weak sense.

There is no strong solution. The proof uses local time and is given in Revuz and Yor (1999). Pathwise uniqueness fails as well. Theorem 5.4 is sharp. Weak existence alone does not give a strong solution.

The modelling consequence is practical. A sampler that needs a path adapted to a given noise source needs a strong solution, or a scheme that builds the noise and the state together. A theorem that only controls the law of the sample, such as a bound in Kullback-Leibler divergence, needs only the martingale problem. ch. 6 and ch. 7 live at that second level once the score or the potential is fixed.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • The martingale problem constrains the law of X through f(X)-\int Lf, with no Brownian motion in the statement.
  • Every weak solution induces a solution of the martingale problem. The proof is Itô’s formula.
  • Under Stroock-Varadhan conditions the martingale problem is well posed, hence the law is unique.
  • Pathwise uniqueness plus weak existence produce a strong solution and uniqueness in law.
  • Tanaka’s equation has the Brownian generator and a weak solution, and no strong solution.

6. Diffusion models

A diffusion model turns a data law into noise with a forward stochastic differential equation, then draws new samples by running time backward. The reverse drift depends on the score, the gradient of the log-density of the law at each time. This chapter computes that drift for Gaussian data and states the Kullback-Leibler guarantee that matches the official reference.

Let \beta>0 be constant and let the forward process solve

dX_t=-\frac12\beta X_t\,dt+\sqrt{\beta}\,dW_t.

This is the variance-preserving Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation. Example 3.5 applies with \gamma=\beta/2 and diffusion coefficient \sqrt{\beta}. The stationary variance equals 1, because \beta/(2\gamma)=1.

Proposition 6.1 — Gaussian marginals

If X_0 is Gaussian with mean m and variance v_0, independent of W, then X_t is Gaussian. Its mean and variance are

m_t=e^{-\beta t/2}m, \qquad v_t=e^{-\beta t}v_0+(1-e^{-\beta t}).

The proof is Example 3.5. The conditional law of X_t given X_0 is Gaussian with mean e^{-\beta t/2}X_0 and variance 1-e^{-\beta t}. The marginal variance is the conditional variance plus the variance of the conditional mean.

Definition 6.4 — Score

If a law on the line has a positive smooth density p, its score is the derivative of \log p. In several dimensions the score is the gradient of \log p. The normalising constant of p disappears, because it does not depend on the state.

The score of a non-degenerate Gaussian is an affine function. At time t,

\nabla\log p_t(x)=-\frac{x-m_t}{v_t}.

Anderson (1982) constructs a reverse-time diffusion with the same marginals. Write \bar X_u=X_{T-u} for u\in[0,T], and assume the density of X_t is smooth and positive. Then \bar X solves a stochastic differential equation whose drift at the moment when the original clock reads t is

-\,b(t,x)+\beta\,\nabla\log p_t(x),

with b(t,x)=-\beta x/2 the forward drift, plus a new Brownian term with the same diffusion coefficient \sqrt{\beta}. Substituting b gives the reverse drift

\frac12\beta x+\beta\nabla\log p_t(x).

The sign is the one checked in the next example. Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon and Poole (2021) use this time reversal as the sampling equation, with \nabla\log p_t replaced by a learned approximation.

Example 6.2 — Equilibrium is reversible

Take m=0 and v_0=1. Then m_t=0 and v_t=1 for every t, and the score equals -x. The reverse drift collapses to

\frac12\beta x+\beta(-x)=-\frac12\beta x,

which is the forward drift. A stationary Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is reversible, so the check had to succeed.

The probability-flow ordinary differential equation of Song and coauthors uses the drift b-\frac12\beta\nabla\log p_t. In this equilibrium case that drift is identically 0. The marginal is already N(0,1) at every time, so the deterministic flow does not need to move.

For a general Gaussian the same algebra is an explicit sampler. No neural network is required. Draw the state at a large horizon from a law that is nearly standard normal, and integrate the reverse equation down to time 0 with the affine score.

The law at time 0 is the original Gaussian in continuous time. Discretisation is the only error. Exercise 9 asks for the mean, the variance and the reversibility check.

Learning enters when p_0 is unknown. Hyvärinen (2005) estimates a score by score matching, without the normalising constant. Vincent (2011) connects that objective to denoising. The practical systems of Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan and Ganguli (2015) and of Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020) train a denoising network on a discrete noising chain. The continuous limit of the variance-preserving chain is the equation at the start of this chapter.

Theorem 6.3 — KL bounds under finite Fisher information

Conforti, Durmus and Gentiloni Silveri (2025) consider score-based samplers whose forward process is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion, or its kinetic counterpart, discretised by an exponential integrator with fixed step. If the data law has finite Fisher information relative to the standard Gaussian, and if the score error is measured in L^2(p_t), then the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the data law and the law of the sampler admits an explicit bound.

The bound does not assume that the score or its estimator is Lipschitz in time. The article is SIAM Journal on Mathematics of Data Science, volume 7, number 1, pages 86–109. We do not copy the constants. The structural point is the hypothesis. Finite Fisher information replaces an early-stopping cutoff and a compactness assumption on the support.

Proof. The argument in the article compares the Fokker-Planck equation of the approximate reverse process with the Fokker-Planck equation of the true reverse process. The Kullback-Leibler divergence along the two flows produces a differential inequality. The score error enters as a source term. Finite Fisher information keeps the coefficients integrable down to time 0. The details, including the kinetic case, are in the paper. ∎

A wrong score is a wrong drift. Theorem 4.2 says that a drift error changes the path measure by an exponential weight. Theorem 6.3 turns that path-space gap into a gap on the law at the terminal time, which is the sample the user sees. The Gaussian calculation of Example 6.2 is the case with zero score error. The divergence is then only the discretisation error of the integrator.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • The variance-preserving forward equation is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion with stationary variance 1.
  • Gaussian data stay Gaussian. The mean decays as e^{-\beta t/2} and the variance is an explicit convex combination.
  • The score of that Gaussian is affine. Anderson’s reverse drift is \beta x/2+\beta\nabla\log p_t.
  • At equilibrium the reverse drift equals the forward drift, and the probability-flow drift is 0.
  • Conforti, Durmus and Gentiloni Silveri (2025) bound the Kullback-Leibler error under finite Fisher information and an L^2 score error.

7. Langevin sampling and the unadjusted Langevin algorithm

The target is a density \pi on \mathbb{R}^d, known up to a constant. Overdamped Langevin dynamics choose the drift \nabla\log\pi so that \pi is invariant. The unadjusted Langevin algorithm is the Euler scheme of that dynamics. The scheme is biased for every fixed step. On a standard Gaussian the bias is an exact formula.

Durmus and Moulines (2019) write the diffusion as

dY_t=-\nabla U(Y_t)\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dB_t,

where \pi(x)\propto e^{-U(x)}. Then -\nabla U=\nabla\log\pi. In one dimension the Fokker-Planck equation of ch. 3 becomes

\partial_t p=\partial_x\!\big(p\,(U)'\big)+\partial_{xx}p.

Proposition 7.1 — Invariance

If p=\pi\propto e^{-U}, the right-hand side above is 0, so \pi is a stationary density.

Proof. For this density, (\log\pi)'=-(U)', hence \pi(U)'=-(\pi)'. The first term of the Fokker-Planck operator is \partial_x(-(\pi)')=-(\pi)''. The second term is (\pi)''. They cancel. ∎

Ellipticity gives uniqueness of the stationary law when U is smooth and confining. The sampler does not need the normalising constant of \pi, because the drift depends on U only through \nabla U.

Definition 7.5 — Unadjusted Langevin algorithm

Choose a step h>0 and an independent sequence of standard Gaussian vectors. The unadjusted Langevin algorithm is the Markov chain in Formula 7.2. It is the Euler-Maruyama discretisation of the overdamped Langevin diffusion, and it does not include a Metropolis filter.

Formula 7.2 — Unadjusted Langevin step

With step h>0 and \xi_{k+1} a standard Gaussian vector, independent of the past,

X_{k+1}=X_k-h\nabla U(X_k)+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_{k+1}.

This is the Euler-Maruyama scheme of the Langevin equation. Durmus and Moulines (2019) call it the unadjusted Langevin algorithm.

Theorem 7.3 — Non-asymptotic bounds under strong convexity

Assume U is continuously differentiable, \nabla U is globally Lipschitz, and U is strongly convex. Durmus and Moulines (2019) prove non-asymptotic bounds on the Wasserstein-2 distance and the total variation distance between the law of X_k and \pi.

The bounds cover a constant step and a decreasing step, and the dimension appears explicitly. For a constant step the chain has its own stationary law \pi_h, and \pi_h is not \pi in general. The paper is Bernoulli, volume 25, number 4A, pages 2854–2882.

Proof. The article couples the Euler chain with the continuous Langevin diffusion and estimates the discretisation error over one step, then sums a geometric contraction coming from strong convexity. We do not reproduce the coupling. The Gaussian case below makes the bias \pi_h\neq\pi visible without estimates. ∎

Example 7.4 — Exact bias on the standard Gaussian

Take d=1 and U(x)=x^2/2, so \pi is standard normal and \nabla U(x)=x. The step reduces to the autoregression

X_{k+1}=(1-h)X_k+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_{k+1}.

A stationary variance v has to satisfy v=(1-h)^2 v+2h. For h\in(0,2) the coefficient 1-h has absolute value strictly less than 1, and

v=\frac{2}{2-h}.

At h=0.2 the stationary variance equals 2/1.8=10/9\approx 1.111. The target variance is 1. The excess is the bias of the Euler scheme. As h\to 0 the variance tends to 1, which matches the continuous invariance in Proposition 7.1.

For h\ge 2 the autoregression has no stationary probability with a finite variance. The step is then too large for this potential, even though \nabla U is Lipschitz.

The limitations follow from the hypotheses and from the example.

  • Strong convexity rules out potentials with several wells. The contraction used by Durmus and Moulines (2019) need not hold, and crossing a low-density region is a waiting time the Euler bias formula does not see.
  • Global Lipschitz continuity of \nabla U rules out superlinear drifts. A step that is safe for a quadratic U can make a heavier-tailed target explode.
  • Constant-step laws are biased. Metropolis-Hastings adjustment, the MALA scheme of Roberts and Tweedie (1996), uses the Euler move as a proposal and rejects with the usual ratio. The invariant law is then exactly \pi, at the price of rejected moves.
  • Decreasing steps, also treated by Durmus and Moulines (2019), drive the bias to 0 while keeping a non-asymptotic Wasserstein bound.

An underdamped extension adds a velocity V. One standard form is dX=V\,dt and

dV=-\gamma V\,dt-\nabla U(X)\,dt+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,dB.

The invariant law is \pi(dx) times a Gaussian N(0,1) on the velocity, for the friction \gamma>0 written here. The check is a Fokker-Planck computation in the pair (x,v), given in Pavliotis (2014). Conforti, Durmus and Gentiloni Silveri (2025) analyse the kinetic analogue on the generative-model side. The overdamped equation is the one the official lecture list puts first.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • The drift -\nabla U makes \pi\propto e^{-U} stationary for the overdamped Langevin diffusion. The proof is one cancellation in Fokker-Planck.
  • The unadjusted Langevin algorithm is Euler-Maruyama with step h and noise variance 2h.
  • On the standard Gaussian the stationary variance of the chain is 2/(2-h) for h\in(0,2), equal to 10/9 at h=0.2.
  • Durmus and Moulines (2019) bound the Wasserstein and total variation error when U is strongly convex and \nabla U is Lipschitz.
  • MALA removes the bias by a Metropolis filter. Decreasing steps are the other extension in that paper. Underdamped dynamics add a velocity.

8. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is self-study, outside the 24 h. It records what the two official research references prove, and four questions they leave open. The voice is that of a reader of the papers. Nothing here is a claim about unpublished work.

8.1 What is proved for score-based samplers

Conforti, Durmus and Gentiloni Silveri (2025) bound the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the data distribution and the law of a score-based sampler. The forward process is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion, or a kinetic Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion. The reverse process is discretised by an exponential integrator with a fixed step.

The hypothesis on the data is finite Fisher information relative to the standard Gaussian. The hypothesis on the algorithm is an L^2(p_t) bound on the score error. Under those two hypotheses the paper gives an explicit KL bound, without a uniform Lipschitz assumption on the score and without stopping the reverse process short of time 0.

Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon and Poole (2021) define the sampling equation that this analysis discretises. Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020) define the discrete variance-preserving chain whose continuous limit is the forward equation of ch. 6. Anderson (1982) is the time-reversal step. Hyvärinen (2005) and Vincent (2011) are the estimation step that produces a score from data.

8.2 What is proved for the unadjusted Langevin algorithm

Durmus and Moulines (2019) bound the Wasserstein-2 distance and the total variation distance between the law of the unadjusted Langevin algorithm and the target \pi\propto e^{-U}. The potential U is strongly convex, and \nabla U is globally Lipschitz. Constant steps and decreasing steps are both covered, and the dimension enters the bounds explicitly.

The constant-step chain is allowed to have a biased stationary law. Example 7.4 computes that bias on the standard Gaussian. The article also bounds the mean square error of a weighted empirical measure for bounded measurable test functions, and it illustrates the algorithm on Bayesian binary regression. Roberts and Tweedie (1996) analyse the Metropolis-adjusted version, which removes the bias.

8.3 Open problems

  • Potentials that are not strongly convex. Theorem 7.3 uses strong convexity for the contraction. A posterior with several modes is outside that hypothesis. The waiting time to cross a low-density region is not described by the stationary variance 2/(2-h) of Example 7.4. Quantitative mixing bounds for the overdamped dynamics in that regime remain a separate theory.
  • The statistical cost of the score. Theorem 6.3 takes an L^2 score error as an input. It does not say how many samples a network needs, in dimension d, to reach that error. The KL bound is conditional on estimation. The estimation rate is open in the same generality as the finite-Fisher hypothesis.
  • Data with infinite Fisher information. The 2025 hypothesis fails for a law supported on a lower-dimensional set, and for some laws with very sharp peaks. Early stopping was the older device for those laws. Which singularities still allow a KL bound, and at what stopping time, is not settled by the finite-Fisher theorem.
  • Integrator versus probability flow, at a fixed budget. Theorem 6.3 analyses one exponential integrator. The probability-flow ordinary differential equation of ch. 6 uses the same score and no Brownian increment. For a multimodal Gaussian mixture and a fixed number of score evaluations, the paper does not rank the stochastic integrator against the ordinary differential equation. A numerical ranking on a mixture with a known score is a concrete test, and it is not a corollary of the theorem.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • The 2025 KL bounds need finite Fisher information and an L^2 score error, not a Lipschitz score.
  • The 2019 ULA bounds need a strongly convex potential and a Lipschitz gradient, and they allow a biased stationary law.
  • Multimodal potentials, score estimation rates, singular data, and the choice of integrator are outside those statements.

The subjects and the exam below are study proposals. They are not the official marking scheme, and they do not assign a weight to the written exam.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Exact Gaussian bias against the 2019 bounds. This paper study reads Durmus and Moulines, Bernoulli 25(4A), 2854–2882, 2019, and compares their constant-step bias with Example 7.4.

    • Implement: Formula 7.2 for U(x)=|x|^2/2 in dimensions 1 and 20, for the steps h\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2\}, from X_0=0, for 10^4 steps after a burn-in of 10^3, over 50 independent runs with a fixed seed.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 3, ch. 7. Evaluation: the report and the talk, against the success criterion.
    • Success: in dimension 1 the empirical variance matches 2/(2-h) within 0.01. In dimension 20 each coordinate variance matches the same number within 0.02.

  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Reverse drift with a known Gaussian score. Reproduce the continuous-time sampler of Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon and Poole, ICLR 2021, in the case where Proposition 6.1 gives the score in closed form.

    • Implement: the variance-preserving equation with \beta=1, data law N(2,0.25), horizon T=4, and both the reverse diffusion and the probability-flow equation, with 2000 steps and 2\cdot 10^4 terminal samples.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 3, ch. 4, ch. 6. Evaluation: the report and the talk, against the success criterion.
    • Success: the sample mean lies within 0.05 of 2 and the sample variance within 0.05 of 0.25, for the reverse diffusion. Report the same two numbers for the probability-flow equation.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] KL hypothesis on a Gaussian, from the 2025 paper. Read Conforti, Durmus and Gentiloni Silveri, SIAM Journal on Mathematics of Data Science 7(1), 86–109, 2025. Restate the KL bound in the notation of ch. 6, then test a deliberate score error on a Gaussian.

    • Split: one member writes the hypothesis list and the differential inequality for the KL. One member implements the reverse equation of Example 6.2 with the score replaced by -(1+\varepsilon)x. One member computes the KL between the terminal Gaussian and N(0,1) from the sample mean and variance.
    • Deliverables: code, a 6 page report, a 15-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 4, ch. 6, ch. 8. Evaluation: the report, the talk, and a short oral question to each member.
    • Success: for \varepsilon=0 the estimated KL is below 0.01. For \varepsilon=0.05 and \varepsilon=0.1 the KL increases. The report states whether that increase is consistent with the source term in the paper.

  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Discrete DDPM chain against the reverse SDE. Use Ho, Jain and Abbeel, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 2020, on a synthetic mixture, with the continuous limit from ch. 6.

    • Data: 10^4 independent draws from the equal mixture of N(-2,0.3^2) and N(2,0.3^2) in one dimension. No image dataset.
    • Split: one member implements the discrete variance-preserving chain and a small denoising network. One member implements the Euler scheme of the reverse equation using the same noise schedule and the same network as the score. One member reports histograms, mode weights and the number of score evaluations.
    • Deliverables: code, a 6 page report, a 15-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 6, ch. 7. Evaluation: the report, the talk, and the mode-weight table.
    • Success: both samplers put a weight within 0.05 of 1/2 on each half-line. The report compares the two histograms at equal number of network evaluations.

Written exam (proposal)

This block is a study proposal for a two-hour exam with no documents. It is not the official paper. The fiche assessment stays a written exam, and no official weight is stated. Four questions, 20 points.

  1. Brownian motion and quadratic variation (5 points). State the definition of a standard Brownian motion. Give \langle W\rangle_t and one Hölder statement about the paths. Say where the quadratic variation enters Itô’s formula.

    Expected: the four properties in Definition 0.2. Quadratic variation t, and Hölder regularity of every order strictly less than 1/2. In Formula 3.1 the second-order term is present because squared increments sum to t rather than to 0.

  2. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck and one Langevin step (6 points). For dX=-X\,dt+dW with X_0=1, compute \mathbb{E}[X_1] and \mathrm{Var}(X_1). Then write the unadjusted Langevin step for the standard Gaussian and its stationary variance as a function of h\in(0,2).

    Expected: mean e^{-1} and variance (1-e^{-2})/2, from Example 3.5. The step is (1-h)X+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi, and the stationary variance is 2/(2-h), from Example 7.4.

  3. A short proof (5 points). Prove the identity below from Itô’s formula. Deduce from the Itô isometry that the second moment equals t^2/2.

    \int_0^t W_s\,dW_s=(W_t^2-t)/2.

    Expected: Apply Formula 3.1 to f(x)=x^2. The derivatives are 2x and the constant 2, so W_t^2 equals twice the integral plus t.

    The isometry says the second moment equals the integral of s up to time t, which is t^2/2.

  4. Girsanov weight (4 points). State Girsanov’s theorem for a constant drift \theta. Write the weight that estimates the expectation of a bounded path functional under that drift, using Wiener paths.

    Expected: Under the changed measure with density Z_T, the shifted process W_t-\theta t is Brownian motion. The estimation identity is the display below, as in Example 4.4.

    \mathbb{E}_{P_\theta}[\varphi]=\mathbb{E}_{P_0}[\varphi Z_T].

Points: 5+6+5+4=20.

Grading rubric (proposal)

This rubric is for self-marking the study proposals above. It is not an official weight for the written exam.

Criterion Weight What earns the marks
Definitions and theorem statements 30% The hypotheses are named, the conclusion matches the chapter, and the object (path, law or algorithm) is the one in the question
Computations 25% The algebra reaches the closed form in the relevant example, and the numerical check uses the printed value or the exact constant from the chapter
Proofs 25% The key cancellation or conditioning step is written, and the appeal to a named theorem is the one that applies
Modelling judgement 20% The answer says whether the gap comes from discretisation, from the score, or from a failed hypothesis such as strong convexity
