Empirical first: a safety study needs a failure probability near 10^{-4}, and a plain Monte Carlo estimate with 10 % accuracy needs about a million runs of the simulator — over a century of computing at one hour per run (Example 0.5). On a classical benchmark, 50 well-chosen runs and a Gaussian-process surrogate recover a probability of 4.4 \cdot 10^{-3} to two significant digits (Example 4.12).

This module is about replacing a costly numerical model by a cheap one without losing control of the error. It follows three routes: acting on the solver itself (model reduction), learning its input–output map from a few runs (Gaussian processes, polynomial chaos, neural networks), and injecting physics into learning (constrained kernels, PINNs, neural operators).

Around these routes it builds the tools that make a surrogate usable: designs of experiments, sensitivity analysis, Bayesian calibration, data assimilation and certified uncertainty statements. It shares kernels with Kernel Methods for Machine Learning, Bayesian inference with Bayesian Machine Learning and inverse problems with Inverse Problems and Imaging.

0. Review: SVD, Gaussian conditioning, Monte Carlo and discretised PDEs

Four M1 tools carry the whole module. The singular value decomposition builds reduced bases, Gaussian conditioning builds kriging and the Kalman filter, Monte Carlo is what every surrogate tries to make affordable, and a discretised PDE is the typical expensive simulator.

This chapter states them in the form used later, with proofs when they are short. The refreshers on linear algebra and probability give more background.

Singular values and best low-rank approximation

Definition 0.1 — Singular value decomposition Every real matrix A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times n} factorises as A = U \Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{\min(N,n)} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^\top , with U and V orthogonal and singular values \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots \geq 0.

The left singular vectors u_i are the eigenvectors of A A^\top, and \sigma_i^2 are its eigenvalues.

Theorem 0.2 — Eckart–Young Let A_r = \sum_{i \le r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top be the truncated SVD. For every matrix B of rank at most r, \|A - B\|_F^2 \;\geq\; \|A - A_r\|_F^2 \;=\; \sum_{i > r} \sigma_i^2 .

Proof. Weyl’s inequality for singular values states that, for all matrices A, B and indices i, j \geq 1, \sigma_{i+j-1}(A) \leq \sigma_i(A - B) + \sigma_j(B).

Take j = r+1. Since B has rank at most r, \sigma_{r+1}(B) = 0, hence \sigma_{i+r}(A) \leq \sigma_i(A-B) for every i.

Squaring and summing over i gives \|A - B\|_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i(A-B)^2 \geq \sum_{k > r} \sigma_k(A)^2 .

Finally A - A_r = \sum_{i>r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top, whose squared Frobenius norm is exactly that sum. ∎

Gaussian vectors and conditioning

Proposition 0.3 — Conditioning a Gaussian vector Let (Z_1, Z_2) be a Gaussian vector with means m_1, m_2 and covariance blocks \Sigma_{11}, \Sigma_{12}, \Sigma_{22}, with \Sigma_{22} invertible. Then Z_1 given Z_2 = z_2 is Gaussian: Z_1 \mid Z_2 = z_2 \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\big(m_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(z_2 - m_2),\; \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\big).

Proof. Set W = Z_1 - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1} Z_2. A direct computation gives \mathrm{Cov}(W, Z_2) = \Sigma_{12} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{22} = 0 .

The pair (W, Z_2) is Gaussian, as a linear image of a Gaussian vector, so zero covariance means independence.

Write Z_1 = W + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1} Z_2. Given Z_2 = z_2, the second term is a constant and W keeps its law.

That law has mean m_1 - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1} m_2 and covariance \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}, which gives the result. ∎

The conditional mean is linear in the data and the conditional covariance does not depend on the observed value. Kriging (ch. 3) and the Kalman filter (ch. 9) are this proposition applied to different vectors.

Monte Carlo and the cost of rare events

Proposition 0.4 — Monte Carlo error Let X_1, \dots, X_N be independent copies of X and Y = f(X) square-integrable. The estimator \hat I_N = \frac1N \sum_j f(X_j) of \mathbb{E}[Y] is unbiased, with variance \mathrm{Var}(Y)/N, and the central limit theorem gives the asymptotic 95 % interval \hat I_N \pm 1.96\, \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sqrt N}.

For a probability p = \mathbb{P}(f(X) > s), take Y as the indicator of the event. The coefficient of variation of the estimator is then \delta_N = \sqrt{\frac{1 - p}{N p}} .

The rate N^{-1/2} does not depend on the input dimension, which is why Monte Carlo is the reference method in uncertainty quantification. Its price is the number of calls to f.

Example 0.5 — A century of computing Take p = 10^{-4} and a target coefficient of variation of 10 %.

  • The formula gives N = (1-p)/(0.01\,p) \approx 10^6 runs.
  • At one hour per run, this is 10^6 hours, that is about 114 years of sequential computing.

The conclusion of this module follows: the simulator must be replaced, at least partly, by something cheaper.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Monte Carlo estimate of a small probability p = P(Z > 3.09)
p_true = norm.sf(3.09)
for N in [10**3, 10**4, 10**5, 10**6]:
    z = rng.standard_normal(N)
    p_hat = np.mean(z > 3.09)
    cv = np.sqrt((1 - p_true) / (N * p_true))      # coefficient of variation
    print(f"N={N:>8d}  p_hat={p_hat:.2e}  true={p_true:.2e}  c.o.v.={cv:.2f}")

# Eckart-Young: truncated SVD error equals the discarded singular values
A = rng.standard_normal((200, 50)) @ np.diag(0.7 ** np.arange(50)) @ rng.standard_normal((50, 80))
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
r = 5
A_r = U[:, :r] @ np.diag(s[:r]) @ Vt[:r]
print("Frobenius error  :", np.linalg.norm(A - A_r))
print("sqrt(sum s_i^2), i>r:", np.sqrt(np.sum(s[r:] ** 2)))

Code 0.6 — What the code shows With p \approx 10^{-3}, a thousand draws see no failure at all and return \hat p = 0. Ten thousand draws give 8 \cdot 10^{-4}, with a coefficient of variation of 32 %.

The two printed Frobenius errors agree to machine precision (29.9170), as Theorem 0.2 predicts.

Discretised PDEs: the typical simulator

Definition 0.7 — Model elliptic problem and its discretisation On (0,1), with a conductivity \kappa(x) \geq \kappa_0 > 0 and a source f, find u with u(0) = u(1) = 0 and -(\kappa u')' = f .

The weak form seeks u \in H^1_0 such that a(u, v) = \ell(v) for every test function v, with a(u, v) = \int_0^1 \kappa\, u' v' \,dx, \qquad \ell(v) = \int_0^1 f v \,dx .

Finite differences on N interior nodes, or Galerkin finite elements, turn it into a linear system A u = b. The matrix A is symmetric positive definite, sparse and of size N.

The bilinear form is coercive: a(v,v) \geq \kappa_0 \|v'\|^2_{L^2}. The Lax–Milgram theorem then gives existence and uniqueness. Céa’s lemma states that the Galerkin solution is the best approximation in the energy norm, a fact reproved for reduced models in ch. 2.

Real simulators couple such systems in three dimensions, with nonlinear terms and time stepping. A single run then takes minutes to days, which is the regime where a metamodel pays off.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • The truncated SVD is the best rank-r approximation, with error equal to the discarded singular values (Theorem 0.2).
  • Conditioning a Gaussian vector gives a mean linear in the data and a covariance independent of the data (Proposition 0.3).
  • Monte Carlo converges at rate N^{-1/2} in any dimension, but a probability p costs about 100/p runs for 10 % accuracy.
  • A discretised elliptic PDE is a large sparse SPD system; repeated solves are what we want to avoid.

1. From the numerical model to the metamodel

A simulator is a map from inputs to outputs that is costly to evaluate. This chapter fixes the vocabulary, gives the questions that decide which surrogate to build, and shows a first way of injecting physics before any learning: dimensional analysis.

Simulators, metamodels and the structuring questions

Definition 1.1 — Simulator A simulator is a deterministic map f : \mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{Y}, computed by a numerical code at a cost c per run. The inputs x gather physical parameters, boundary and initial conditions and geometric parameters.

The output space \mathcal{Y} may be \mathbb{R} (a scalar quantity of interest), \mathbb{R}^m (a discretised field or a time series) or a function space (a whole solution).

Definition 1.2 — Metamodel, surrogate, emulator A metamodel is a map \hat f that approximates f at a much lower cost per evaluation. It is built either from the equations of the solver, or from a design of runs \mathcal{D}_n = \{(x_i, f(x_i)),\ i = 1, \dots, n\}, which the course calls distilled simulations: the knowledge of the code is distilled into a data set.

The three words are synonyms here; emulator is common in statistics, surrogate in engineering.

The course description asks a few structuring questions before any method. They form the checklist below, used in every recap.

Key result — the five questions to ask before building a surrogate

  1. Target: what exactly is approximated — a scalar output, a field, a trajectory, the solution operator?
  2. Physics: where does physical knowledge enter — inputs, prior, loss, architecture, projection?
  3. Access: can we act inside the solver (intrusive) or only run it (black box)?
  4. Cost: what is the training cost (runs plus fitting) and the inference cost per evaluation?
  5. Guarantees: what can be certified on accuracy, robustness, interpretability and uncertainty?

Definition 1.3 — The three routes of metamodelling

  • Intrusive or semi-intrusive: the discretised equations are projected onto a small basis (POD, reduced bases, Galerkin projection, ch. 2). This needs access to the operators of the solver.
  • Non-intrusive: the simulator is a black box, and the input–output map is learned from distilled runs (Gaussian processes, polynomial chaos, neural networks, ch. 3–6).
  • Hybrid or physics-informed: physical constraints, operator structure or equation residuals enter the learning (constrained GPs, PINNs, neural operators, ROM + deep learning, ch. 4, 7, 8).

What a surrogate is used for

The same surrogate rarely serves all purposes, so the use must be fixed first:

  • propagation: compute the law of Y = f(X) for random inputs X, or summaries of it;
  • sensitivity analysis: rank the inputs by their influence on Y (ch. 5);
  • optimisation and design: find the best x, possibly under uncertainty (ch. 4 and ch. 11);
  • calibration and inverse problems: infer parameters from measurements (ch. 9);
  • real-time use: digital twins, monitoring and control, where each evaluation has a deadline.

Definition 1.4 — Quantities of interest and sources of uncertainty With X random of law P_X and Y = f(X), the usual quantities of interest are the mean and variance of Y, a quantile q_\beta, and an exceedance probability p_s = \mathbb{P}(f(X) > s).

Uncertainty is aleatory when it comes from intrinsic variability (loads, material scatter) and epistemic when it comes from lack of knowledge (few runs, unknown parameters, model error). Only the second kind shrinks with more information.

Industrial practice organises a study in steps: specify the model and the quantity of interest, quantify the input uncertainties, propagate them, then rank the inputs (de Rocquigny, Devictor and Tarantola, 2008). A metamodel enters at the propagation and ranking steps, and brings its own error into the budget.

Proposition 1.5 — What a surrogate error costs Let \hat f be a surrogate and \varepsilon > 0. For the mean, \big|\mathbb{E} f(X) - \mathbb{E} \hat f(X)\big| \leq \mathbb{E}\, |f(X) - \hat f(X)|.

For an exceedance probability, the error is controlled by the mass near the threshold and the probability of a large surrogate error: \big|\mathbb{P}(f(X) > s) - \mathbb{P}(\hat f(X) > s)\big| \leq \mathbb{P}(|f(X) - s| \leq \varepsilon) + \mathbb{P}(|f(X) - \hat f(X)| > \varepsilon).

Proof. The first bound is Jensen’s inequality for the absolute value.

For the second, the two probabilities differ at most by the probability of the symmetric difference of the events \{f > s\} and \{\hat f > s\}.

Take x with f(x) > s \geq \hat f(x). Either f(x) \leq s + \varepsilon, so |f(x) - s| \leq \varepsilon, or f(x) > s + \varepsilon, so f(x) - \hat f(x) > \varepsilon.

The case \hat f(x) > s \geq f(x) is symmetric. ∎

The second bound is the reason for the adaptive designs of ch. 4: to estimate a failure probability, the surrogate needs to be accurate only near the level set \{f = s\}, not everywhere.

Physics at the input level: dimensional analysis

Before learning anything, physics can shrink the input space. A physical law cannot depend on the choice of units, and this invariance removes inputs.

Theorem 1.6 — Buckingham’s Π theorem Let a physical relation q_0 = F(q_1, \dots, q_k) hold in every system of units, among k+1 quantities whose dimension matrix has rank m.

Then there are k + 1 - m independent dimensionless products \Pi_0, \dots, \Pi_{k-m} of powers of the q_j, and a function \Phi with \Pi_0 = \Phi(\Pi_1, \dots, \Pi_{k-m}).

Proof. Sketch (Buckingham, 1914; Barenblatt, 1996, ch. 1). Write the dimension of q_j as a vector a_j of exponents over the base units (length, mass, time, …).

A product \prod_j q_j^{\lambda_j} is dimensionless exactly when \sum_j \lambda_j a_j = 0, that is when \lambda lies in the kernel of the dimension matrix. By the rank–nullity theorem, that kernel has dimension k + 1 - m.

Choose m quantities with independent dimensions as a basis. Rescaling the base units changes these m quantities freely while every \Pi stays fixed.

Since F is valid in every system of units, the relation can only link the \Pi’s. ∎

Example 1.7 — The period of a pendulum The period T may depend on the length L, the gravity g, the mass M and the release angle \theta_0.

  • Five quantities, three base dimensions (length, mass, time), so two groups: T\sqrt{g/L} and \theta_0.
  • The mass cannot appear in any group with T, so it drops out.
  • Hence T = \sqrt{L/g}\; \Phi(\theta_0), with a single unknown function of one variable.

For this pendulum the function is known in closed form, with K the complete elliptic integral of the first kind: \Phi(\theta_0) = 4\, K\big(\sin^2(\theta_0/2)\big), \qquad \Phi(\theta_0) \to 2\pi \text{ as } \theta_0 \to 0 .

A surrogate of T over four inputs becomes a surrogate of \Phi over one input: a few runs suffice.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.special import ellipk

def simulator(L, g, theta0):
    """'Expensive' simulator: period of a pendulum by ODE integration."""
    rhs = lambda t, y: [y[1], -(g / L) * np.sin(y[0])]
    # the pendulum released at rest crosses theta = 0 after a quarter period
    hit = lambda t, y: y[0]
    hit.terminal, hit.direction = True, -1
    sol = solve_ivp(rhs, [0, 100], [theta0, 0.0], events=hit, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
    return 4 * sol.t_events[0][0]

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
L = rng.uniform(0.5, 2.0, 6)            # length (m)

2. Low-dimensional structure, POD and reduced-order models

A parametrised PDE is solved again and again for new parameter values, but its solutions often live close to a space of small dimension. This chapter measures that dimension, builds the best linear basis from snapshots, and projects the equations on it: the intrusive route.

The solution manifold and its width

Consider the discretised parametric problem A(\mu)\, u(\mu) = b(\mu), with u(\mu) \in \mathbb{R}^{N_h} and parameters \mu in a compact set \mathcal{P} \subset \mathbb{R}^p. The solution manifold is the set of all solutions: \mathcal{M} = \{u(\mu) : \mu \in \mathcal{P}\}.

Definition 2.1 — Kolmogorov n-width The Kolmogorov n-width of \mathcal{M} is the worst-case error of the best n-dimensional linear space: d_n(\mathcal{M}) = \inf_{\dim V_n = n}\ \sup_{u \in \mathcal{M}}\ \inf_{v \in V_n} \|u - v\| .

When d_n decays fast, a few basis vectors represent every solution and linear reduction works. For elliptic problems depending smoothly on a few parameters, the decay is very fast, often exponential (Cohen and DeVore, 2015).

For transport-dominated problems the decay is slow. For the family of indicator functions of shifted intervals in L^2, the width decays no faster than n^{-1/2} (Ohlberger and Rave, 2016). A moving front needs many linear modes, a limitation that motivates the nonlinear reductions of ch. 6.

Proper orthogonal decomposition

Definition 2.2 — Snapshots and POD basis Solve the full model for n_s training parameters and store the snapshot matrix S = \big[u(\mu_1), \dots, u(\mu_{n_s})\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{N_h \times n_s}.

A POD basis of size r is a matrix V \in \mathbb{R}^{N_h \times r} with orthonormal columns that minimises the total projection error \sum_j \|u(\mu_j) - V V^\top u(\mu_j)\|^2.

Theorem 2.3 — POD is the truncated SVD Let S = U \Sigma W^\top be an SVD. The first r left singular vectors U_r form an optimal POD basis, and the minimal error is the discarded energy: \min_{V^\top V = I_r} \sum_{j=1}^{n_s} \|u(\mu_j) - V V^\top u(\mu_j)\|^2 = \sum_{i > r} \sigma_i^2 .

Proof. The total error equals \|S - V V^\top S\|_F^2, and V V^\top S has rank at most r. By Eckart–Young (Theorem 0.2), the error is at least \sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2.

For V = U_r, the projection U_r U_r^\top S equals the truncated SVD S_r, because U_r^\top u_i = 0 for i > r. The bound is attained. ∎

In practice, the number of modes is chosen by an energy criterion. For a tolerance \varepsilon, take the smallest r such that \sum_{i \leq r} \sigma_i^2 \;\geq\; (1 - \varepsilon) \sum_{i} \sigma_i^2 .

Two practical remarks. With a finite-element mass matrix M, the relevant norm is \|u\|_M = \sqrt{u^\top M u}, and one applies the theorem to M^{1/2} S.

When N_h is much larger than n_s, it is cheaper to diagonalise the small n_s \times n_s correlation matrix S^\top M S: this is the method of snapshots of Sirovich (1987).

Galerkin projection and the offline–online split

Definition 2.4 — Galerkin reduced-order model Look for u(\mu) \approx V a(\mu) with a(\mu) \in \mathbb{R}^r, and impose that the residual be orthogonal to the reduced space: V^\top A(\mu) V\, a(\mu) = V^\top b(\mu).

This is an r \times r system instead of an N_h \times N_h one.

Proposition 2.5 — Best approximation in the energy norm If A(\mu) is symmetric positive definite, the Galerkin solution is the best approximation of u(\mu) in the reduced space for the energy norm \|v\|_A = \sqrt{v^\top A v}: \|u - V a\|_A = \min_{c \in \mathbb{R}^r} \|u - V c\|_A .

Proof. Subtract V^\top A V a = V^\top b from V^\top A u = V^\top b: the error e = u - Va satisfies V^\top A e = 0, Galerkin orthogonality.

For any c, write u - Vc = e + V(a - c). The cross term 2 (a-c)^\top V^\top A e vanishes, so \|u - Vc\|_A^2 = \|e\|_A^2 + \|V(a-c)\|_A^2 \geq \|e\|_A^2 . ∎

The reduced model is only fast if the projected operator can be formed without touching size-N_h objects online. This holds under an affine decomposition A(\mu) = \sum_{q=1}^{Q} \theta_q(\mu)\, A_q, \qquad b(\mu) = \sum_{q=1}^{Q'} \theta'_q(\mu)\, b_q .

Offline, compute the small matrices V^\top A_q V and vectors V^\top b_q once. Online, for each new \mu, assemble the reduced system in O(Q r^2) operations and solve it in O(r^3), independently of N_h.

Proposition 2.6 — A posteriori error bound Let r(\mu) = b(\mu) - A(\mu) V a(\mu) be the residual of the reduced solution, computable without u(\mu). If A(\mu) is SPD with smallest eigenvalue \lambda_{\min}(\mu), then \|u(\mu) - V a(\mu)\|_2 \leq \frac{\|r(\mu)\|_2}{\lambda_{\min}(\mu)} .

Proof. The error solves A(\mu)\,(u - Va) = r(\mu), so u - Va = A(\mu)^{-1} r(\mu), and \|A(\mu)^{-1}\|_2 = 1/\lambda_{\min}(\mu). ∎

In practice \lambda_{\min}(\mu) is replaced by a cheap lower bound, and the residual norm is also split offline–online thanks to the affine decomposition.

Definition 2.7 — Greedy reduced-basis algorithm Start from one snapshot. At each step, evaluate the error bound of Proposition 2.6 over a large training set of parameters, solve the full model at the worst parameter, add the snapshot to the basis after orthonormalisation, and stop when the bound is below the tolerance.

The bound makes the basis certified: the error is guaranteed on the training set, without any extra full solve (Hesthaven, Rozza and Stamm, 2016).

Nonlinear terms and hyper-reduction For a nonlinear term g(Va), the projection V^\top g(Va) still costs O(N_h) online. The discrete empirical interpolation method (DEIM, Chaturantabut and Sorensen, 2010) approximates g in a POD basis U_m of its own snapshots, from only m selected entries: g(Va) \approx U_m (P^\top U_m)^{-1} P^\top g(Va), where P selects m rows. Only m entries of g are evaluated online. For time-dependent problems, the same projection applied to \dot u = F(u, \mu) gives a small system of ODEs (Benner, Gugercin and Willcox, 2015).

A worked reduced model

Example 2.8 — A heat-conduction problem with four parameters Solve -(\kappa u')' = 1 on (0,1) with zero boundary values. The conductivity is one plus four Gaussian bumps of weights \mu_q \in [0, 20]: \kappa(x;\mu) = 1 + \sum_{q=1}^{4} \mu_q\, e^{-((x - c_q)/0.15)^2}, \qquad c_q \in \{0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}.

The finite-difference matrix is affine in \mu with Q = 5 terms. The full model has N_h = 2000 unknowns; 80 snapshots train the POD basis, and 40 new parameters test it.

import time
import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp
import scipy.sparse.linalg as spla

# -(kappa u')' = 1 on (0,1), u(0)=u(1)=0, kappa(x; mu) = 1 + sum_q mu_q phi_q(x)
N = 2000                                   # interior nodes (full model size)
h = 1.0 / (N + 1)
xm = (np.arange(N + 1) + 0.5) * h          # midpoints of the N+1 cells
phi = [np.ones_like(xm)] + [np.exp(-((xm - c) / 0.15) ** 2) for c in (0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8)]

def stiffness(w):                          # FD matrix of -(w u')' with cell weights w
    w = w / h**2
    return sp.diags([w[:-1] + w[1:], -w[1:-1], -w[1:-1]], [0, 1, -1], format="csc")

A_q = [stiffness(p) for p in phi]          # affine terms: A(mu) = A_0 + sum mu_q A_q
theta = lambda mu: np.concatenate([[1.0], mu])
f = np.ones(N)
full = lambda mu: spla.spsolve(sum(t * A for t, A in zip(theta(mu), A_q)), f)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
draw = lambda n: rng.uniform(0, 20, size=(n, 4))          # mu in [0, 20]^4
S = np.column_stack([full(mu) for mu in draw(80)])        # snapshot matrix
U, s, _ = np.linalg.svd(S, full_matrices=False)
print("sigma_i / sigma_1:", np.array2string(s[:10] / s[0], precision=1))

test = draw(40)
t0 = time.perf_counter(); U_true = [full(mu) for mu in test]; t_full = time.perf_counter() - t0
for r in [2, 4, 6, 8, 10]:
    V = U[:, :r]
    A_r = [V.T @ (A @ V) for A in A_q]                      # offline: r x r blocks
    f_r = V.T @ f
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    U_rom = [V @ np.linalg.solve(sum(t * Ar for t, Ar in zip(theta(mu), A_r)), f_r) for mu in test]
    t_rom = time.perf_counter() - t0
    err = max(np.linalg.norm(a - b) / np.linalg.norm(a) for a, b in zip(U_true, U_rom))
    print(f"r={r:2d}: max relative error {err:.1e}, online speed-up x{t_full / t_rom:.0f}")

Code 2.9 — Reading the output

  • The normalised singular values decay quickly: 1, 9.5 \cdot 10^{-2}, 1.9 \cdot 10^{-2}, then 4 \cdot 10^{-4} at the tenth.
  • The worst relative error over the 40 test parameters is 8.4 \cdot 10^{-2} for r = 2, 1.9 \cdot 10^{-2} for r = 6 and 1.3 \cdot 10^{-3} for r = 10.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • The Kolmogorov width measures how well any n-dimensional linear space can represent the solution manifold; it decays fast for diffusion and slowly for transport.
  • POD is the truncated SVD of the snapshot matrix, and its error is the discarded energy (Theorem 2.3).
  • Galerkin projection gives the best reduced approximation in the energy norm for SPD problems (Proposition 2.5); an affine decomposition makes it independent of N_h online.
  • The residual gives a computable error bound, which drives the greedy reduced-basis algorithm.
  • Balance sheet: intrusive; physics kept exactly through projection; very fast online with certified errors; needs the solver’s operators and an affine (or hyper-reduced) structure; fails on slowly decaying widths.

3. Gaussian-process metamodels from distilled simulations

With only tens or hundreds of runs, we need a surrogate that interpolates the runs, behaves smoothly in between and says how uncertain it is. Gaussian-process regression, called kriging in geostatistics and computer experiments (Matheron, 1963; Sacks, Welch, Mitchell and Wynn, 1989), does all three.

Gaussian processes and kernels

Definition 3.1 — Gaussian process A random function F on the input space is a Gaussian process with mean m and covariance kernel k, written F \sim \mathcal{GP}(m, k), if for all points x_1, \dots, x_n the vector of values F(x_i) is Gaussian with \text{mean } (m(x_i))_{i}, \qquad \text{covariance matrix } K = (k(x_i, x_j))_{i,j} .

A function k is a valid covariance exactly when every such matrix is positive semi-definite.

The kernel encodes what we believe about f before any run. Two families dominate computer experiments, written here with r = \|x - x'\|/\ell, a variance \sigma^2 and a length scale \ell: k_{\mathrm{SE}}(x, x') = \sigma^2 e^{-r^2/2}, \qquad k_{5/2}(x, x') = \sigma^2 \Big(1 + \sqrt5\, r + \tfrac53 r^2\Big) e^{-\sqrt5\, r}.

The squared-exponential kernel gives infinitely differentiable sample paths, often too smooth for physics. The Matérn 5/2 kernel gives paths that are twice differentiable in mean square, and is the usual default. With one length scale \ell_j per input, the fitted scales also indicate which inputs matter.

The kriging equations

Theorem 3.2 — Kriging predictor and variance Let F \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k) and observe y = (f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n)) without noise, with K invertible. Write k_n(x) = (k(x, x_i))_i. Given the data, F(x) is Gaussian with m_n(x) = k_n(x)^\top K^{-1} y, \qquad s_n^2(x) = k(x,x) - k_n(x)^\top K^{-1} k_n(x).

Proof. The vector (F(x), F(x_1), \dots, F(x_n)) is Gaussian with covariance blocks k(x,x), k_n(x)^\top and K.

Proposition 0.3, with Z_1 = F(x) and Z_2 = y, gives both formulas. ∎

Three variants are used in practice.

  • Noisy or stochastic codes: replace K by K + \tau^2 I (a nugget). A tiny nugget also stabilises the Cholesky factorisation.
  • Universal kriging: a trend h(x)^\top \beta with unknown \beta replaces the zero mean. Then \beta is estimated by generalised least squares, and the variance gains a term for the estimation of \beta.
  • Vector outputs: a field output is often first compressed by PCA, and one GP is fitted per coefficient (ch. 5).

Proposition 3.3 — Interpolation Without nugget, the predictor interpolates the runs and the variance vanishes there: m_n(x_i) = y_i, \qquad s_n(x_i) = 0 .

Indeed k_n(x_i) is the i-th column of K, so k_n(x_i)^\top K^{-1} = e_i^\top. Both formulas follow at once.

A deterministic error bound

The GP model is a modelling assumption: the simulator is not random. The next theorem gives a deterministic meaning to s_n, through the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) \mathcal{H}_k of the kernel, the completion of the span of the functions k(\cdot, x) with inner product \langle k(\cdot, x), k(\cdot, x')\rangle = k(x, x').

Theorem 3.4 — Kriging error bound in the RKHS Let f \in \mathcal{H}_k and let m_n be the kriging predictor built from the exact values f(x_i). Then, at every point x, |f(x) - m_n(x)| \leq s_n(x)\, \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_k} .

Moreover, m_n is the interpolant of the data with the smallest RKHS norm.

Proof. Set c(x) = K^{-1} k_n(x), so that m_n(x) = \sum_i c_i(x) f(x_i).

By the reproducing property f(x) = \langle f, k(\cdot, x)\rangle, we can write f(x) - m_n(x) = \Big\langle f,\ k(\cdot, x) - \sum_i c_i(x)\, k(\cdot, x_i) \Big\rangle .

Expanding the squared norm of the right-hand function gives k(x,x) - 2 c^\top k_n(x) + c^\top K c, and this equals s_n^2(x) because Kc = k_n(x).

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality concludes. The minimum-norm property is the representer theorem: any interpolant equals m_n plus a function orthogonal to every k(\cdot, x_i). ∎

The bound separates what the design controls, s_n(x), from what the simulator brings, its RKHS norm. Filling the space with design points makes s_n small everywhere (Wendland, 2004), and placing points where s_n is large is the first sequential design of ch. 4.

Estimating the hyperparameters

The kernel parameters \vartheta = (\sigma^2, \ell_1, \dots, \ell_d) are estimated from the runs, usually by maximum likelihood.

Proposition 3.5 — Profile likelihood Write K = \sigma^2 R_\ell, where R_\ell is a correlation matrix. The log-likelihood of the data is \log L = -\tfrac12\, y^\top K^{-1} y - \tfrac12 \log \det K - \tfrac n2 \log(2\pi).

Its maximiser in \sigma^2 is \hat\sigma^2 = y^\top R_\ell^{-1} y / n. Up to constants, it remains to maximise in the length scales -\tfrac n2 \log \hat\sigma^2(\ell) - \tfrac12 \log \det R_\ell .

The first formula is the Gaussian density. Setting the derivative in \sigma^2 to zero gives \hat\sigma^2, and substituting it gives the profile.

The maximisation is non-convex, so it is restarted from several initial points. Each evaluation needs a Cholesky factorisation, in O(n^3) operations: exact GPs suit up to a few thousand runs. Beyond, one uses sparse approximations with inducing points, local GPs, or the neural surrogates of ch. 6.

Validating a GP metamodel

A metamodel is useless without an estimate of its error. Since runs are expensive, the standard tool is leave-one-out (LOO) cross-validation, which is free for GPs.

Proposition 3.6 — Closed-form leave-one-out (Dubrule, 1983) Let m_{-i} and s_{-i}^2 be the kriging predictor and variance built without run i, with the same hyperparameters. Then y_i - m_{-i}(x_i) = \frac{[K^{-1} y]_i}{[K^{-1}]_{ii}}, \qquad s_{-i}^2(x_i) = \frac{1}{[K^{-1}]_{ii}} .

Proof sketch: reorder so that run i comes last and invert K by blocks. The Schur complement of the last entry is s_{-i}^2(x_i), and the last row of K^{-1} gives the residual (Exercise 6).

All n LOO residuals thus cost one matrix inverse instead of n refits. Three diagnostics follow, used again in ch. 9.

Definition 3.7 — Predictivity and calibration diagnostics

  • The predictivity coefficient, on LOO or on a test set, is Q^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}. It equals 1 for a perfect surrogate.
  • The standardised residuals (y_i - m_{-i}(x_i))/s_{-i}(x_i) should look like a standard normal sample if the variance is well calibrated.
  • The coverage of the 95 % bands m_n \pm 1.96\, s_n on test points should be close to 95 %.

Example 3.8 — Nine runs of a one-dimensional code Take the test function of Forrester, Sóbester and Keane (2008), f(x) = (6x - 2)^2 \sin(12x - 4), \qquad x \in [0, 1], which has a deep minimum near x = 0.76. Nine equally spaced runs train a Matérn 5/2 GP, fitted by maximum likelihood with five restarts.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import ConstantKernel, Matern

f = lambda x: (6 * x - 2) ** 2 * np.sin(12 * x - 4)       # Forrester et al. (2008)
X = np.linspace(0, 1, 9)[:, None]                          # 9 "distilled" runs
y = f(X).ravel()

kernel = ConstantKernel(10.0, (1e-2, 1e4)) * Matern(0.2, (1e-2, 10.0), nu=2.5)
gp = GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel, alpha=1e-10, normalize_y=False,
                              n_restarts_optimizer=5, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
print("fitted kernel:", gp.kernel_)

# closed-form leave-one-out (Dubrule, 1983) with the fitted covariance K
K = gp.kernel_(X) + 1e-10 * np.eye(len(X))
Kinv = np.linalg.inv(K)
loo_res = (Kinv @ y) / np.diag(Kinv)                       # y_i - m_{-i}(x_i)
loo_sd = 1 / np.sqrt(np.diag(Kinv))                        # sd of m_{-i}(x_i)
# brute force check on point i = 4 (same hyperparameters, refit without x_4)
i = 4
gp_i = GaussianProcessRegressor(gp.kernel_, alpha=1e-10, optimizer=None)
gp_i.fit(np.delete(X, i, 0), np.delete(y, i))
print("LOO residual, closed form vs refit:", loo_res[i], y[i] - gp_i.predict(X[i:i+1])[0])
print("standardised LOO residuals:", np.round(loo_res / loo_sd, 2))

Xt = np.linspace(0, 1, 500)[:, None]
m, sd = gp.predict(Xt, return_std=True)
Q2 = 1 - np.sum((f(Xt).ravel() - m) ** 2) / np.sum((f(Xt).ravel() - f(Xt).mean()) ** 2)
cover = np.mean(np.abs(f(Xt).ravel() - m) <= 1.96 * sd)
print(f"test Q2 = {Q2:.4f}, empirical coverage of the 95% band = {cover:.2f}")

Code 3.9 — Reading the output

  • The closed-form LOO residual at x_4 is -0.2031, identical to the brute-force refit (Proposition 3.6).
  • The standardised LOO residuals lie between -1.05 and 1.81, a plausible standard normal sample.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Kriging is Gaussian conditioning: a predictor linear in the runs and a variance that depends only on the design (Theorem 3.2).
  • For f in the RKHS of the kernel, the kriging standard deviation bounds the error deterministically (Theorem 3.4).
  • Hyperparameters come from the profile likelihood, at a cost of O(n^3) per evaluation.
  • Leave-one-out residuals and variances come in closed form (Proposition 3.6), and give Q^2, standardised residuals and coverage.
  • Balance sheet: non-intrusive; physics enters only through the kernel; excellent with tens to thousands of runs in moderate dimension; built-in uncertainty; limited by O(n^3) cost, stationarity and dimension.

4. Advanced Gaussian processes: constraints, multifidelity, adaptive enrichment

Chapter 3 treated the simulator as an anonymous function sampled on a fixed design. Three kinds of knowledge are usually available and wasted that way: physical laws the output must satisfy, cheaper approximate codes, and the freedom to choose the next run. This chapter uses all three.

Physical constraints

Linear knowledge — derivatives, integrals, a linear PDE — keeps the Gaussian framework intact.

Proposition 4.1 — Linear operators of a GP Let F \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k) with k smooth enough, and \mathcal{L} a linear operator such as a derivative or an integral. Then (F, \mathcal{L}F) is jointly Gaussian, with \mathrm{Cov}\big(\mathcal{L}F(x), F(x')\big) = \mathcal{L}_x k(x, x'), \qquad \mathrm{Cov}\big(\mathcal{L}F(x), \mathcal{L}F(x')\big) = \mathcal{L}_x \mathcal{L}_{x'} k(x, x').

The covariance of \mathcal{L}F is obtained by applying \mathcal{L} to each argument of the kernel, which is allowed by exchanging \mathcal{L} with the expectation.

Observations of \mathcal{L}f can therefore be added to the data and conditioned on with Proposition 0.3. Typical cases are gradients returned by an adjoint solver, measured fluxes, or the values of a PDE operator at collocation points, a first form of the physics-informed learning of ch. 7.

Proposition 4.2 — Kernels that satisfy the physics If \mathcal{L}_x k(x, x') = 0 for every x', then every kriging predictor built with k satisfies the constraint exactly: \mathcal{L}\, m_n = 0 .

Proof. By Theorem 3.2, m_n = \sum_i \alpha_i\, k(\cdot, x_i) with \alpha = K^{-1} y, a finite combination of functions killed by \mathcal{L}. ∎

Example 4.3 — A kernel of harmonic functions Identify \mathbb{R}^2 with \mathbb{C} and set k(z, w) = \mathrm{Re}\, e^{z \bar w}.

  • The kernel e^{z \bar w} = \sum_j z^j \bar w^j / j! is Hermitian positive definite. For real coefficients, the quadratic form of its real part is the same real number, so k is a valid real kernel.
  • For fixed w, the map z \mapsto e^{z\bar w} is holomorphic, so its real part is harmonic: \Delta_z k(z, w) = 0.

By Proposition 4.2, every predictor built with k solves Laplace’s equation, for instance a steady temperature field interpolated from a few sensors. Divergence-free kernels play the same role for incompressible velocity fields.

Inequality constraints — positivity, bounds, monotonicity, convexity — are not linear and break Gaussianity. Da Veiga and Marrel (2012) impose them at a finite set of virtual points, which leads to a truncated Gaussian posterior handled by sampling.

Definition 4.4 — Finite-dimensional constrained GP Following Maatouk and Bay (2017) and López-Lopera, Bachoc, Durrande and Roustant (2018), approximate the process on hat functions \varphi_j attached to knots t_j: F_N(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \xi_j\, \varphi_j(x), \qquad \xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Gamma), \quad \Gamma_{jl} = k(t_j, t_l).

Since F_N is piecewise linear, bounds, monotonicity and convexity of F_N are equivalent to linear inequalities \xi \in C on the coefficients. The posterior is the law of \xi conditioned on the interpolation equations and on \xi \in C, a truncated Gaussian.

Its mode solves a quadratic programme, and its samples come from truncated-Gaussian samplers.

Multifidelity: borrowing strength from cheap codes

Simulators often come in several fidelities: a coarse mesh and a fine one, a 1D model and a 3D one. Cheap runs are plentiful, expensive ones rare.

Definition 4.5 — Autoregressive multifidelity model (Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2000) With a low-fidelity code f_L and a high-fidelity code f_H, model f_H(x) = \rho\, f_L(x) + \delta(x), with f_L \sim \mathcal{GP}(m_L, k_L) and \delta \sim \mathcal{GP}(m_\delta, k_\delta) independent, and a scale factor \rho.

The joint covariances follow: \mathrm{Cov}(f_H(x), f_L(x')) = \rho\, k_L(x, x'), \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(f_H(x), f_H(x')) = \rho^2 k_L(x,x') + k_\delta(x,x').

Proposition 4.6 — Recursive co-kriging for nested designs Assume the expensive design is included in the cheap one, X_H \subset X_L, and write y_L, y_H for the data. Then \mathbb{E}[f_H(x) \mid y_L, y_H] = \rho\, \mathbb{E}[f_L(x) \mid y_L] + \mathbb{E}\big[\delta(x) \mid \delta(X_H) = y_H - \rho\, f_L(X_H)\big], and the posterior variance is \rho^2 s_L^2(x) + s_\delta^2(x).

Proof. Since X_H \subset X_L, the values f_L(X_H) are part of y_L. Observing y_H is thus equivalent to observing \delta(X_H) = y_H - \rho f_L(X_H).

The information is the pair (y_L, \delta(X_H)), made of two independent blocks: one depends on f_L, the other on \delta.

Conditioning f_L(x) on it uses only y_L, and conditioning \delta(x) uses only \delta(X_H). The two posteriors are independent, so the variances add. ∎

Each level is an ordinary kriging, on its own small data set (Le Gratiet and Garnier, 2014). The scale \rho and any trend of \delta are estimated by least squares or maximum likelihood. Peherstorfer, Willcox and Gunzburger (2018) survey multifidelity methods beyond GPs.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import ConstantKernel as C, Matern

fH = lambda x: (6 * x - 2) ** 2 * np.sin(12 * x - 4)     # expensive code
fL = lambda x: 0.5 * fH(x) + 10 * (x - 0.5) - 5           # cheap code
XL = np.linspace(0, 1, 11)[:, None]                       # 11 cheap runs
XH = XL[[0, 4, 6, 10]]                                    # 4 nested expensive runs
kern = lambda: C(1.0, (1e-3, 1e4)) * Matern(0.2, (1e-2, 10), nu=2.5)
fit = lambda X, y: GaussianProcessRegressor(kern(), alpha=1e-8, normalize_y=True,
                                            n_restarts_optimizer=5, random_state=0).fit(X, y)

gpL = fit(XL, fL(XL).ravel())                             # level 1: cheap code
yH, yLH, xH = fH(XH).ravel(), fL(XH).ravel(), XH.ravel()  # nested design
A = np.column_stack([yLH, np.ones_like(xH), xH])
rho, b0, b1 = np.linalg.lstsq(A, yH, rcond=None)[0]      # rho and a linear trend of delta
gpD = fit(XH, yH - rho * yLH - b0 - b1 * xH)              # level 2: GP on the rest of delta
gpH = fit(XH, yH)                                         # baseline: expensive runs only

Xt = np.linspace(0, 1, 400)[:, None]
pred_mf = rho * gpL.predict(Xt) + b0 + b1 * Xt.ravel() + gpD.predict(Xt)
rmse = lambda p: np.sqrt(np.mean((p - fH(Xt).ravel()) ** 2))
print(f"rho = {rho:.3f}, trend of delta = {b0:.2f} + {b1:.2f} x")
print(f"RMSE, 4 expensive runs only : {rmse(gpH.predict(Xt)):.3f}")
print(f"RMSE, co-kriging 4 + 11 runs: {rmse(pred_mf):.3f}")

Example 4.7 — Four expensive runs, eleven cheap ones The cheap code of Forrester, Sóbester and Keane (2008) is a distorted version of the function of Example 3.8: f_L(x) = \tfrac12 f_H(x) + 10(x - 0.5) - 5 .

  • With a linear trend for \delta, the estimates are \rho = 2.000 and \delta(x) = 20 - 20x, the exact relation.
  • With the four expensive runs alone, the GP has a test RMSE of 5.46.
  • Co-kriging with the eleven cheap runs brings it down to 0.18, a thirty-fold gain for the same expensive budget.

Designs of experiments

Before any adaptivity, the initial runs must cover the input space.

Definition 4.8 — Latin hypercube and maximin designs A Latin hypercube sample (LHS) of size n in [0,1]^d cuts each axis into n equal intervals and places the points so that each interval of each axis holds exactly one point (McKay, Beckman and Conover, 1979).

A maximin design maximises the smallest distance between two points, \min_{i \neq j} \|x_i - x_j\|. A common choice is the best of many random LHS for this criterion. Low-discrepancy sequences, such as Sobol’ sequences, are an alternative.

Proposition 4.9 — Variance of the LHS mean (Stein, 1987) Let X be uniform on [0,1]^d and f square-integrable, with main effects f_j(x_j) = \mathbb{E}[f(X) \mid X_j = x_j] - \mathbb{E} f(X).

The mean of f over an LHS of size n has variance \frac1n \Big(\mathrm{Var} f(X) - \sum_{j=1}^d \mathrm{Var} f_j(X_j)\Big) + o\Big(\frac1n\Big).

Compared with plain Monte Carlo, LHS removes the additive part of the variance. The proof is in Stein (1987).

Sequential design and adaptive enrichment

Definition 4.10 — Sequential design After n runs, choose the next one by maximising an infill criterion computed from the GP posterior, x_{n+1} \in \arg\max_{x}\, J_n(x), then run the simulator at x_{n+1}, update the GP and repeat.

The criterion depends on the goal: J_n = s_n^2 for global accuracy, the expected improvement for optimisation, or a misclassification measure for a failure probability.

Theorem 4.11 — Expected improvement (Jones, Schonlau and Welch, 1998) To minimise f, let y_{\min} be the best value observed. If F(x) is \mathcal{N}(m, s^2) with s > 0, the expected improvement is \mathrm{EI}_n(x) = \mathbb{E}\big[(y_{\min} - F(x))^+\big] = (y_{\min} - m)\,\Phi(z) + s\,\varphi(z), \qquad z = \frac{y_{\min} - m}{s}, where \Phi and \varphi are the standard normal distribution and density functions.

Proof. Write F(x) = m + s U with U standard normal. The improvement is positive exactly when U < z, so \mathrm{EI}_n(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{z} s\,(z - u)\,\varphi(u)\,du .

The first part gives s z\, \Phi(z) = (y_{\min} - m)\Phi(z).

For the second, \int_{-\infty}^{z} u\, \varphi(u)\, du = -\varphi(z) because \varphi'(u) = -u\varphi(u), which gives + s\varphi(z). ∎

The first term rewards a low predicted value (exploitation), the second a large uncertainty (exploration). Maximising EI repeatedly is the EGO algorithm, the standard tool for optimising expensive codes and the starting point of Bayesian optimisation.

To estimate a failure probability p = \mathbb{P}(g(X) \leq 0), only the sign of g matters. The GP misclassifies the sign of g(x) with probability \Phi(-U_n(x)), where U_n(x) = \frac{|m_n(x)|}{s_n(x)} .

The AK-MCS method of Echard, Gayton and Lemaire (2011) draws a Monte Carlo population of inputs once. It repeatedly runs g at the population point with the smallest U_n, and stops when every U_n \geq 2, that is when no sign is wrong with probability above \Phi(-2) \approx 2.3 %.

Stepwise uncertainty reduction (SUR) strategies choose instead the point that most reduces the expected future uncertainty on p itself (Bect, Ginsbourger, Li, Picheny and Vazquez, 2012). Christen and Ray (2011) review related active-learning designs.

import warnings
import numpy as np
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import ConstantKernel as C, RBF
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")

def g(x):                                   # four-branch series system, failure if g <= 0
    a, b = x[:, 0], x[:, 1]
    return np.min([3 + 0.1 * (a - b) ** 2 - (a + b) / np.sqrt(2),
                   3 + 0.1 * (a - b) ** 2 + (a + b) / np.sqrt(2),
                   (a - b) + 6 / np.sqrt(2), (b - a) + 6 / np.sqrt(2)], axis=0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pool = rng.standard_normal((20_000, 2))                    # Monte Carlo population, X ~ N(0, I)
pf_ref = np.mean(g(pool) <= 0)                             # 20 000 calls to g

X = rng.uniform(-5, 5, (12, 2)); y = g(X)                  # initial design: 12 calls
kernel = C(1.0, (1e-2, 1e3)) * RBF([1.0, 1.0], (1e-1, 1e2))
for it in range(100):
    opt = "fmin_l_bfgs_b" if it % 5 == 0 else None         # re-estimate every 5 steps
    gp = GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel, alpha=1e-8, normalize_y=True,
                                  optimizer=opt).fit(X, y)
    kernel = gp.kernel_
    m, s = gp.predict(pool, return_std=True)
    U = np.abs(m) / np.maximum(s, 1e-12)                   # U-function of AK-MCS
    if U.min() >= 2:                                       # stopping rule of Echard et al.
        break
    x_new = pool[np.argmin(U)][None]                       # most ambiguous point
    X, y = np.vstack([X, x_new]), np.append(y, g(x_new))
print(f"reference P_f = {pf_ref:.3e} with 20000 calls")
print(f"AK-MCS    P_f = {np.mean(m <= 0):.3e} with {len(y)} calls")

Example 4.12 — The four-branch benchmark The limit state g is the minimum of four branches, a classical test for reliability methods, with two standard normal inputs and a failure domain made of four regions.

  • Plain Monte Carlo on the 20 000 points of the population gives p = 4.45 \cdot 10^{-3}.
  • AK-MCS stops after 50 runs of g (12 initial and 38 adaptive) with \hat p = 4.40 \cdot 10^{-3}.

By construction, the adaptive runs are placed where the sign of g is ambiguous, that is near the limit state \{g = 0\}. This is what Proposition 1.5 asks for: accuracy is needed only there.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Linear physics (derivatives, integrals, linear PDEs) enters a GP exactly, either as data or through the kernel (Propositions 4.1–4.2); inequality constraints lead to truncated Gaussians.
  • Co-kriging combines many cheap runs and few expensive ones; with nested designs it splits into two ordinary krigings (Proposition 4.6).
  • Initial designs should fill the space (LHS, maximin); LHS removes the additive part of the Monte Carlo variance.
  • Expected improvement has a closed form (Theorem 4.11) and balances exploitation and exploration; AK-MCS and SUR target failure probabilities.
  • Balance sheet: constraints and fidelities make GPs more data-efficient; adaptive designs need a model whose variance can be trusted, and remain sequential, which limits parallel computing.

5. Polynomial chaos, functional representations and global sensitivity

Which inputs drive the output, and through which interactions? This chapter answers with a variance decomposition, then builds a surrogate — polynomial chaos — whose coefficients give that decomposition for free. It ends with functional inputs and outputs; low-rank formats and dependent inputs are complements in the self-study ch. 11.

The Hoeffding–Sobol decomposition

Throughout, X = (X_1, \dots, X_d) has independent components, Y = f(X) is square-integrable, and for u \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\} we write X_u = (X_j)_{j \in u}.

Theorem 5.1 — Functional ANOVA (Hoeffding, Sobol) There is a unique decomposition f(X) = \sum_{u \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}} f_u(X_u), with f_\emptyset = \mathbb{E} f(X) and \mathbb{E}[f_u(X_u) \mid X_{u \setminus \{j\}}] = 0 for every u \neq \emptyset and j \in u. Its terms are pairwise orthogonal and given by f_u = \sum_{v \subseteq u} (-1)^{|u| - |v|}\, \mathbb{E}[f(X) \mid X_v].

Proof. Existence. Set g_v = \mathbb{E}[f(X) \mid X_v] and define f_u by the formula. Möbius inversion on the subsets gives \sum_{u} f_u = g_{\{1,\dots,d\}} = f(X).

Zero conditional means. By independence, for v \subseteq u, \mathbb{E}[g_v \mid X_{u \setminus \{j\}}] = g_{v \setminus \{j\}} . Pair each v \ni j with v \setminus \{j\}: the two terms carry opposite signs and cancel.

Orthogonality. For u \neq v, take j in one set and not the other, say j \in u \setminus v. Conditioning on all variables except X_j gives \mathbb{E}[f_u f_v] = \mathbb{E}\big[f_v\, \mathbb{E}[f_u \mid X_{u \setminus\{j\}}]\big] = 0 .

Uniqueness. The difference of two such decompositions is a zero sum of orthogonal terms, so each term vanishes. ∎

Orthogonality turns the decomposition into a split of the variance, \mathrm{Var}\, Y = \sum_{u \neq \emptyset} D_u with D_u = \mathrm{Var} f_u(X_u).

Definition 5.2 — Sobol indices The Sobol index of a group u is S_u = D_u / \mathrm{Var}\,Y. The first-order and total indices of input i are S_i = \frac{\mathrm{Var}\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i]}{\mathrm{Var}\, Y}, \qquad S_{T_i} = \sum_{u \ni i} S_u = \frac{\mathbb{E}\, \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid X_{-i})}{\mathrm{Var}\, Y}.

S_i measures the effect of X_i alone; S_{T_i} adds all its interactions. An input with S_{T_i} \approx 0 can be fixed at a nominal value.

Proposition 5.3 — Pick-freeze estimation Let X' be an independent copy of X, and let Y^{i} be computed at the input that keeps X_i and takes the other components from X'. Then \mathrm{Cov}(Y, Y^{i}) = \mathrm{Var}\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i].

Proof. Given X_i, the outputs Y and Y^{i} are independent with the same conditional law. Hence \mathbb{E}[Y Y^{i}] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i]^2\big], and both outputs have mean \mathbb{E}\, Y. ∎

Replacing the covariance and the variance by empirical versions gives a consistent estimator of S_i (Sobol, 2001; Janon, Klein, Lagnoux, Nodet and Prieur, 2014). It costs N(d+1) runs, too many for an expensive code but cheap on a surrogate (Exercise 8).

Definition 5.4 — High-dimensional model representation (HDMR) When interactions of order three and more are negligible, truncating Theorem 5.1 gives a surrogate built from functions of one and two variables (Rabitz and Aliş, 1999): f(x) \approx f_\emptyset + \sum_{i} f_i(x_i) + \sum_{i < j} f_{ij}(x_i, x_j).

Cut-HDMR replaces the conditional expectations by slices through a reference point, which needs only one-dimensional and two-dimensional sweeps of the code.

Polynomial chaos expansions

Definition 5.5 — Polynomial chaos basis For each input law P_j, let (\psi^{(j)}_k)_{k \geq 0} be the orthonormal polynomials of L^2(P_j): Hermite polynomials for a standard Gaussian, Legendre polynomials for a uniform law, and the other families of the Askey scheme (Xiu and Karniadakis, 2002). For a multi-index \alpha \in \mathbb{N}^d, set \psi_\alpha(x) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} \psi^{(j)}_{\alpha_j}(x_j).

The total-degree truncation keeps the multi-indices with |\alpha| \leq p, that is \binom{d+p}{p} terms.

Theorem 5.6 — Completeness If each P_j has moments of all orders and is determined by them, as for Gaussian, uniform, beta and gamma laws, the family (\psi_\alpha) is an orthonormal basis of L^2(P_X) (Ernst, Mugler, Starkloff and Ullmann, 2012). Hence f(X) = \sum_{\alpha} c_\alpha\, \psi_\alpha(X), \qquad c_\alpha = \mathbb{E}[f(X)\, \psi_\alpha(X)]. For smooth f the truncation error decays fast; for bounded inputs and f analytic on a neighbourhood of their support, it decays exponentially with the degree p.

Proposition 5.7 — Moments and Sobol indices from the coefficients (Sudret, 2008) With D = \sum_{\alpha \neq 0} c_\alpha^2, the mean of f(X) is c_0, its variance is D, and S_u = \frac{1}{D} \sum_{\alpha :\, \mathrm{supp}(\alpha) = u} c_\alpha^2, \qquad S_{T_i} = \frac{1}{D} \sum_{\alpha :\, \alpha_i > 0} c_\alpha^2 .

Proof. Group the expansion by the support of \alpha: g_u = \sum_{\mathrm{supp}(\alpha) = u} c_\alpha \psi_\alpha depends only on x_u.

For j \in u, every term contains \psi^{(j)}_{\alpha_j} with \alpha_j \geq 1, which has zero mean, so integrating out x_j kills g_u.

By uniqueness in Theorem 5.1, g_u = f_u. Parseval’s identity gives D_u = \sum_{\mathrm{supp}(\alpha) = u} c_\alpha^2. ∎

The coefficients are computed in three ways.

  • Projection: c_\alpha = \mathbb{E}[f \psi_\alpha] by tensor or sparse Gauss quadrature, efficient in low dimension.
  • Regression: least squares on n runs, with n two or three times the number of terms.
  • Sparse regression: least-angle regression selects the few significant terms, which pushes polynomial chaos to tens of inputs (Blatman and Sudret, 2011).

The intrusive counterpart: stochastic Galerkin Inserting the expansion u(x, \xi) = \sum_\alpha u_\alpha(x) \psi_\alpha(\xi) into the PDE and projecting the residual on each \psi_\alpha gives one large coupled deterministic system for the coefficient fields u_\alpha. This is the stochastic finite element method of Ghanem and Spanos (1991), developed by Le Maître and Knio (2010). It is accurate but needs a new solver; non-intrusive chaos reuses the existing one.

Example 5.8 — The Ishigami function A standard benchmark of sensitivity analysis (Ishigami and Homma, 1990), with X_j uniform on [-\pi, \pi], a = 7 and b = 0.1: f(x) = \sin x_1 + a \sin^2 x_2 + b\, x_3^4 \sin x_1 .

Its variance is D = a^2/8 + b\pi^4/5 + b^2\pi^8/18 + 1/2 = 13.8446. The exact indices are S_1 = 0.3139, \qquad S_2 = 0.4424, \qquad S_3 = 0 .

Yet X_3 matters through its interaction with X_1, since S_{T_3} = 0.2437.

import itertools
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.legendre import legval

a, b = 7.0, 0.1                                            # Ishigami function, X ~ U(-pi, pi)^3
ishigami = lambda x: np.sin(x[:, 0]) + a * np.sin(x[:, 1]) ** 2 + b * x[:, 2] ** 4 * np.sin(x[:, 0])
D = a**2 / 8 + b * np.pi**4 / 5 + b**2 * np.pi**8 / 18 + 0.5     # exact variance
S_exact = [(0.5 * (1 + b * np.pi**4 / 5) ** 2) / D, a**2 / 8 / D, 0.0]

def psi(k, u):                       # orthonormal Legendre polynomial on U(-1, 1)
    c = np.zeros(k + 1); c[k] = 1
    return np.sqrt(2 * k + 1) * legval(u, c)

p, d = 10, 3
alphas = [al for al in itertools.product(range(p + 1), repeat=d) if sum(al) <= p]
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, (600, d)); y = ishigami(X)        # 600 runs
Phi = np.column_stack([np.prod([psi(k, X[:, j] / np.pi) for j, k in enumerate(al)], axis=0)
                       for al in alphas])
coef = np.linalg.lstsq(Phi, y, rcond=None)[0]                     # regression PCE
var = np.sum(coef[1:] ** 2)                                       # alpha = 0 is the mean
S1 = [sum(c**2 for al, c in zip(alphas, coef) if al[i] > 0 and sum(al) == al[i]) / var
      for i in range(d)]
ST = [sum(c**2 for al, c in zip(alphas, coef) if al[i] > 0) / var for i in range(d)]
print(f"{len(alphas)} basis terms; mean {coef[0]:.4f} (exact {a/2:.4f}); variance {var:.4f} (exact {D:.4f})")
print("first-order S_i:", np.round(S1, 4), " exact:", np.round(S_exact, 4))
print("total S_Ti     :", np.round(ST, 4))

Functional inputs and outputs

Physical inputs are often random fields — a permeability, a load history — and outputs are often fields too.

Theorem 5.10 — Karhunen–Loève expansion Let Z be a centred process on a compact domain T, with continuous covariance C.

Let (\lambda_k, e_k) be the eigenpairs of the integral operator of kernel C, with \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots. Then, in mean square and uniformly in t, Z(t) = \sum_{k \geq 1} \sqrt{\lambda_k}\, \xi_k\, e_k(t), \qquad \xi_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_k}} \int_T Z(s)\, e_k(s)\, ds, where the \xi_k are uncorrelated with unit variance, and independent standard Gaussians when Z is Gaussian.

Truncating after M terms leaves the mean-square error \sum_{k > M} \lambda_k, the smallest possible for M terms. The proof rests on Mercer’s theorem.

A random-field input thus becomes M independent Gaussian inputs, ready for polynomial chaos or a GP. For a field output, the discrete version of the theorem is the POD of ch. 2. One compresses the output snapshots by PCA and builds one surrogate per retained coefficient.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • With independent inputs, the output splits uniquely into orthogonal terms of increasing interaction order (Theorem 5.1), and its variance splits into Sobol indices.
  • Pick-freeze estimates Sobol indices by Monte Carlo, affordable on a surrogate.
  • Polynomial chaos expands f on polynomials orthonormal for the input law; its mean, variance and Sobol indices are sums of squared coefficients (Proposition 5.7).
  • Karhunen–Loève turns a random field into independent scalar inputs; PCA of output fields is its discrete twin.
  • Balance sheet: non-intrusive; sensitivity analysis comes for free; excellent for smooth outputs and independent inputs; suffers from the number of terms in high dimension, from discontinuities and from dependence.

6. Towards deep learning: neural surrogates, nonlinear reduction, neural ODEs

Gaussian processes and polynomial chaos struggle with thousands of runs, with inputs and outputs of high dimension, and with solution manifolds that no linear space captures. Neural networks address these three limits; this chapter makes the transition and keeps track of what is lost on the way, mainly built-in uncertainty.

Neural surrogates

A multilayer perceptron \hat f_\theta is trained on the distilled runs by minimising the empirical squared error, with stochastic gradients and automatic differentiation. Its training cost grows linearly with the number of runs, against O(n^3) for an exact GP, and its inference runs in batches on a GPU.

Theorem 6.1 — Approximation by one hidden layer With a continuous non-polynomial activation, one-hidden-layer networks are dense in the continuous functions on any compact set (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik, 1991; Leshno, Lin, Pinkus and Schocken, 1993).

For a rate, let f have a Fourier transform with finite first moment C_f = \int |\omega|\, |\hat f(\omega)|\, d\omega, and let \mu be a probability measure on the ball of radius r. Barron (1993) shows that n sigmoidal neurons achieve \int (f - f_n)^2\, d\mu \leq \frac{(2 r C_f)^2}{n} .

The rate n^{-1/2} does not depend on the dimension, although C_f may grow with it. In practice, the comparison with GPs is empirical: neural surrogates win with many runs and high-dimensional outputs, GPs with few runs and when calibrated uncertainty is needed.

Autoencoders: nonlinear reduction

Definition 6.2 — Autoencoder An encoder E_\phi : \mathbb{R}^{N} \to \mathbb{R}^{r} and a decoder D_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{r} \to \mathbb{R}^{N} are trained together on snapshots u_j to minimise the reconstruction error \sum_{j} \|u_j - D_\theta(E_\phi(u_j))\|^2 .

The decoder’s image is an r-dimensional nonlinear manifold, no longer a linear subspace (Hinton and Salakhutdinov, 2006).

Proposition 6.3 — A linear autoencoder learns POD If the encoder and the decoder are linear maps W_1 and W_2, the reconstruction error is at least the discarded energy \sum_{i > r} \sigma_i^2 of the snapshot matrix, and the POD pair W_1 = U_r^\top, W_2 = U_r attains it (Baldi and Hornik, 1989).

Indeed W_2 W_1 S has rank at most r, so Theorem 0.2 gives the lower bound, and Theorem 2.3 shows that POD attains it.

Nonlinearity is therefore the only thing an autoencoder adds to POD — and it is exactly what transport problems need.

Example 6.4 — A travelling bump The snapshots are Gaussian bumps of width 0.05 centred at a random position c \in [0.2, 0.8], on a 128-point grid. The family has one parameter, but its POD singular values decay slowly, as for every transported profile (ch. 2).

import numpy as np
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)

# snapshots of a transported bump: u(x; c) = exp(-((x - c) / 0.05)^2)
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 128)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
bumps = lambda c: np.exp(-((x[None, :] - c[:, None]) / 0.05) ** 2)
S, S_test = bumps(rng.uniform(0.2, 0.8, 200)), bumps(rng.uniform(0.2, 0.8, 50))

# linear reduction: POD with r modes, error on unseen snapshots
_, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(S, full_matrices=False)
for r in [1, 2, 5, 10]:
    P = Vt[:r].T @ Vt[:r]
    print(f"POD r={r:2d}: test relative error "
          f"{np.linalg.norm(S_test - S_test @ P) / np.linalg.norm(S_test):.3f}")

# nonlinear reduction: autoencoder with a latent dimension of 1
T = torch.nn.Tanh
enc = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(128, 64), T(), torch.nn.Linear(64, 1))
dec = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(1, 64), T(), torch.nn.Linear(64, 64), T(),
                          torch.nn.Linear(64, 128))
St, Ste = (torch.tensor(a, dtype=torch.float32) for a in (S, S_test))
opt = torch.optim.Adam([*enc.parameters(), *dec.parameters()], lr=3e-3)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, 1500)
for step in range(1500):
    loss = ((dec(enc(St)) - St) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    err = torch.linalg.norm(dec(enc(Ste)) - Ste) / torch.linalg.norm(Ste)
print(f"autoencoder, latent dimension 1: test relative error {err.item():.3f}")

The latent variable plays the role of the bump position, which no linear basis can represent compactly.

Deep reduced-order models

Several architectures turn this observation into reduced models for parametrised and time-dependent PDEs.

  • DL-ROM (Fresca and Manzoni, 2021): a network maps (t, \mu) to the latent coordinates, and the decoder of an autoencoder maps them to the full field; everything is trained on snapshots.
  • POD-DL-ROM (Fresca, Manzoni, Dedè and Quarteroni, 2022): the snapshots are first projected on a large POD basis, and the autoencoder works on those coordinates, which cuts the training cost.
  • Manifold Galerkin (Lee and Carlberg, 2020): the equations are projected on the tangent space of the decoder manifold. The model stays intrusive and keeps the physics, like the Galerkin ROM of ch. 2.

Neural ordinary differential equations

Definition 6.6 — Neural ODE (Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt and Duvenaud, 2018) The state follows a vector field given by a network, and the output is the state at time T: \dot z(t) = F_\theta(z(t), t), \qquad z(0) = z_0 .

A loss \mathcal{J}(z(T)) is minimised over \theta.

Theorem 6.7 — Adjoint method Let the adjoint a(t) solve, backwards in time from a(T) = \nabla \mathcal{J}(z(T)), \dot a(t) = -\Big(\frac{\partial F_\theta}{\partial z}\Big)^{\!\top} a(t).

Then the gradient of the loss is \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J} = \int_0^T \Big(\frac{\partial F_\theta}{\partial \theta}\Big)^{\!\top} a(t)\, dt .

Proof. Sketch. Perturb \theta by \delta\theta. The state perturbation solves the linearised equation \dot{\delta z} = \partial_z F\, \delta z + \partial_\theta F\, \delta\theta, \qquad \delta z(0) = 0 .

By the adjoint equation, the derivative of a^\top \delta z is a^\top \partial_\theta F\, \delta\theta.

Integrating from 0 to T gives \delta \mathcal{J} = a(T)^\top \delta z(T) = \int_0^T a^\top \partial_\theta F\, dt\; \delta\theta . ∎

The memory cost does not grow with the number of time steps, since no intermediate state needs storing. Neural ODEs learn latent dynamics of reduced models, or a correction term added to known physics, \dot z = F_{\mathrm{phys}}(z) + F_\theta(z) — the universal differential equations of Rackauckas and coauthors (2020).

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Neural surrogates scale linearly with the number of runs and approximate at a dimension-free rate for Barron-regular targets, but give no built-in uncertainty.
  • A linear autoencoder can do no better than POD (Proposition 6.3); a nonlinear one beats it on transport, with one latent variable instead of ten modes.
  • DL-ROM, POD-DL-ROM and manifold Galerkin turn autoencoders into reduced models; VAEs and deep GPs, which add a probabilistic layer, are in the self-study ch. 11.
  • Neural ODEs learn dynamics; the adjoint method computes their gradients with constant memory.
  • Balance sheet: non-intrusive (except manifold Galerkin); handles many runs, large outputs and nonlinear manifolds; costly training, tuning by trial and error, weak extrapolation and no guarantee.

7. Physics-informed neural networks

When the equations are known but data are scarce, the equations themselves can train the network. A physics-informed neural network (PINN) is a network u_\theta(x) whose loss penalises the residual of the PDE at sampled points (Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis, 2019; Karniadakis and coauthors, 2021).

The physics-informed loss

Definition 7.1 — PINN loss For a PDE \mathcal{N}[u] = f in \Omega with boundary condition \mathcal{B}[u] = g on \partial\Omega, and possibly measurements y_k at points \xi_k, the loss is \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{\lambda_r}{N_r} \sum_{i} \big|\mathcal{N}[u_\theta](x_i) - f(x_i)\big|^2 + \frac{\lambda_b}{N_b} \sum_{j} \big|\mathcal{B}[u_\theta](z_j) - g(z_j)\big|^2 + \frac{\lambda_d}{N_d} \sum_{k} |u_\theta(\xi_k) - y_k|^2 .

The derivatives in \mathcal{N} are computed exactly by automatic differentiation, at collocation points x_i that need no mesh.

The same loss solves inverse problems: an unknown coefficient of the PDE becomes a trainable parameter next to \theta, and the data term identifies it (Exercise 10). Boundary conditions can also be imposed exactly, by writing u_\theta = G + B\, N_\theta with G satisfying the condition and B vanishing on the boundary (Lagaris, Likas and Fotiadis, 1998).

Does a small residual mean a small error?

A small loss only says that the residual is small at the sampled points. Two questions remain: does a small residual imply a small error, and does the empirical loss reflect the true residual?

Proposition 7.2 — Stability for the 1D Poisson problem Let u solve -u'' = f on (0,1), and let v be smooth with residual r = f + v''. Then \|u - v\|_\infty \leq \max\big(|u(0) - v(0)|,\, |u(1) - v(1)|\big) + \tfrac14 \|r\|_{L^1} .

Proof. The error e = u - v satisfies -e'' = -u'' + v'' = f - (f - r) = r.

Write e = \ell + w, where \ell is the affine function with the boundary values of e, and w vanishes at both ends with -w'' = r.

The Green’s function of -w'' with zero boundary values is G(x, y) = \min(x,y)\,(1 - \max(x,y)), so w(x) = \int_0^1 G(x,y)\, r(y)\, dy.

Since 0 \leq G \leq 1/4, we get |w(x)| \leq \frac14 \|r\|_{L^1}. An affine function is bounded by its values at the ends. ∎

Since \|r\|_{L^1} \leq \|r\|_{L^2} on (0,1), the error is controlled by the root of the residual loss, provided the empirical average over collocation points estimates the true integral. Similar stability estimates hold for well-posed linear PDEs, with constants depending on the operator.

The second question is statistical. Doumèche, Biau and Boyer (2025) show that minimising the empirical PINN risk alone can overfit the collocation points, and that adding a ridge-type penalty restores consistency. Doumèche, Bach, Biau and Boyer (2024) show that for linear PDE constraints, physics-informed learning is a kernel method, with a kernel built from the differential operator. This yields convergence rates and shows when the physics speeds up learning — a bridge with the constrained GPs of ch. 4.

A worked PINN

import math
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)

# Poisson problem -u'' = pi^2 sin(pi x) on (0,1), u(0) = u(1) = 0; exact u = sin(pi x)
net = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(1, 32), torch.nn.Tanh(),
                          torch.nn.Linear(32, 32), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(32, 1))
u = lambda x: x * (1 - x) * net(x)          # hard constraint: boundary conditions hold exactly
f = lambda x: math.pi**2 * torch.sin(math.pi * x)

opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=1e-2)
for step in range(1500):
    x = torch.rand(64, 1, requires_grad=True)                 # fresh collocation points
    du = torch.autograd.grad(u(x).sum(), x, create_graph=True)[0]
    d2u = torch.autograd.grad(du.sum(), x, create_graph=True)[0]
    loss = ((-d2u - f(x)) ** 2).mean()                        # empirical PDE residual
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 500 == 0 or step == 1499:
        print(f"step {step:4d}: residual loss {loss.item():.2e}")

xt = torch.linspace(0, 1, 201)[:, None]
with torch.no_grad():
    err = (u(xt) - torch.sin(math.pi * xt)).abs().max()
print(f"max error |u_theta - u| on a fine grid: {err.item():.1e}")

Example 7.3 — Reading the output The residual loss falls from 42.1 to 1.5 \cdot 10^{-5} in 1500 Adam steps, with 64 new collocation points at each step. The maximal error on a fine grid is 1.9 \cdot 10^{-4}.

Proposition 7.2 predicts a bound of about \frac14 \sqrt{1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}} \approx 10^{-3}, since the boundary error is zero by construction. The observed error is five times smaller, as expected from a worst-case bound.

Failure modes and remedies

PINNs fail in reproducible ways, which recent work has mapped.

  • Spectral bias: networks learn low frequencies first (Rahaman and coauthors, 2019), so oscillatory or multiscale solutions converge slowly. Fourier feature embeddings help.
  • Unbalanced gradients: the residual and boundary terms can have gradients of very different sizes, and training stalls (Wang, Teng and Perdikaris, 2021). Adaptive weights \lambda or hard constraints help.
  • Hard regimes: for convection with large coefficients, training fails although the network could represent the solution (Krishnapriyan, Gholami, Zhe, Kirby and Mahoney, 2021). Curriculum training on the coefficient helps.
  • Sampling: adding collocation points where the residual is large concentrates effort where it matters (Lu, Meng, Mao and Karniadakis, 2021).

For a single forward solve of a low-dimensional PDE, a classical solver is faster and more accurate. PINNs are worth it for inverse problems with sparse data, for mesh-free settings, or when the solution is sought over parameters as well.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • A PINN minimises the PDE residual at collocation points, plus boundary and data terms; derivatives come from automatic differentiation.
  • Stability turns a small residual into a small error (Proposition 7.2), provided the empirical loss estimates the true residual.
  • Without regularisation, the empirical PINN risk can overfit; for linear PDEs, physics-informed learning is a kernel method.
  • Spectral bias, unbalanced gradients and hard regimes are the main failure modes, each with known remedies.
  • Balance sheet: physics in the loss; non-intrusive with respect to the solver but needs the equations; mesh-free and natural for inverse problems; slow to train, no certified error, rarely competitive for one forward solve.

8. Operator learning and hybrid deep models

A PINN solves one PDE instance; a surrogate of ch. 3–6 maps a few parameters to an output. Many studies need more: the map from an input function — a coefficient field, an initial condition, a geometry — to the solution function. Learning that operator once gives a solver for every new input at the cost of a forward pass.

Operators between function spaces

Let \mathcal{G} : a \mapsto u map an input function to the solution, and suppose we have pairs (a_j, \mathcal{G}(a_j)) computed by a solver. A good operator surrogate should accept inputs given on any grid: this is discretisation invariance.

Theorem 8.1 — Universal approximation of operators (Chen and Chen, 1995) Let \mathcal{G} be a continuous operator on a compact set of continuous functions. For every \varepsilon > 0 there are sensor points x_1, \dots, x_m and continuous functions b_k and t_k, realisable by one-hidden-layer networks, such that, uniformly over the compact set of inputs and over y in a compact domain, \Big| \mathcal{G}(a)(y) - \sum_{k=1}^{p} b_k\big(a(x_1), \dots, a(x_m)\big)\, t_k(y) \Big| < \varepsilon .

DeepONet (Lu, Jin, Pang, Zhang and Karniadakis, 2021) implements this form with two deep networks: a branch net for the coefficients b_k and a trunk net for the basis functions t_k. The trunk can be queried at any point y, but the branch needs the input at fixed sensors.

Definition 8.2 — Neural operator and Fourier layer A neural operator lifts the input to c channels, applies L layers, then projects back (Kovachki and coauthors, 2023). Each layer combines a pointwise linear map W and a learned integral operator: v_{\ell+1}(x) = \sigma\Big(W v_\ell(x) + \int \kappa_\theta(x, y)\, v_\ell(y)\, dy\Big).

In the Fourier neural operator (FNO, Li and coauthors, 2021), the kernel is translation invariant. The integral is then a convolution, applied in Fourier space to the lowest k_{\max} modes with learned complex weights: \Big(\int \kappa_\theta(x - y)\, v(y)\, dy\Big) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(R_\theta \cdot \mathcal{F} v\big)(x).

The weights R_\theta act on frequencies, not on grid points, so the same network applies on any grid fine enough to resolve k_{\max} modes. Each layer costs O(N \log N) through the FFT.

Proposition 8.3 — The heat equation is one Fourier layer On the periodic interval [0,1), the solution of \partial_t u = \nu\, \partial_{xx} u at time T is a Fourier multiplier of the initial condition: \hat u_k(T) = e^{-\nu (2\pi k)^2 T}\, \hat u_k(0).

A single linear Fourier layer with these weights represents the solution operator exactly on band-limited inputs.

Proof. Each Fourier mode e^{2\pi i k x} is an eigenfunction of \partial_{xx} with eigenvalue -(2\pi k)^2. The equation decouples into scalar ODEs \dot{\hat u}_k = -\nu (2\pi k)^2 \hat u_k, whose solutions are the stated exponentials. ∎

Nonlinear PDEs couple the modes; the pointwise maps and activations between Fourier layers provide that coupling.

import math
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)

def data(n, grid, nuT=0.002, K=8):
    """Random periodic initial conditions u0 and heat-equation solutions u(T)."""
    k = torch.arange(1, K + 1)
    a, b = torch.randn(n, K) / k, torch.randn(n, K) / k      # decaying random Fourier coefficients
    x = torch.arange(grid) / grid
    damp = torch.exp(-nuT * (2 * math.pi * k) ** 2)          # exact solution operator, mode by mode
    wave = lambda c, s: (c[:, :, None] * torch.cos(2 * math.pi * k[None, :, None] * x)
                         + s[:, :, None] * torch.sin(2 * math.pi * k[None, :, None] * x)).sum(1)
    return wave(a, b)[:, None], wave(a * damp, b * damp)[:, None]

class SpectralConv(torch.nn.Module):                         # v -> F^{-1}(R . F v), lowest modes only
    def __init__(self, ch, modes):
        super().__init__()
        self.modes = modes
        self.R = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.randn(ch, ch, modes, dtype=torch.cfloat) / ch)
    def forward(self, v):
        vh = torch.fft.rfft(v)
        out = torch.zeros_like(vh)
        out[..., :self.modes] = torch.einsum("bim,iom->bom", vh[..., :self.modes], self.R)
        return torch.fft.irfft(out, n=v.shape[-1])

class FNO(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, ch=16, modes=12, layers=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.lift, self.proj = torch.nn.Conv1d(1, ch, 1), torch.nn.Conv1d(ch, 1, 1)
        self.spec = torch.nn.ModuleList(SpectralConv(ch, modes) for _ in range(layers))
        self.loc = torch.nn.ModuleList(torch.nn.Conv1d(ch, ch, 1) for _ in range(layers))
    def forward(self, u):
        v = self.lift(u)
        for i, (s, w) in enumerate(zip(self.spec, self.loc)):
            v = s(v) + w(v)
            v = torch.nn.functional.gelu(v) if i < len(self.spec) - 1 else v
        return self.proj(v)

u0, uT = data(256, 64)
model = FNO()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=5e-3)
for step in range(300):
    loss = ((model(u0) - uT) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
rel = lambda p, t: (torch.linalg.norm(p - t) / torch.linalg.norm(t)).item()
with torch.no_grad():
    for grid in (64, 256):                                    # 256: never seen in training
        a, b = data(200, grid)
        print(f"test relative error on a {grid}-point grid: {rel(model(a), b):.3f}")

Example 8.4 — Learning the heat operator The FNO has two Fourier layers, 16 channels and 12 modes. It is trained on 256 pairs sampled on a 64-point grid, with \nu T = 0.002.

  • The test relative error is 0.005 on the training grid.
  • On a 256-point grid, never seen in training, the error is 0.006: the operator transfers across resolutions, as Definition 8.2 predicts.

Graphs, meshes and transformers

Industrial simulators work on unstructured meshes with varying geometry, where FFTs do not apply.

Definition 8.5 — Message passing on a mesh Treat mesh nodes as graph nodes i with features v_i (state, material, node type), and mesh edges as graph edges with features e_{ij} (relative position, distance). One processing step updates e'_{ij} = \phi_e(e_{ij}, v_i, v_j), \qquad v'_i = \phi_v\Big(v_i, \sum_{j \sim i} e'_{ij}\Big), with small networks \phi_e and \phi_v.

An encode–process–decode model stacks such steps and predicts the state at the next time step, applied again to roll out a trajectory (Sanchez-Gonzalez and coauthors, 2020; Pfaff, Fortunato, Sanchez-Gonzalez and Battaglia, 2021).

Using relative positions makes the model invariant to translations, and the same weights apply to any mesh. The price is error accumulation along long rollouts, reduced by training with noise injected in the inputs.

Attention is another learned integral operator. With queries, keys and values computed from the field, the update at x_i is a weighted average of the values at all points x_j. It is a discretised kernel integral \int \kappa(x, y)\, v(y)\, dy whose kernel depends on the data.

Universal physics transformers (Alkin and coauthors, 2024) encode an arbitrary mesh or particle set into a fixed number of latent tokens, evolve them, and decode at any query point. This lets neural operators scale to large simulations whose meshes or particle sets vary in size.

Hybrid models

Hybrid architectures keep part of the classical solver and learn only the rest.

  • POD + regression: project on a POD basis and learn the map from \mu to the reduced coefficients with a GP or a network (Hesthaven and Ubbiali, 2018). Non-intrusive, and cheap online.
  • POD-DL-ROM and manifold Galerkin: ch. 6.
  • Closure and correction: keep the physical model and learn only the missing term, \dot u = F_{\mathrm{phys}}(u) + N_\theta(u), or learn the discrepancy between a coarse and a fine solver.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Operator learning maps input functions to solutions; DeepONet follows the Chen–Chen form, neural operators stack learned integral operators.
  • The FNO applies its kernel on Fourier modes; the heat operator is one linear Fourier layer (Proposition 8.3), and a trained FNO transfers across grids.
  • Message passing on meshes and attention handle unstructured geometries; universal physics transformers scale this to large simulations.
  • Hybrid models learn only what the physics misses: reduced coefficients, closures, corrections.
  • Balance sheet: physics enters through the architecture; non-intrusive; very fast inference over whole families of inputs; needs thousands of solver runs, extrapolates poorly, and gives no error certificate without the tools of ch. 10.

9. Calibration, inverse problems, data assimilation and validation

A surrogate is rarely an end in itself. It is used to infer parameters from measurements, to track a system with streaming data, or to optimise and control it. This chapter covers inference and tracking, and the validation that must precede any use; optimisation and control loops are complements in ch. 11.

Inverse problems and their Bayesian form

Recovering parameters \theta from data y = \mathcal{G}(\theta) + \varepsilon is usually ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard: a solution may not exist, may not be unique, or may not depend continuously on the data. Regularisation restores stability, and the Bayesian view explains which regularisation.

Proposition 9.1 — Tikhonov regularisation is a Gaussian posterior Let y = G\theta + \varepsilon with G linear, noise \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Gamma) and prior \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_0, C_0). The posterior is Gaussian, with covariance and mean C_{\mathrm{post}} = \big(G^\top \Gamma^{-1} G + C_0^{-1}\big)^{-1}, \qquad \theta_{\mathrm{post}} = C_{\mathrm{post}}\big(G^\top \Gamma^{-1} y + C_0^{-1} \theta_0\big).

The posterior mean minimises the Tikhonov functional \|y - G\theta\|^2_{\Gamma^{-1}} + \|\theta - \theta_0\|^2_{C_0^{-1}} .

Proof. By Bayes’ formula, minus twice the log-posterior equals the Tikhonov functional up to a constant. It is a quadratic form in \theta with Hessian 2\, C_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}.

Completing the square shows a Gaussian density, centred at the minimiser, which solves the normal equations C_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}\theta = G^\top \Gamma^{-1} y + C_0^{-1}\theta_0 . ∎

For a nonlinear simulator, the posterior is no longer Gaussian and must be sampled (Stuart, 2010; Kaipio and Somersalo, 2005).

Bayesian calibration with a surrogate

Definition 9.2 — Bayesian calibration and the Metropolis algorithm With measurements y_i = f(x_i, \theta) + \varepsilon_i, Gaussian noise of variance \sigma^2 and a prior \pi_0, the posterior density is \pi(\theta \mid y) \propto \pi_0(\theta)\, \exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_i \big(y_i - f(x_i, \theta)\big)^2\Big).

The random-walk Metropolis algorithm proposes \theta' = \theta + \tau Z with Z standard normal, and accepts it with probability \min\big(1, \pi(\theta' \mid y)/\pi(\theta \mid y)\big). The chain has \pi(\cdot \mid y) as invariant law.

Each step calls the simulator, and a chain needs thousands of steps: calibration is the textbook case for a surrogate. Replacing f by a kriging mean m_n ignores the surrogate error. Accounting for it is simple.

Proposition 9.3 — Surrogate uncertainty in the likelihood Let the GP posterior of ch. 3 give, jointly over the measurement points and independently of the measurement noise, \big(f(x_i, \theta)\big)_i \sim \mathcal{N}\big(m_n(\theta), S_n(\theta)\big).

Then, integrating the surrogate out, y \mid \theta \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\big(m_n(\theta),\; \sigma^2 I + S_n(\theta)\big).

The proof is the sum of two independent Gaussian vectors. Where the surrogate is uncertain, the likelihood flattens instead of being wrong.

Stuart and Teckentrup (2018) bound the distance between the true posterior and the surrogate posterior by the surrogate error, which justifies goal-oriented enrichment: add runs where the posterior puts its mass, not everywhere.

Definition 9.4 — Model discrepancy (Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001) A simulator is never exact. The calibration model y(x) = \rho\, f(x, \theta) + \delta(x) + \varepsilon, \qquad \delta \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k_\delta), adds a discrepancy function \delta, and infers \theta and \delta jointly.

Without discrepancy, \theta absorbs the model error and becomes over-confident and biased. With a vague discrepancy, \theta and \delta are confounded: many pairs explain the data equally well. Brynjarsdóttir and O’Hagan (2014) show that realistic prior information on \delta is needed to learn the physical parameter.

import warnings
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import qmc
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import ConstantKernel as C, Matern
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")

t = np.linspace(0, 5, 20)
def model(th):                        # damped oscillator x(t), theta = (omega, zeta), batched
    w, z = th[:, :1], th[:, 1:]
    return np.exp(-z * w * t) * np.cos(w * np.sqrt(1 - z**2) * t)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sigma = 0.05                                                        # measurement noise
y_obs = model(np.array([[2.0, 0.1]]))[0] + sigma * rng.standard_normal(t.size)
lo, hi = np.array([1.0, 0.0]), np.array([3.0, 0.5])                 # uniform prior box
log_lik = lambda pred: -np.sum((y_obs - pred) ** 2, axis=1) / (2 * sigma**2)

def mh(log_post, n_chains=200, n_steps=400, step=0.01):
    """Random-walk Metropolis with many parallel chains (batched log-posterior)."""
    th = lo + (hi - lo) * rng.random((n_chains, 2)); lp = log_post(th)
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        prop = th + step * (hi - lo) * rng.standard_normal(th.shape)
        inside = np.all((prop > lo) & (prop < hi), axis=1)
        lp_prop = np.where(inside, log_post(np.clip(prop, lo, hi)), -np.inf)
        acc = np.log(rng.random(n_chains)) < lp_prop - lp
        th[acc], lp[acc] = prop[acc], lp_prop[acc]
    return th                                                       # final state of each chain

exact = mh(lambda th: log_lik(model(th)))
design = qmc.scale(qmc.LatinHypercube(d=2, seed=0).random(30), lo, hi)
for rnd in range(3):                                                # goal-oriented enrichment
    gp = GaussianProcessRegressor(C(1.0) * Matern([0.5, 0.1], nu=2.5), alpha=1e-8,
                                  normalize_y=True).fit(design, model(design))
    post = mh(lambda th: log_lik(gp.predict(th)))
    print(f"round {rnd}: {len(design)} runs -> mean {post.mean(0).round(3)}, sd {post.std(0).round(3)}")
    design = np.vstack([design, post[rng.choice(200, 5, replace=False)]])
print(f"exact model     -> mean {exact.mean(0).round(3)}, sd {exact.std(0).round(3)}")

Example 9.5 — Calibrating a damped oscillator The simulator returns 20 values of the damped oscillation x(t) = e^{-\zeta\omega t}\cos\big(\omega\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\, t\big), with true parameters \omega = 2 and \zeta = 0.1, and noise of standard deviation 0.05.

Data assimilation

In forecasting, the state of a dynamical system is corrected each time a measurement arrives. With a forecast model \mathcal{M}, a linear observation operator H and Gaussian noises, x_{k+1} = \mathcal{M}(x_k) + \eta_k, \qquad y_k = H x_k + \varepsilon_k, \quad \varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R).

Theorem 9.6 — Kalman analysis step If the forecast is x \sim \mathcal{N}(x^f, P^f), then given y = Hx + \varepsilon the state is Gaussian with x^a = x^f + K\,(y - H x^f), \qquad P^a = (I - KH)\,P^f, \qquad K = P^f H^\top \big(H P^f H^\top + R\big)^{-1}.

Proof. The pair (x, y) is Gaussian with \mathrm{Cov}(x, y) = P^f H^\top and \mathrm{Var}(y) = H P^f H^\top + R.

Proposition 0.3 with Z_1 = x and Z_2 = y gives the formulas. ∎

For a linear model, alternating this analysis with the exact forecast of mean and covariance is the Kalman filter. For large nonlinear models, the ensemble Kalman filter (Evensen, 1994) propagates an ensemble of states, replaces P^f by their empirical covariance, and updates each member with perturbed observations. Variational methods (3D-Var, 4D-Var) instead minimise a Tikhonov functional as in Proposition 9.1 (Asch, Bocquet and Nodet, 2016). A cheap surrogate forecast model allows larger ensembles.

import numpy as np

def lorenz_step(X, dt=0.01, s=10.0, r=28.0, b=8 / 3):        # one RK4 step, X has shape (n, 3)
    f = lambda X: np.column_stack([s * (X[:, 1] - X[:, 0]),
                                   X[:, 0] * (r - X[:, 2]) - X[:, 1],
                                   X[:, 0] * X[:, 1] - b * X[:, 2]])
    k1 = f(X); k2 = f(X + dt / 2 * k1); k3 = f(X + dt / 2 * k2); k4 = f(X + dt * k3)
    return X + dt / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
H = np.array([[1.0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1.0]])                      # we observe x and z only
R = 2.0 * np.eye(2)                                           # observation-error covariance
truth = np.array([[1.0, 1.0, 1.0]])
free = truth + 3 * rng.standard_normal((30, 3))               # 30-member ensemble, no data
enkf = free.copy()
err_free, err_enkf = [], []
for k in range(1, 2001):
    truth, free, enkf = lorenz_step(truth), lorenz_step(free), lorenz_step(enkf)
    if k % 20 == 0:                                           # an observation every 0.2 time units
        y = H @ truth[0] + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(2), R)
        A = enkf - enkf.mean(0)
        P = A.T @ A / (len(enkf) - 1)                         # ensemble covariance
        K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(H @ P @ H.T + R)          # Kalman gain
        Y = y + rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(2), R, len(enkf))   # perturbed observations
        enkf = enkf + (Y - enkf @ H.T) @ K.T
        if k > 1000:
            err_free.append(np.linalg.norm(free.mean(0) - truth[0]))
            err_enkf.append(np.linalg.norm(enkf.mean(0) - truth[0]))
print(f"mean error of the ensemble mean, no assimilation: {np.mean(err_free):.2f}")
print(f"mean error of the ensemble mean, EnKF analysis  : {np.mean(err_enkf):.2f}")

Example 9.7 — Tracking a chaotic system The Lorenz-63 system is chaotic: two ensembles started close together soon disagree. Without data, the error of the ensemble mean averages 13.2 over the second half of the run, the size of the attractor.

With an EnKF of 30 members, assimilating noisy observations of x and z every 0.2 time units, the error drops to 0.73, below the observation noise of standard deviation 1.41.

Validating a metamodel before use

Validation asks whether the surrogate is accurate enough for the intended use, a question distinct from verification, which asks whether the code solves its equations correctly (Oberkampf and Roy, 2010).

  • Error estimates: hold-out, k-fold or leave-one-out Q^2 and maximal errors (Definition 3.7), computed where the use needs them — near the threshold for a failure probability, in the posterior mass for a calibration.
  • Uncertainty checks: standardised residuals and coverage of the predictive intervals; chapter 10 makes coverage exact.
  • Domain of validity: the surrogate is trusted only inside the convex hull of its runs and the range of its training inputs; extrapolation must be detected and flagged.
  • Certified bounds where available: the residual bound of Proposition 2.6 for reduced models.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Tikhonov regularisation is the Gaussian posterior mean (Proposition 9.1); nonlinear inverse problems need MCMC, made affordable by surrogates.
  • The surrogate variance belongs in the likelihood (Proposition 9.3); enrichment should target the posterior mass.
  • The Kennedy–O’Hagan discrepancy prevents over-confident parameters but needs informative priors to stay identifiable.
  • The Kalman analysis step is Gaussian conditioning (Theorem 9.6); the EnKF extends it to large nonlinear models.
  • Validation is goal-oriented: error and coverage where the use needs them, inside the domain of the runs.
  • Balance sheet: surrogates make calibration, assimilation and optimisation feasible; they add an error that must be propagated, and they never replace a check against the real system.

10. Uncertainty quantification in prediction

A prediction made with a surrogate carries three uncertainties: that of the inputs, that of the surrogate, and that of the model itself. This chapter separates them, reviews how neural surrogates estimate theirs, and gives two tools with guarantees that hold whatever the surrogate: conformal prediction and proper scoring of ensembles.

Separating the sources

Proposition 10.1 — Law of total variance For a square-integrable Y and any random element \Theta, \mathrm{Var}\, Y = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid \Theta)\big] + \mathrm{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \Theta]\big).

Expand the conditional variance, \mathrm{Var}(Y \mid \Theta) = \mathbb{E}[Y^2 \mid \Theta] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \Theta]^2, take expectations, and add \mathrm{Var}\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \Theta]: the squared conditional means cancel.

Take Y = F(X), where F is a sample path of the GP surrogate and X the random input. Conditioning on F separates the input variability, \mathbb{E}_F \mathrm{Var}_X, from the surrogate uncertainty, \mathrm{Var}_F\, \mathbb{E}_X. The second term is estimated by computing the quantity of interest on several sample paths; if it dominates, more runs are worth more than more input data.

Conditioning instead on the parameters \theta of a Bayesian model splits aleatoric noise, \mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid \theta), from epistemic uncertainty, \mathrm{Var}\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \theta]. This is the decomposition used by the neural methods below.

Uncertainty for neural surrogates

Neural networks give no posterior by default. The survey of He, Jiang, Xiao, Xu and Li (2023) classifies the remedies; three are standard.

  • Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017): train M networks from different initialisations, each predicting a mean \mu_m and a variance \sigma_m^2. The ensemble predicts the mixture, with mean \bar\mu and variance \frac1M \sum_m (\sigma_m^2 + \mu_m^2) - \bar\mu^2.
  • Monte Carlo dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016): keep dropout active at prediction time and average many stochastic passes, an approximation of a Bayesian network.
  • Laplace and variational methods: a Gaussian approximation of the posterior of the weights, around the trained network or by optimisation.

These variances are often poorly calibrated, especially out of distribution. The next tool corrects calibration without assumptions on the surrogate.

Conformal prediction

Theorem 10.2 — Split conformal coverage Fit any predictor \hat f on a training set. On n calibration points, compute the scores S_i = |y_i - \hat f(x_i)| / \hat s(x_i), for a fixed positive scale function \hat s.

Let \hat q be the k-th smallest score, with k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil \leq n. If the calibration and test points are exchangeable, the interval \hat f(x) \pm \hat q\, \hat s(x) covers a new output with probability \mathbb{P}\big(|y_{n+1} - \hat f(x_{n+1})| \leq \hat q\, \hat s(x_{n+1})\big) \geq 1 - \alpha .

If the scores have no ties, the coverage is also at most 1 - \alpha + 1/(n+1).

Proof. The predictor and the scale are fixed given the training set, so the scores S_1, \dots, S_{n+1} are exchangeable.

The test point is covered exactly when S_{n+1} \leq \hat q, that is when S_{n+1} is among the k smallest of the n+1 scores.

By exchangeability, and without ties, the rank of S_{n+1} is uniform on \{1, \dots, n+1\}, so this has probability k/(n+1), which lies in [1-\alpha,\, 1-\alpha + 1/(n+1)).

With ties, the probability can only increase. ∎

The guarantee is marginal: it averages over test inputs, and exact conditional coverage at every x is impossible without assumptions (Foygel Barber, Candès, Ramdas and Tibshirani, 2021). Normalising by the GP standard deviation, as proposed by Jaber and coauthors (2024), keeps the guarantee and adapts the width to the local difficulty. Gopakumar and coauthors (2024) apply the same principle to neural PDE surrogates with field outputs. Fontana, Zeni and Vantini (2023) review the theory.

import warnings
import numpy as np
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import ConstantKernel as C, RBF, WhiteKernel
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def simulator(x):                         # stochastic code with heteroscedastic noise
    return np.sin(3 * x[:, 0]) * x[:, 1] + 0.1 * (1 + x[:, 1] ** 2) * rng.standard_normal(len(x))
draw = lambda n: rng.uniform(-2, 2, (n, 2))

X_tr = draw(60); y_tr = simulator(X_tr)                          # training runs
gp = GaussianProcessRegressor(C(1.0) * RBF([1.0, 1.0]) + WhiteKernel(0.01),
                              normalize_y=True).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
sd = lambda X: gp.predict(X, return_std=True)[1]
alpha, n = 0.1, 100
k = int(np.ceil((n + 1) * (1 - alpha)))                          # rank of the conformal quantile
for name, scale in [("absolute residual", lambda X: np.ones(len(X))), ("GP-normalised", sd)]:
    cover, width = [], []
    for rep in range(100):                                       # new calibration and test sets
        X_cal, X_te = draw(n), draw(500)
        y_cal, y_te = simulator(X_cal), simulator(X_te)
        q = np.sort(np.abs(y_cal - gp.predict(X_cal)) / scale(X_cal))[k - 1]
        half = q * scale(X_te)
        cover.append(np.mean(np.abs(y_te - gp.predict(X_te)) <= half))
        width.append(2 * half.mean())
    se = np.std(cover) / np.sqrt(len(cover))                     # Monte Carlo standard error
    print(f"{name:18s}: coverage {np.mean(cover):.3f} +/- {se:.3f}, mean width {np.mean(width):.3f}")
print(f"guaranteed marginal coverage: k/(n+1) = {k / (n + 1):.3f}")

Example 10.3 — Conformal intervals for a stochastic code A GP is trained on 60 runs of a code with noise growing with |x_2|. Calibration uses 100 fresh runs, with \alpha = 0.1, so k = 91; the experiment is repeated over 100 calibration sets.

  • Absolute residuals: coverage 0.907 \pm 0.003, mean width 1.09.
  • GP-normalised scores: coverage 0.898 \pm 0.003, mean width 1.04.
  • Theorem 10.2 guarantees 91/101 = 0.901.

Both coverages match the guarantee within Monte Carlo error. The normalised intervals are narrower on average because they adapt to the local noise level.

Ensemble forecasts and proper scores

An ensemble forecast runs the model from perturbed initial states, parameters or model versions, and reads the spread of the members as forecast uncertainty. Two tools check such forecasts against observations.

Definition 10.4 — Continuous ranked probability score For a forecast distribution F and an observation y, \mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \big(F(z) - \mathbb{1}[y \leq z]\big)^2\, dz = \mathbb{E}|Z - y| - \tfrac12\, \mathbb{E}|Z - Z'| , where Z, Z' are independent with law F. For an ensemble, F is its empirical distribution.

The CRPS is a strictly proper score: its expectation under the true law is minimal only when the forecast equals that law (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). It rewards sharpness and calibration together, and reduces to the absolute error for a deterministic forecast.

The rank histogram checks calibration alone. If the observation and the M members are exchangeable, the rank of the observation among them is uniform on \{1, \dots, M+1\}, by the argument of Theorem 10.2. A U-shaped histogram reveals an under-dispersed ensemble, a dome an over-dispersed one.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • The law of total variance separates input variability from surrogate uncertainty, and aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty.
  • Deep ensembles, MC dropout and Laplace approximations estimate neural uncertainty, without calibration guarantees.
  • Split conformal prediction gives finite-sample marginal coverage for any surrogate under exchangeability (Theorem 10.2); GP-normalised scores adapt the widths.
  • Ensemble forecasts are scored by the CRPS, a strictly proper score, and checked by rank histograms.
  • Balance sheet: conformal prediction is cheap, model-agnostic and exact on average, but only marginal and only under exchangeability, which a distribution shift breaks.

Low-rank formats

Polynomial chaos with total degree p in dimension d has \binom{d+p}{p} terms, which explodes with d. Low-rank formats exploit a different structure: separability.

Definition 11.1 — Canonical low-rank approximation A rank-R approximation writes f as a sum of R products of univariate functions, each expanded on p+1 polynomials: f(x) \approx \sum_{l=1}^{R} b_l \prod_{j=1}^{d} v^{(l)}_j(x_j), \qquad v^{(l)}_j(x_j) = \sum_{k=0}^{p} z^{(l)}_{k,j}\, \psi_k(x_j).

The number of unknowns grows like R\,d\,(p+1), linearly in the dimension, instead of \binom{d+p}{p}. Alternating least squares fits one direction at a time, each step being a small linear regression (Chevreuil, Lebrun, Nouy and Rai, 2015; Konakli and Sudret, 2016). Tensor-train formats extend the idea.

Dependent inputs, Shapley effects and explanations

With dependent inputs, the decomposition of Theorem 5.1 loses its orthogonality. Hooker (2007) and Chastaing, Gamboa and Prieur (2012) replace mutual orthogonality by hierarchical orthogonality, in which each f_u is orthogonal to every f_v with v \subsetneq u. They give conditions on the joint density under which this decomposition exists and is unique; the variance then splits into structural and correlative contributions.

Definition 11.2 — Shapley effects (Owen, 2014) Let c(u) be the share of variance explained by the group u, c(u) = \frac{\mathrm{Var}\,\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_u]}{\mathrm{Var}\,Y} .

The Shapley effect of input i is its Shapley value in the game c: \mathrm{Sh}_i = \frac1d \sum_{u \subseteq \{1,\dots,d\} \setminus \{i\}} \binom{d-1}{|u|}^{-1} \big(c(u \cup \{i\}) - c(u)\big).

The effects are non-negative, sum to 1, and stay meaningful for dependent inputs.

Proposition 11.3 — Shapley effects with independent inputs If the inputs are independent, each interaction is shared equally among its members: \mathrm{Sh}_i = \sum_{u \ni i} \frac{S_u}{|u|}, \qquad \text{hence} \qquad S_i \leq \mathrm{Sh}_i \leq S_{T_i}.

Proof. By Theorem 5.1, c(u) = \sum_{\emptyset \neq v \subseteq u} S_v, so c is a sum of games u \mapsto S_v\, \mathbb{1}[v \subseteq u].

The Shapley value is linear in the game. In the game \mathbb{1}[v \subseteq u], players outside v never change the value (null players), and players of v are symmetric.

Efficiency then gives each player of v the share 1/|v|. Summing over v gives the formula, and the bounds follow from 1/|u| \leq 1. ∎

The same Shapley construction, applied for a single input point x with the game u \mapsto \mathbb{E}[f(X) \mid X_u = x_u], gives the SHAP explanations of machine learning (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). Ferrere, Bousquet, Gamboa and Loubes (2026) derive generalised functional ANOVA decompositions in closed form and use them as a unified view of such additive explanations. Sensitivity analysis is thus also the explainability layer of a surrogate (Da Veiga, Gamboa, Iooss and Prieur, 2021).

Example 11.4 — Shapley effects of a simple interaction Let Y = X_1 + X_2 + X_1 X_2 with independent X_j uniform on [-1, 1]. The three orthogonal terms have variances 1/3, 1/3 and 1/9, so S_1 = S_2 = \tfrac37, \qquad S_{12} = \tfrac17, \qquad S_{T_1} = S_{T_2} = \tfrac47 .

By Proposition 11.3 the interaction is shared equally, which gives \mathrm{Sh}_1 = \mathrm{Sh}_2 = \tfrac37 + \tfrac1{14} = \tfrac12, between S_1 and S_{T_1} as predicted.

Probabilistic latent models

This section complements the autoencoders of ch. 6 with a probabilistic latent space.

Proposition 11.5 — Evidence lower bound (Kingma and Welling, 2014) Let p_\theta(u, z) = p(z)\, p_\theta(u \mid z) be a latent-variable model and q_\phi(z \mid u) any encoder distribution. Then \log p_\theta(u) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid u)}\big[\log p_\theta(u \mid z)\big] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z \mid u)\,\|\,p(z)\big).

Proof. Write \log p_\theta(u) = \log \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\big[p_\theta(u, z)/q_\phi(z \mid u)\big] and apply Jensen’s inequality to the concave logarithm. Splitting \log p_\theta(u,z) = \log p_\theta(u \mid z) + \log p(z) gives the two terms. ∎

A variational autoencoder maximises this bound. Its latent space carries a distribution, which gives a generative surrogate: sampling z produces plausible fields. Deep Gaussian processes compose GPs, F = F_L \circ \dots \circ F_1, to model non-stationary responses while keeping a Bayesian treatment (Damianou and Lawrence, 2013).

Geometry, shapes and non-Euclidean inputs

This section extends ch. 8 to inputs that are not vectors of real numbers.

When the geometry varies, three representations are common. A parametrised shape can be mapped to a reference domain, which keeps the methods of ch. 2. A mesh or point cloud can be the input of a graph network or a transformer. A signed distance function turns the shape into a field on a fixed grid, ready for an FNO.

Kernels also extend beyond Euclidean inputs. On a graph with Laplacian \Delta, Borovitskiy and coauthors (2021) define Matérn GPs with covariance proportional to (2\nu/\kappa^2 + \Delta)^{-\nu}, which tends to the heat kernel as \nu \to \infty; similar constructions hold on Riemannian manifolds. Topological summaries such as persistent homology (Carlsson, 2009) provide shape descriptors that are stable to small deformations, usable as surrogate inputs.

Metamodels in optimisation, control and networked systems

The industrial uses named in the syllabus share one pattern: an outer loop calls the model many times under a time or budget constraint.

  • Optimisation under uncertainty: minimise a robust objective such as \mathbb{E}[f(x, X)] + \kappa\, \mathrm{sd}[f(x, X)], or a quantile, under chance constraints \mathbb{P}(g(x, X) > 0) \leq \epsilon. Each candidate x needs an inner Monte Carlo loop, affordable only on a surrogate; EGO (ch. 4) drives the outer loop.
  • Control: model predictive control solves, at each sampling instant, an optimisation over a horizon subject to the dynamics. A reduced model or a neural ODE makes this feasible in real time, and its error must enter the constraint margins.
  • Networks: power, gas, water and heat networks carry states on nodes and flows on edges. Graph neural surrogates (ch. 8) approximate the network solver and transfer across topologies, which matters for screening many outage scenarios.
  • Digital twins and maintenance: a twin couples a surrogate, data assimilation and calibration to follow one physical asset over time.

12. Research directions and open problems

This self-study chapter points to active research questions on the topics of the module. It cites only work whose content is summarised in the chapters above or in the official references, and it should be read with the papers at hand.

Theory of physics-informed learning

Why and when does physics help learning? Work by Doumèche, Biau and Boyer (2025) analyses the consistency of PINNs and shows the role of regularisation. Work by Doumèche, Bach, Biau and Boyer (2024) recasts physics-informed learning with linear PDEs as a kernel method, which gives convergence rates and quantifies the gain brought by the physics.

Open questions include nonlinear PDEs, where the kernel view no longer applies directly, and the interplay between optimisation and statistics: the training of PINNs is far from the idealised estimators of the theory.

Scaling neural operators

Universal physics transformers (Alkin and coauthors, 2024) and pretrained operator models such as Poseidon (Herde and coauthors, 2024) explore a foundation-model route: one large model trained on many PDE families, then adapted to a new problem with few simulations. Approximation theory for neural operators is developing in parallel (Kovachki, Lanthaler and Mishra, 2021; Lanthaler, Mishra and Karniadakis, 2022).

The open problems are data — simulations are expensive, so pretraining corpora are small — and trust: how to certify a model trained on other physics for a safety-relevant study.

Guarantees for surrogates

Conformal prediction gives distribution-free coverage (ch. 10). Jaber and coauthors (2024) combine it with GP surrogates, and Gopakumar and coauthors (2024) with neural PDE surrogates over whole fields.

Three questions remain open. Coverage is marginal, while an engineer needs it near critical configurations. Exchangeability fails when the surrogate is used outside its training distribution. And simultaneous guarantees over a whole field or trajectory require new score functions.

Deep reduced models with certificates

Deep reduced-order models (Fresca and Manzoni, 2021; Lee and Carlberg, 2020) break the Kolmogorov barrier of linear reduction. Reduced-basis methods, in turn, come with certified error bounds (Hesthaven, Rozza and Stamm, 2016). Obtaining both — nonlinear compression and a computable, rigorous error bound — is an active and largely open topic.

Sensitivity analysis and explanations

Generalised functional ANOVA for dependent inputs (Hooker, 2007; Chastaing, Gamboa and Prieur, 2012) has recently been obtained in closed form and used as a unified view of additive explanations (Ferrere, Bousquet, Gamboa and Loubes, 2026). Estimating such decompositions in high dimension, and connecting them with the explanations used for deep surrogates, are open directions.

Adaptive designs for rare events and deep surrogates

Stepwise uncertainty reduction (Bect and coauthors, 2012) gives principled sequential designs for GPs. Extending them to neural surrogates, whose uncertainty estimates are less reliable, and to batches of parallel runs on large computing clusters, remains open.

A list of open problems

  • Detect and quantify extrapolation of a surrogate outside the domain of its runs.
  • Keep autoregressive rollouts of mesh GNNs and neural operators stable over long horizons.
  • Make the Kennedy–O’Hagan model identifiable with weak prior information on the discrepancy.
  • Allocate a computing budget between fidelities, runs and validation in a principled way.
  • Agree on validation standards that let a learned surrogate enter a safety demonstration.

Key takeaways — chapter 12

  • The theory of physics-informed learning now explains when physics speeds up learning, mainly for linear PDEs.
  • Large pretrained operators are promising, but data scarcity and certification are the bottlenecks.
  • Conformal guarantees are marginal and need exchangeability; conditional and field-wise guarantees are open.
  • Certified nonlinear reduction, dependent-input explanations and adaptive designs for neural surrogates are active directions.

f = lambda x: np.sin(x[:, 0]) + a * np.sin(x[:, 1]) ** 2 + b * x[:, 2] ** 4 * np.sin(x[:, 0])
N, d = 10_000, 3
X, Xp = rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, (2, N, d))
Y = f(X)
for i in range(d):
    Xi = Xp.copy(); Xi[:, i] = X[:, i]           # keep X_i, resample the others
    Yi = f(Xi)
    m = np.mean((Y + Yi) / 2)
    S = (np.mean(Y * Yi) - m**2) / (np.mean((Y**2 + Yi**2) / 2) - m**2)   # Janon et al. (2014)
    print(f"S_{i+1} ~ {S:.3f}")

    The estimates are (0.321, 0.448, 0.024) against the exact (0.314, 0.442, 0), with 40 000 evaluations. The error, of order N^{-1/2}, is larger than that of the chaos expansion built from 600 runs. On an expensive code, pick-freeze is run on the surrogate.

  9. ★★ What a linear autoencoder learns. Assume \sigma_r > \sigma_{r+1}. Show that at a global minimum of the linear autoencoder of Proposition 6.3, the map W_2 W_1 coincides with the orthogonal projector onto the span of U_r on the range of S. (ch. 6)

    Solution: at a minimum, W_2 W_1 S is a matrix of rank at most r attaining the Eckart–Young bound. With a strict gap \sigma_r > \sigma_{r+1}, the best rank-r approximation is unique, so W_2 W_1 S = S_r = U_r U_r^\top S.

    Thus W_2 W_1 and U_r U_r^\top agree on every column of S, hence on its range. Outside the range, the loss does not see W_2 W_1. The encoder itself is only determined up to an invertible r \times r map.

  10. ★★ An inverse problem with a PINN. In -\kappa u'' = \pi^2 \sin(\pi x) with zero boundary values, the conductivity \kappa is unknown. Ten noisy measurements of u are available. Estimate \kappa jointly with the network. (ch. 7)

    Solution: train \log\kappa as an extra parameter, with a data term in the loss:

    import math
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(1)
# unknown conductivity kappa in -kappa u'' = pi^2 sin(pi x), u(0)=u(1)=0; true kappa = 2
xd = torch.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 10)[:, None]
yd = torch.sin(math.pi * xd) / 2 + 0.01 * torch.randn_like(xd)          # 10 noisy measurements
net = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(1, 32), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(32, 32),
                          torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(32, 1))
log_kappa = torch.zeros(1, requires_grad=True)                          # start from kappa = 1
u = lambda x: x * (1 - x) * net(x)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([*net.parameters(), log_kappa], lr=1e-2)
for step in range(2000):
    x = torch.rand(64, 1, requires_grad=True)
    du = torch.autograd.grad(u(x).sum(), x, create_graph=True)[0]
    d2u = torch.autograd.grad(du.sum(), x, create_graph=True)[0]
    res = -log_kappa.exp() * d2u - math.pi**2 * torch.sin(math.pi * x)
    loss = (res ** 2).mean() + 100 * ((u(xd) - yd) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print(f"estimated kappa = {log_kappa.exp().item():.3f} (true 2)")

    The estimate is \hat\kappa = 2.023. The amplitude of u is 1/\kappa, so the measurement noise (0.01 on values of order 0.5) limits the accuracy on \kappa to the order of one per cent, as observed. The weight 100 of the data term is a choice; varying it shows how the estimate trades data fit against residual.

  11. ★★ The Kalman update is a Tikhonov problem. Show that the analysis x^a of Theorem 9.6 minimises the 3D-Var functional J(x) = \|x - x^f\|^2_{(P^f)^{-1}} + \|y - Hx\|^2_{R^{-1}} . (ch. 9)

    Solution: by Proposition 9.1 with \theta_0 = x^f, C_0 = P^f and G = H, the minimiser is x^f + \big((P^f)^{-1} + H^\top R^{-1} H\big)^{-1} H^\top R^{-1}\, (y - H x^f).

    The push-through identity \big((P^f)^{-1} + H^\top R^{-1} H\big)^{-1} H^\top R^{-1} = P^f H^\top \big(H P^f H^\top + R\big)^{-1} follows by multiplying both sides by (P^f)^{-1} + H^\top R^{-1} H on the left and by H P^f H^\top + R on the right. The right-hand side is the Kalman gain.

  12. ★★ Small calibration sets. In Theorem 10.2, compute the coverage for 19 calibration points and \alpha = 0.1. What happens for 9 points and \alpha = 0.05? Show that the empirical (1-\alpha)-quantile of the scores, without the n+1 correction, can undercover. (ch. 10)

    Solution: for n = 19, k = \lceil 20 \times 0.9 \rceil = 18 and the coverage is 18/20 = 0.90 exactly.

    For n = 9 and \alpha = 0.05, k = \lceil 9.5 \rceil = 10 > n: no finite interval has the guarantee, and one must return the whole real line. In general n \geq 1/\alpha - 1 is needed.

    Without the correction, for n = 10 and \alpha = 0.1 the rank is \lceil 9 \rceil = 9, giving a coverage 9/11 = 0.82 < 0.9.

  13. ★★★ Allocating a multifidelity budget. The expensive code of Example 4.7 costs ten cheap runs. With a budget of six expensive runs, compare nested designs with (n_H, n_L) = (6, 0), (5, 10), (4, 20), (3, 30). Then add a nonlinear term 2 \sin(8x) to the cheap code and repeat. (ch. 4)

    Hints and expected result: adapt the code of Example 4.7, with expensive runs taken among the cheap ones. For the Forrester pair, the test RMSE falls from 4.22 for (6, 0) to 0.24, 0.025 and 0.006, because the discrepancy is an exact linear trend and cheap runs carry almost all the information.

    With the added term, the discrepancy needs its own GP, and the optimum moves towards more expensive runs. Explain the trade-off with Proposition 4.6: the posterior variance is \rho^2 s_L^2 + s_\delta^2, and the two terms are reduced by different runs.

  14. ★★★ Where operator learning stops transferring. With the FNO of Example 8.4, measure the test error on initial conditions containing modes up to k = 16, beyond the 8 modes seen in training and the 12 retained. Then compare with a DeepONet whose branch net reads 64 fixed sensors. (ch. 8)

    Hints and expected result: the resolution transfer of Example 8.4 holds (0.006 on 256 points), but the error on out-of-band inputs rises to about 0.06. The modes above 12 skip the Fourier weights and pass only through the pointwise path, which never learnt to damp them.

    The DeepONet cannot take the 256-point input without interpolation to its sensors. Discuss which inductive bias — Fourier truncation or fixed sensors — suits which application.

  15. ★★★ Identifiability with a model discrepancy. The simulator is f(x, \theta) = \theta x and the real system is y(x) = 2x + 0.5 x^2 + \varepsilon on [0, 1]. Calibrate \theta without discrepancy, then with a GP discrepancy as in Definition 9.4, with and without the constraints \delta(0) = \delta'(0) = 0. (ch. 9)

    Hints and expected result: without discrepancy, least squares on a uniform design converges to \hat\theta = 2 + 0.5\, \frac{\int_0^1 x^3 dx}{\int_0^1 x^2 dx} = 2.375 , with a posterior standard deviation that shrinks with n: precise and wrong.

    With a vague GP discrepancy, the posterior of \theta becomes very wide, since \theta x and \delta can trade the linear part. Constraining \delta(0) = \delta'(0) = 0 makes \theta the slope at the origin and recovers \theta = 2 (Brynjarsdóttir and O’Hagan, 2014).
