Audience: a student with a good M1 in mathematics, computer science or physics, at ease with linear algebra, probability and partial derivatives, who has never opened a scanner image and wants the models that turn such images into measurements of anatomy.

Empirical first: take two T1-weighted brain MRIs of the same person, acquired a week apart. Subtracting them shows mostly the motion of the head, not any change in the brain. Within each scan, the intensity of white matter drifts smoothly from one side of the head to the other, so a single threshold that separates grey from white matter on one side fails on the other. Registration (ch. 5–6) and model-based classification with a bias field (ch. 3) are the cures; in the code of ch. 3 the error falls from 11 % to below 1 %.

The course is an introduction to medical image analysis, centred on the two problems that start most pipelines. Segmentation asks which voxels belong to which structure; registration asks which point of one image corresponds to which point of another. Each problem is solved with an explicit model: a generative model of the intensities (mixtures, Markov fields, bias fields), a geometric model of shapes and transformations (deformable contours, rotations, diffeomorphisms), or a biophysical model of the tissues (elasticity, reaction–diffusion).

It prepares Deep Learning for Medical Imaging, where the same tasks are learned from data, and the geometric courses Geometry and Shape Spaces and Deformable Models and Geodesic Methods for Image Analysis.

What you will be able to do

  • Convert between voxel indices and world coordinates, and predict the effect of voxel size, partial volume and noise models on an analysis.
  • Filter an image with linear, morphological and nonlinear diffusion operators, and choose among them for a given noise and structure.
  • Classify tissues with a Gaussian mixture fitted by EM, add a Markov random field prior and a bias field, and prove that EM increases the likelihood.
  • Segment an organ with a snake, a level set or a Chan–Vese model, and discretise the corresponding Euler–Lagrange equations stably.
  • Register two point sets in closed form with the SVD, run ICP, and predict the target registration error from the fiducial configuration.
  • Choose a similarity measure (SSD, correlation, correlation ratio, mutual information) from a model of the relation between two modalities.
  • Run a demons registration, build diffeomorphisms by scaling and squaring, and check a deformation through its Jacobian determinant.
  • Simulate soft-tissue elasticity with finite elements and tumour growth with a reaction–diffusion equation, and say which parameters images can identify.
  • Average rotations and deformations with Fréchet means, and build a statistical shape model.

0. Review: the mathematical toolbox

Four tools come back in every chapter: the Fourier transform, for image formation and filtering; the singular value decomposition, for rigid registration and shape models; Bayes’ rule with Jensen’s inequality, for classification; and the Euler–Lagrange equation, for deformable models and registration. This chapter states them in the form used later.

0.1 Images, convolution and the Fourier transform

Definition 0.1 — Image, convolution, Fourier transform A d-dimensional image (d = 2 or 3) is a function I : \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}, sampled on a grid of voxels. The convolution of two integrable functions and the Fourier transform are (f * g)(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(y)\, g(x - y)\, dy, \qquad \hat f(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x)\, e^{-2i\pi \langle \xi, x \rangle}\, dx .

Theorem 0.2 — Convolution theorem For integrable f and g, \widehat{f * g} = \hat f \, \hat g .

Proof. By Fubini’s theorem, \widehat{f * g}(\xi) is the double integral of f(y)\, g(x - y)\, e^{-2i\pi \langle \xi, x \rangle} over x and y.

Substitute x = y + z: the exponential splits into a factor in y times a factor in z, e^{-2i\pi \langle \xi, x \rangle} = e^{-2i\pi \langle \xi, y \rangle}\, e^{-2i\pi \langle \xi, z \rangle}, and the integral factorises into \hat f(\xi)\, \hat g(\xi). ∎

The Gaussian kernel and its Fourier transform are G_\sigma(x) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2} e^{-|x|^2 / (2\sigma^2)}, \qquad \hat G_\sigma(\xi) = e^{-2\pi^2 \sigma^2 |\xi|^2} .

Multiplying two such transforms adds the variances: G_\sigma * G_\tau = G_{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}} .

The same theorem holds for discrete images with circular convolution and the discrete Fourier transform, which makes filtering cost O(N \log N) for N voxels.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
f = rng.standard_normal((64, 64))                  # a white-noise "image"
x = np.arange(64) - 32
g = np.exp(-(x[:, None]**2 + x[None, :]**2) / (2 * 2.0**2))
g = np.fft.ifftshift(g / g.sum())                  # Gaussian kernel, sigma = 2, centred at 0

# circular convolution computed directly, then through the Fourier transform
direct = np.zeros_like(f)
for i in range(64):
    for j in range(64):
        direct += f[i, j] * np.roll(np.roll(g, i, axis=0), j, axis=1)
fourier = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(np.fft.fft2(f) * np.fft.fft2(g)))
print("max |direct - Fourier| =", np.abs(direct - fourier).max())
print("std before / after smoothing:", f.std().round(3), fourier.std().round(3))

The two computations agree to 10^{-15}. The noise standard deviation drops from 1 to 0.15, close to the value 1 / (2\sqrt{\pi}\, \sigma) \approx 0.14 predicted for white noise in 2D (Exercise 3).

0.2 Least squares and the singular value decomposition

Theorem 0.3 — Singular value decomposition Every real m \times n matrix factorises as M = U \Sigma V^\top, with U and V orthogonal and \Sigma diagonal with non-negative entries \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots \geq 0.

Proof. The matrix M^\top M is symmetric positive semi-definite, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis v_1, \dots, v_n with eigenvalues \sigma_i^2.

For \sigma_i > 0, the vectors u_i = M v_i / \sigma_i are orthonormal, since \langle M v_i, M v_j \rangle = \sigma_i^2 \delta_{ij}. Completing them into an orthonormal basis gives U, and M v_i = \sigma_i u_i for every i. ∎

The next lemma is the key step of closed-form point registration (ch. 5.3).

Lemma 0.4 — Trace of an orthogonal matrix times a positive diagonal Let Q be orthogonal and \Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_n) with \sigma_i \geq 0. Then \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma) \leq \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma), with equality at Q = I, and only there when all \sigma_i > 0.

Proof. \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma) = \sum_i Q_{ii} \sigma_i, and |Q_{ii}| \leq 1 because the columns of Q are unit vectors. Equality with every \sigma_i > 0 forces Q_{ii} = 1 for all i, so each column is the corresponding basis vector. ∎

Two other facts are used without proof. The least-squares problem \min_x \|Ax - b\|^2 is solved by the normal equations A^\top A x = A^\top b. The best rank-k approximation of a centred data matrix is given by its first k singular vectors (Eckart–Young), which is principal component analysis.

0.3 Bayes’ rule, Gaussian likelihoods and Jensen’s inequality

Most models of this course are written as a likelihood p(y \mid \theta) of the image y given parameters \theta, and a prior p(\theta). Bayes’ rule gives the posterior, and the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate maximises it: p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{p(y)}, \qquad \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_\theta \big[-\log p(y \mid \theta) - \log p(\theta)\big].

With Gaussian noise, y = f(\theta) + \varepsilon and \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I), the negative log-likelihood is a sum of squares, up to a constant: -\log p(y \mid \theta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \|y - f(\theta)\|^2 + \text{const}.

This is why the sum of squared differences appears in registration (ch. 5.6) and Gaussian classes in classification (ch. 3).

Theorem 0.5 — Jensen’s inequality and the Kullback–Leibler divergence For a concave function \psi and an integrable random variable X, \mathbb{E}[\psi(X)] \leq \psi(\mathbb{E}[X]). Consequently, for two probability distributions q and p on the same finite set,

Key result — Gibbs’ inequality \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = \sum_z q(z) \log \frac{q(z)}{p(z)} \geq 0, \quad \text{with equality iff } q = p .

Proof. Jensen’s inequality follows from the tangent line: \psi(x) \leq \psi(m) + a\,(x - m) for some slope a at m = \mathbb{E}[X], and taking expectations removes the linear term.

Apply it to the concave logarithm and X = p(Z)/q(Z) with Z \sim q: -\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\log \frac{p(Z)}{q(Z)}\Big] \leq \log \sum_z q(z) \frac{p(z)}{q(z)} \leq \log 1 = 0 . The logarithm is strictly concave, so equality forces p/q to be constant on the support of q, hence p = q. ∎

0.4 Calculus of variations

Deformable models and non-rigid registration minimise functionals: energies whose unknown is a function (a curve, a displacement field).

Theorem 0.6 — Euler–Lagrange equation Let E(u) = \int_a^b L(s, u, u', u'')\, ds with L smooth, over smooth functions u that are periodic, or have fixed values and derivatives at a and b. A minimiser satisfies \frac{\partial L}{\partial u} - \frac{d}{ds} \frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} + \frac{d^2}{ds^2} \frac{\partial L}{\partial u''} = 0 .

Proof. For every admissible perturbation h (periodic, or vanishing with its derivative at a and b), the function \varepsilon \mapsto E(u + \varepsilon h) is minimal at \varepsilon = 0, so its derivative vanishes: \int_a^b \Big( \frac{\partial L}{\partial u} h + \frac{\partial L}{\partial u'} h' + \frac{\partial L}{\partial u''} h'' \Big)\, ds = 0 .

Integrating by parts once in the second term and twice in the third moves all derivatives onto the coefficients, and the boundary terms vanish. The bracket of the statement, integrated against every smooth h, gives zero, so it is zero (fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations). ∎

The left-hand side is the gradient \nabla E(u) for the L^2 inner product. The gradient flow \partial_t u = -\nabla E(u) decreases E, and is how most deformable models are run.

Example 0.7 — Two energies, two equations

  • E(u) = \frac12 \int |\nabla u|^2 in any dimension gives \nabla E = -\Delta u; its gradient flow is the heat equation \partial_t u = \Delta u (ch. 2.2).
  • Adding the data term \frac12 \int (u - f)^2 to \frac{\lambda}{2} \int |\nabla u|^2 gives the equation u - \lambda \Delta u = f. Its solution is a linear smoothing of f, and the gradient term reappears as a regulariser in registration (ch. 6.1).

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Convolution is a product in the Fourier domain; Gaussian blurs compose by adding variances.
  • The SVD and the bound \mathrm{tr}(Q\Sigma) \leq \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma) solve orthogonal least-squares problems.
  • Gaussian noise turns maximum likelihood into least squares; Jensen’s inequality gives \mathrm{KL} \geq 0, the engine of EM.
  • Minimisers of integral energies satisfy Euler–Lagrange equations, and gradient flows decrease the energy.

1. Medical image modalities and image formation

A medical image is a measurement, not a photograph: each modality measures a different physical quantity, with its own resolution, contrast and noise. This chapter derives how the main modalities form their images, because every later model (noise, bias, similarity measure) depends on it.

1.1 Anatomical and functional modalities

Anatomical modalities show structure; functional ones show a physiological process such as metabolism or blood flow.

Modality Physical quantity Typical resolution Noise and artefacts
Radiography, CT X-ray attenuation coefficient \mu sub-millimetre Gaussian-like after reconstruction; streaks near metal; ionising
MRI proton density weighted by relaxation times T_1, T_2 about 1 mm Rician magnitude noise; smooth bias field; no ionisation
PET, SPECT concentration of a radioactive tracer several millimetres Poisson counts; low resolution; ionising
Ultrasound echoes at changes of acoustic impedance sub-millimetre along the beam multiplicative speckle; shadows; real time

Several modalities are often combined: PET with CT, or anatomical and diffusion MRI. Combining them needs registration (ch. 5–6) with similarity measures adapted to different contrasts (ch. 5.6).

1.2 X-ray and computed tomography

A beam of X-ray photons crossing tissue of linear attenuation coefficient \mu loses intensity at a rate proportional to itself.

Proposition 1.1 — Beer–Lambert law If dI/ds = -\mu(s)\, I along a ray, the detected intensity is I = I_0 \exp\Big(-\int_{\text{ray}} \mu(s)\, ds\Big).

Proof. Divide by I and integrate along the ray: \log I - \log I_0 = -\int \mu\, ds. ∎

A radiograph therefore records, for each ray, the line integral \log(I_0 / I) of \mu. Computed tomography (CT) records these integrals along all directions of a slice and inverts them. In 2D, write \boldsymbol\theta = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta) and \boldsymbol\theta^\perp for the orthogonal unit vector.

Definition 1.2 — Radon transform and sinogram The Radon transform of f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R} is the set of its line integrals: (\mathcal{R} f)(\theta, s) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(s\, \boldsymbol\theta + t\, \boldsymbol\theta^\perp)\, dt, \qquad \theta \in [0, \pi),\ s \in \mathbb{R} . Displayed as an image in (\theta, s), it is the sinogram: a point source traces a sinusoid.

Theorem 1.3 — Fourier slice theorem For an integrable f, the one-dimensional Fourier transform of a projection, taken in s, is a slice of the two-dimensional Fourier transform of f: \widehat{\mathcal{R} f}(\theta, \omega) = \hat f(\omega\, \boldsymbol\theta) .

Proof. By definition, \widehat{\mathcal{R} f}(\theta, \omega) is the double integral \int_{\mathbb{R}} \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(s\boldsymbol\theta + t\boldsymbol\theta^\perp)\, e^{-2i\pi \omega s}\, dt\, ds .

The change of variables x = s\boldsymbol\theta + t\boldsymbol\theta^\perp is a rotation, with Jacobian 1, and s = \langle x, \boldsymbol\theta \rangle. The integral becomes \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} f(x)\, e^{-2i\pi \langle \omega \boldsymbol\theta, x \rangle}\, dx = \hat f(\omega \boldsymbol\theta). ∎

Theorem 1.4 — Filtered back-projection Let f be smooth and compactly supported, and filter each projection with the ramp |\omega|: \hat q_\theta(\omega) = |\omega|\, \widehat{\mathcal{R} f}(\theta, \omega) . Then f is the back-projection of the filtered projections: f(x) = \int_0^\pi q_\theta\big(\langle x, \boldsymbol\theta \rangle\big)\, d\theta .

Proof. Write the inverse Fourier transform of f in polar coordinates \xi = \omega \boldsymbol\theta, with \theta \in [0, \pi) and \omega \in \mathbb{R}; the Jacobian is |\omega|: f(x) = \int_0^\pi \int_{\mathbb{R}} \hat f(\omega \boldsymbol\theta)\, e^{2i\pi \omega \langle \boldsymbol\theta, x \rangle}\, |\omega|\, d\omega\, d\theta .

By Theorem 1.3, the product \hat f(\omega\boldsymbol\theta)\, |\omega| equals \hat q_\theta(\omega), so the inner integral is the inverse 1D Fourier transform of \hat q_\theta evaluated at \langle \boldsymbol\theta, x \rangle. ∎

The ramp amplifies high frequencies, hence noise: in practice it is multiplied by a smooth window (Shepp–Logan, Hann). Without the ramp, plain back-projection returns f blurred by a kernel in 1/|x|.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import rotate

n = 128
yy, xx = np.mgrid[-1:1:n * 1j, -1:1:n * 1j]
ell = lambda cx, cy, a, b: (((xx - cx) / a) ** 2 + ((yy - cy) / b) ** 2 <= 1) * 1.0
phantom = ell(0, 0, 0.7, 0.9) - 0.7 * ell(-0.3, 0, 0.18, 0.4) - 0.7 * ell(0.3, 0, 0.18, 0.4)
phantom += 0.5 * ell(0, -0.55, 0.06, 0.06)               # a small dense lesion

angles = np.linspace(0, 180, 180, endpoint=False)
# Radon transform: rotate the image, then integrate along the columns
sino = np.array([rotate(phantom, a, reshape=False, order=1).sum(axis=0) for a in angles])

# ramp filter |omega| applied to each projection (Theorem 1.4)
ramp = np.abs(np.fft.fftfreq(n))
filtered = np.real(np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(sino, axis=1) * ramp, axis=1))

def backproject(proj):
    rec = np.zeros((n, n))
    for a, p in zip(angles, proj):     # smear each projection back along its rays
        rec += rotate(np.tile(p, (n, 1)), -a, reshape=False, order=1)
    return rec * np.pi / len(angles)

corr = lambda a, b: np.corrcoef(a.ravel(), b.ravel())[0, 1]
bp, fbp = backproject(sino), backproject(filtered)
print(f"correlation with the phantom: plain back-projection {corr(bp, phantom):.3f}, "
      f"filtered back-projection {corr(fbp, phantom):.3f}")
# add Gaussian noise of 2 % of the maximum to the projections and reconstruct again
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
noisy = sino + 0.02 * sino.max() * rng.standard_normal(sino.shape)
fbp_noisy = backproject(np.real(np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(noisy, axis=1) * ramp, axis=1)))
print(f"with 2 % noise on the projections: {corr(fbp_noisy, phantom):.3f}")

Plain back-projection reaches a correlation of 0.76 with the phantom, filtered back-projection 0.99, and 0.97 with noisy projections.

Definition 1.5 — Hounsfield units CT intensities are reported on a scale where air is -1000 and water 0: \mathrm{HU}(\mu) = 1000\, \frac{\mu - \mu_{\text{water}}}{\mu_{\text{water}} - \mu_{\text{air}}} .

Because the HU scale is absolute, a fixed intensity window selects a tissue in every CT scan; MRI has no such scale.

1.3 Magnetic resonance imaging

In a field B_0, hydrogen nuclei precess at the Larmor frequency f_0 = \frac{\gamma}{2\pi} B_0, with \frac{\gamma}{2\pi} \approx 42.58 MHz/T: about 64 MHz at 1.5 T and 128 MHz at 3 T. A radio-frequency pulse tips their magnetisation, which then relaxes: its longitudinal part recovers with time constant T_1, its transverse part decays with T_2.

Gradient coils add a field \langle G(t), x \rangle that makes the frequency depend on position. After demodulation, the received signal is, ignoring relaxation during readout, s(t) = \int \rho(x)\, e^{-2i\pi \langle k(t), x \rangle}\, dx, \qquad k(t) = \frac{\gamma}{2\pi} \int_0^t G(\tau)\, d\tau .

The scanner thus samples the Fourier transform of the weighted proton density \rho, along a trajectory in k-space steered by the gradients.

Proposition 1.6 — Field of view and resolution Sampling k-space on a Cartesian grid of step \Delta k with N samples per axis reconstructs an image that is periodic with period \mathrm{FOV} = 1/\Delta k, with pixel size \Delta x = \frac{\mathrm{FOV}}{N} = \frac{1}{N\, \Delta k} .

Proof. Sampling \hat\rho with step \Delta k multiplies it by a Dirac comb; by the Poisson summation formula, the inverse transform is the sum of the copies of \rho shifted by multiples of 1/\Delta k.

Keeping N samples truncates k-space to a width N \Delta k, which limits the resolution to 1/(N\Delta k). ∎

An object larger than the field of view folds back onto itself: this is the wrap-around artefact. Contrast is set by the sequence timing. For a spin-echo sequence with repetition time TR and echo time TE, the signal of a tissue is approximately S \propto \rho \,\big(1 - e^{-\mathrm{TR}/T_1}\big)\, e^{-\mathrm{TE}/T_2} .

Short TR and TE give a T_1-weighted image (white matter bright), long TR and TE a T_2-weighted one (fluid bright).

Noise and bias. The k-space samples carry independent complex Gaussian noise, and the unitary Fourier transform keeps it complex Gaussian with variance \sigma^2 per component. Displayed images are magnitudes. A voxel of true value A then has a Rician density p(m) = \frac{m}{\sigma^2} \exp\Big(-\frac{m^2 + A^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\, I_0\Big(\frac{A m}{\sigma^2}\Big), \qquad m \geq 0, where I_0 is a modified Bessel function. In the background (A = 0), it reduces to a Rayleigh density of mean \sigma\sqrt{\pi/2}. The inhomogeneity of the receive coils also multiplies the image by a smooth bias field, which ch. 3.4 estimates.

1.4 Nuclear imaging and ultrasound

PET. A positron emitted by the tracer annihilates with an electron into two photons of 511 keV emitted in opposite directions. Detecting both defines a line of response. With x_j the activity in voxel j and a_{ij} the probability that a decay in j is detected on line i, the counts are independent: y_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}\big((Ax)_i\big) .

Shepp and Vardi (1982) maximise this likelihood with the multiplicative MLEM update, which keeps the activity non-negative: x_j \leftarrow \frac{x_j}{\sum_i a_{ij}} \sum_i a_{ij} \frac{y_i}{(Ax)_i} .

The update is an instance of the EM algorithm, whose monotonicity is proved in ch. 3.2.

Ultrasound. A transducer emits a short pulse and records its echoes; an echo received after a time t comes from the depth d = c\, t / 2, with c \approx 1540 m/s in soft tissue. Within a resolution cell, many sub-resolution scatterers add with random phases. Their sum is approximately complex Gaussian, so the envelope follows a Rayleigh law: this is speckle.

Example 1.7 — Speckle is multiplicative For a Rayleigh variable of scale s, the mean is s\sqrt{\pi/2} and the standard deviation s\sqrt{(4 - \pi)/2}. Their ratio does not depend on s: \frac{\text{mean}}{\text{std}} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4 - \pi}} \approx 1.91 .

A brighter region is proportionally noisier, so filters designed for additive Gaussian noise (ch. 2) are often applied to the logarithm of ultrasound images.

1.5 From scanner to array: voxels and world coordinates

A scanner stores an array I[i, j, k] together with the position of each voxel in the patient. Analysis software must use that geometry, not the array indices.

Definition 1.8 — Voxel-to-world transform The world position of voxel (i, j, k) is given by an affine map x_{\text{world}} = M \begin{pmatrix} i \\ j \\ k \end{pmatrix} + t, where the columns of M are the axis directions scaled by the voxel spacing, and t is the position of voxel (0, 0, 0).

The two common formats disagree on the orientation of the world axes. DICOM, the hospital format, uses patient coordinates in which x points to the patient’s left, y to the back and z to the head (LPS). NIfTI, the research format, stores the affine map for axes pointing to the right, the front and the head (RAS). Forgetting the sign flip between them swaps left and right.

Example 1.9 — Anisotropic voxels and partial volume A clinical brain MRI often has voxels of 0.9 \times 0.9 \times 5 mm, so one voxel holds 4 mm³. A voxel that straddles the boundary between grey matter (fraction \alpha) and cerebrospinal fluid has an intensity close to the mixture \alpha \mu_{\text{GM}} + (1 - \alpha)\mu_{\text{CSF}}.

This partial-volume effect blurs every boundary over one voxel. It is larger across the 5 mm direction, and biases volumes measured by counting voxels.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • CT measures line integrals of attenuation; the Fourier slice theorem makes filtered back-projection exact, and the ramp filter amplifies noise.
  • MRI samples the Fourier transform of a weighted proton density: the k-space step sets the field of view, the extent of k-space sets the resolution.
  • Noise models differ: Rician magnitudes in MRI, Poisson counts in PET, multiplicative speckle in ultrasound, plus a smooth bias field in MRI.
  • An image is an array plus a voxel-to-world affine map; anisotropic voxels and partial volume matter for every measurement.

2. Image filtering and low-level operations

Before any model, images are denoised, differentiated, cleaned and resampled. This chapter presents these low-level operators with the property that decides when to use each one.

2.1 Noise and linear filtering

Model an image as y = x + \varepsilon, with \varepsilon white noise of variance \sigma^2. Averaging n independent voxels divides the variance by n, but also averages across edges.

A linear filter is a convolution y * h. The Gaussian kernel G_\sigma is the standard choice, for three reasons:

  • it is separable: a 3D filter is three 1D filters, so the cost per voxel grows like 3k instead of k^3 for a kernel of width k;
  • it is smooth and positive, so it creates no oscillation;
  • it generates the scale-space of the next section.

2.2 Scale-space and differential operators

Proposition 2.1 — Gaussian smoothing solves the heat equation The solution of \partial_t u = \Delta u with u(\cdot, 0) = u_0 is u(\cdot, t) = G_{\sqrt{2t}} * u_0 . Smoothing at scale \sigma then at scale \tau equals smoothing at scale \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}.

Proof. In the Fourier domain, the equation reads \partial_t \hat u = -4\pi^2 |\xi|^2 \hat u, so \hat u(\xi, t) = e^{-4\pi^2 |\xi|^2 t}\, \hat u_0(\xi).

This is \hat G_\sigma\, \hat u_0 with 2\pi^2\sigma^2 = 4\pi^2 t, that is \sigma^2 = 2t. The semigroup property is G_\sigma * G_\tau = G_{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}} (ch. 0.1). ∎

Derivatives commute with convolution: \partial_i (G_\sigma * u) = (\partial_i G_\sigma) * u. Image derivatives are therefore computed stably by convolving with derivatives of a Gaussian. The gradient magnitude |\nabla (G_\sigma * u)| is large on edges, and its local maxima along the gradient direction give the Canny edge detector.

Second derivatives describe the local shape of the intensity. Let \lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3 be the eigenvalues of the Hessian of G_\sigma * u at a voxel, sorted by increasing absolute value.

Definition 2.2 — Hessian signature of a bright tube Inside a bright tubular structure of radius close to \sigma, such as a contrast-filled vessel, |\lambda_1| \approx 0, \qquad \lambda_2 \approx \lambda_3 \ll 0 : the intensity is flat along the vessel axis and decreases in the two orthogonal directions. A bright plate has one large negative eigenvalue; a bright blob has three.

Frangi et al. (1998) turn this signature into a vesselness measure, zero when \lambda_2 > 0 or \lambda_3 > 0, and otherwise V = \Big(1 - e^{-R_A^2 / (2\alpha^2)}\Big)\, e^{-R_B^2 / (2\beta^2)}\, \Big(1 - e^{-S^2 / (2c^2)}\Big), with R_A = |\lambda_2| / |\lambda_3| (tube versus plate), R_B = |\lambda_1| / \sqrt{|\lambda_2 \lambda_3|} (tube versus blob) and S = \|\lambda\| (contrast). Taking the maximum of V over several scales \sigma detects vessels of several radii.

2.3 Nonlinear filtering

Linear filters blur edges because they ignore them. Three nonlinear filters avoid this.

  • The median filter replaces a voxel by the median of its neighbourhood. It removes isolated outliers and preserves a straight step edge exactly.
  • The bilateral filter (Tomasi and Manduchi, 1998) averages neighbours with weights that decrease with both the spatial distance and the intensity difference.
  • Anisotropic diffusion (Perona and Malik, 1990) slows the heat equation where the gradient is large.

Definition 2.3 — Perona–Malik diffusion With an edge threshold K > 0 and a decreasing diffusivity c, \partial_t u = \operatorname{div}\big(c(|\nabla u|)\, \nabla u\big), \qquad c(s) = \frac{1}{1 + (s/K)^2} \ \text{ or } \ c(s) = e^{-(s/K)^2} .

Proposition 2.4 — Forward and backward diffusion In one dimension, with the flux \phi(s) = s\, c(s), the equation reads \partial_t u = \phi'(u_x)\, u_{xx}. For the first diffusivity, \phi'(s) = \frac{1 - (s/K)^2}{\big(1 + (s/K)^2\big)^2} . Slopes below K are smoothed (forward diffusion); slopes above K are sharpened (backward diffusion).

Proof. \partial_t u = \partial_x \phi(u_x) = \phi'(u_x)\, u_{xx} by the chain rule, and differentiating s / (1 + s^2/K^2) gives the formula. Where \phi' > 0 the equation is a heat equation with a positive coefficient; where \phi' < 0 it is a backward heat equation, which concentrates the variation. ∎

Backward diffusion makes the continuous problem ill-posed. Catté et al. (1992) restore well-posedness by evaluating c on the smoothed gradient |\nabla (G_\sigma * u)|. In practice an explicit scheme with a few dozen steps is used.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
clean = np.zeros((96, 96)); clean[:, 48:] = 1.0            # a vertical step edge
noisy = clean + 0.15 * rng.standard_normal(clean.shape)

def perona_malik(u, K=0.3, tau=0.2, steps=50):
    """Explicit scheme for u_t = div(c(|grad u|) grad u), c(s) = exp(-(s/K)^2)."""
    u = u.copy()
    c = lambda d: np.exp(-(d / K) ** 2)
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = np.pad(u, 1, mode="edge")                        # no flux across the border
        d = [p[:-2, 1:-1] - u, p[2:, 1:-1] - u, p[1:-1, :-2] - u, p[1:-1, 2:] - u]
        u = u + tau * sum(c(di) * di for di in d)            # four neighbour fluxes
    return u

gauss = gaussian_filter(noisy, 2.0)
pm = perona_malik(noisy)
flat = (slice(None), slice(8, 40))                           # a region far from the edge
for name, u in [("noisy", noisy), ("Gaussian", gauss), ("Perona-Malik", pm)]:
    jump = u[:, 49].mean() - u[:, 46].mean()                 # contrast across 3 pixels
    print(f"{name:13s} noise std {u[flat].std():.3f}   jump across the edge {jump:.2f}")

The Gaussian filter reduces the noise from 0.151 to 0.021 but halves the edge contrast (0.55). Perona–Malik reduces it to 0.009 and keeps the full jump of 1.00.

2.4 Mathematical morphology

Morphology processes binary masks, such as thresholded images, with a structuring element B, a small symmetric set like a ball. Write B_x for B translated to x.

Definition 2.5 — Erosion, dilation, opening, closing For a set X: \varepsilon_B(X) = \{x : B_x \subset X\}, \qquad \delta_B(X) = \{x : B_x \cap X \neq \emptyset\}, and the opening and closing are \gamma_B = \delta_B \circ \varepsilon_B and \varphi_B = \varepsilon_B \circ \delta_B. On grey-level images, erosion and dilation become the minimum and maximum over B_x.

Proposition 2.6 — The opening is a filter The opening \gamma_B(X) is the union of the translates B_y contained in X. Consequently it is anti-extensive, increasing and idempotent: \gamma_B(X) \subset X, \qquad X \subset Y \Rightarrow \gamma_B(X) \subset \gamma_B(Y), \qquad \gamma_B(\gamma_B(X)) = \gamma_B(X) .

Proof. A point x lies in \delta_B(\varepsilon_B(X)) iff some y with B_y \subset X satisfies x \in B_y, by symmetry of B. This is the union formula, and the first two properties follow from it.

For idempotence, every B_y \subset X is also contained in \gamma_B(X), so it appears in the union defining \gamma_B(\gamma_B(X)). Hence \gamma_B(X) \subset \gamma_B(\gamma_B(X)), and the reverse inclusion is anti-extensivity. ∎

An opening removes structures thinner than B, such as noise specks and thin bridges between organs; a closing fills holes and gaps smaller than B. Together with connected-component labelling and the distance transform, these operators clean every segmentation of this course.

2.5 Histograms and thresholding

Thresholding at t splits the voxels into two classes with proportions \omega_0(t), \omega_1(t) and means \mu_0(t), \mu_1(t). The total variance splits into a within-class and a between-class part, the latter being \sigma_B^2(t) = \omega_0 \omega_1 (\mu_0 - \mu_1)^2 .

Otsu’s method (1979) chooses the t that maximises \sigma_B^2, which is the same as minimising the within-class variance since their sum does not depend on t. It works when the histogram is bimodal; ch. 3 replaces it with a probabilistic model.

2.6 Interpolation and resampling

Registration and reformatting evaluate images between grid points. An interpolating model writes I(x) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}^d} c_k\, \beta(x - k), with a kernel \beta: nearest neighbour (the box \beta^0), linear (the tent \beta^1), or the cubic B-spline \beta^3. For \beta^3 the coefficients c_k are not the samples, and a recursive prefilter computes them (Unser, 1999).

Label maps must be resampled by nearest neighbour, or by interpolating one probability map per label. Downsampling needs a Gaussian prefilter, otherwise high frequencies alias into the coarse grid.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Gaussian smoothing is the heat equation; scales compose by adding variances, and derivatives are computed by convolving with Gaussian derivatives.
  • Hessian eigenvalues detect tubes, plates and blobs, the basis of vesselness filters.
  • Perona–Malik diffusion smooths slopes below a threshold set by K (K itself for the first diffusivity, K/\sqrt2 for the exponential one) and sharpens those above it.
  • Openings and closings are idempotent filters that remove small structures and fill small holes.
  • Interpolate intensities with splines and labels with nearest neighbour; prefilter before downsampling.

3. Image classification with generative models of intensities

Tissue classification assigns each voxel to a class, such as grey matter, white matter or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the brain. This chapter builds a generative model of the intensities, a mixture of Gaussians, then adds spatial regularity and the bias field, and fits everything with the EM algorithm.

3.1 Voxel classification as a Bayesian decision

Let z_i \in \{1, \dots, K\} be the class of voxel i and y_i its intensity. If class k has prior probability \pi_k and intensity density p(y \mid k), the classifier that minimises the probability of error picks the class of maximal posterior: \hat z_i = \arg\max_k \ \pi_k\, p(y_i \mid k) .

Proposition 3.1 — Two Gaussian classes With p(y \mid k) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma^2) for k = 0, 1 and \mu_0 < \mu_1, the Bayes classifier chooses class 1 iff y > t, where t = \frac{\mu_0 + \mu_1}{2} + \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu_1 - \mu_0} \log \frac{\pi_0}{\pi_1} .

Proof. Class 1 wins iff \log \pi_1 - \frac{(y - \mu_1)^2}{2\sigma^2} > \log \pi_0 - \frac{(y - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma^2} . The quadratic terms in y cancel, which leaves a linear inequality in y; solving it gives t. ∎

Example 3.2 — Grey and white matter Take \mu_0 = 0.6 (grey matter), \mu_1 = 0.9 (white matter), \sigma = 0.08, and priors 0.6 and 0.4. The threshold moves from the midpoint 0.75 towards the rarer class: t = 0.75 + \frac{0.0064}{0.3} \log 1.5 \approx 0.759 .

The error rate is 0.6\, P(Z > 1.98) + 0.4\, P(Z > 1.77) \approx 0.0297, against 0.0304 at the midpoint.

In practice the means, variances and priors are unknown and differ between scanners, so they must be estimated from the image itself.

3.2 Gaussian mixtures and the EM algorithm

Definition 3.3 — Gaussian mixture model The intensities are independent with density p(y \mid \theta) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(y; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2), \qquad \theta = (\pi_k, \mu_k, \sigma_k)_{k \leq K} . The class z_i is a hidden variable: P(z_i = k) = \pi_k, and given z_i = k, y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2).

The log-likelihood, a sum over voxels of the logarithm of a sum over classes, has no closed-form maximiser. If the classes were known, the estimates would be class proportions, means and variances. EM alternates between guessing the classes softly and re-estimating:

  • E-step: compute the responsibilities r_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(y_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\sum_l \pi_l\, \mathcal{N}(y_i; \mu_l, \sigma_l^2)} = P(z_i = k \mid y_i, \theta) ;
  • M-step: with n_k = \sum_i r_{ik}, update \pi_k = \frac{n_k}{n}, \qquad \mu_k = \frac{1}{n_k} \sum_i r_{ik}\, y_i, \qquad \sigma_k^2 = \frac{1}{n_k} \sum_i r_{ik} (y_i - \mu_k)^2 .

Theorem 3.4 — EM never decreases the likelihood For any distribution q of the hidden classes z = (z_i), define the free energy F(q, \theta) = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p(y, z \mid \theta)\big] - \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log q(z)\big] .

Key result — the EM decomposition \log p(y \mid \theta) = F(q, \theta) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\|\, p(z \mid y, \theta)\big)

The E-step maximises F in q and the M-step maximises it in \theta. Hence the iterates satisfy \log p(y \mid \theta^{t+1}) \geq \log p(y \mid \theta^t) .

Proof. Write p(y, z \mid \theta) = p(z \mid y, \theta)\, p(y \mid \theta), take the logarithm and the expectation under q: this is the decomposition.

The E-step sets q^t = p(z \mid y, \theta^t), which cancels the divergence, so F(q^t, \theta^t) = \log p(y \mid \theta^t).

The M-step chooses \theta^{t+1} maximising F(q^t, \cdot), whose \theta-dependent part is the expected complete log-likelihood; setting its derivatives to zero gives the updates above. Since the divergence is non-negative (Theorem 0.5), \log p(y \mid \theta^{t+1}) \geq F(q^t, \theta^{t+1}) \geq F(q^t, \theta^t) = \log p(y \mid \theta^t). ∎

Under mild regularity conditions, EM converges to a stationary point of the likelihood, which depends on the initialisation; k-means or intensity quantiles are common starts. The likelihood is unbounded if a class collapses on a single intensity (\sigma_k \to 0), so a variance floor is useful.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 128
yy, xx = np.mgrid[-1:1:n * 1j, -1:1:n * 1j]
r = np.sqrt(xx ** 2 + (yy / 1.2) ** 2)
truth = np.select([r < 0.25, r < 0.55, r < 0.8], [0, 2, 1], -1)   # 0 CSF, 1 GM, 2 WM
truth[(np.abs(xx) < 0.06) & (r < 0.55)] = 0                        # a CSF "sulcus"
mask = truth >= 0
y = np.array([0.3, 0.6, 0.9])[truth[mask]] + 0.08 * rng.standard_normal(mask.sum())

def em_gmm(y, mu, iters=50):
    """EM for a 1D Gaussian mixture (Definition 3.3); returns parameters and responsibilities."""
    K = len(mu); pi = np.full(K, 1 / K); var = np.full(K, y.var())
    for _ in range(iters):
        logp = -0.5 * (y[:, None] - mu) ** 2 / var - 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * var) + np.log(pi)
        resp = np.exp(logp - logp.max(1, keepdims=True))
        resp /= resp.sum(1, keepdims=True)                          # E-step
        nk = resp.sum(0)                                            # M-step
        pi, mu = nk / len(y), resp.T @ y / nk
        var = (resp * (y[:, None] - mu) ** 2).sum(0) / nk
    loglik = np.log(np.exp(logp).sum(1)).sum()
    return pi, mu, np.sqrt(var), resp, loglik

pi, mu, sd, resp, ll = em_gmm(y, mu=np.quantile(y, [0.1, 0.5, 0.9]))
print("weights", pi.round(3), " means", mu.round(3), " std", sd.round(3))
print("true weights", np.bincount(truth[mask]) / mask.sum())
print(f"voxel error rate {(resp.argmax(1) != truth[mask]).mean():.3%}")

EM recovers the means 0.32, 0.60, 0.90 and the class proportions within three percentage points. The error rate of 4.4 % is close to the Bayes error of this noise level: the overlap of the Gaussians, not the fit, limits it.

3.3 Spatial priors: Markov random fields

Voxels are classified independently above, so isolated noisy voxels get isolated wrong labels. Neighbouring voxels usually share a class, which a prior on the label image z can express.

Definition 3.5 — Potts model On a grid where i \sim j denotes neighbouring voxels, the Potts prior with parameter \beta > 0 is p(z) \propto \exp\Big(-\beta \sum_{i \sim j} \mathbb{1}[z_i \neq z_j]\Big) . The MAP labelling under the mixture likelihood minimises the energy U(z) = -\sum_i \log\big(\pi_{z_i}\, p(y_i \mid z_i)\big) + \beta \sum_{i \sim j} \mathbb{1}[z_i \neq z_j] .

The Potts model is a Markov random field: given its neighbours, a label is independent of the rest of the image. By the Hammersley–Clifford theorem, every positive distribution with this Markov property has such a Gibbs form: the exponential of minus a sum of potentials over cliques of the neighbourhood graph. Geman and Geman (1984) brought this Gibbs view to image restoration.

Proposition 3.6 — Iterated conditional modes ICM (Besag, 1986) visits the voxels in turn and replaces z_i by the label k minimising its local energy U_i(k) = -\log\big(\pi_k\, p(y_i \mid k)\big) + \beta\, \#\{j \sim i : z_j \neq k\} . Each update does not increase U, and ICM stops after finitely many sweeps.

Proof. U is the local energy of voxel i plus terms that do not involve z_i, so minimising the local energy minimises U over z_i with the others fixed.

Keep the current label in case of ties: every change then strictly decreases U. There are finitely many labellings, so changes stop. ∎

ICM reaches a local minimum. For two classes the exact MAP is a minimum cut in a graph (Greig, Porteous and Seheult, 1989). Van Leemput et al. (1999) and Zhang, Brady and Smith (2001) combine the Potts prior with EM, through approximations of the label posterior, which is intractable on a grid.

3.4 The bias field and atlas priors

The MRI bias field of ch. 1.3 multiplies the image by a smooth function. Taking logarithms makes it additive: \ell_i = \log y_i = \log x_i + b_i, with b smooth. Wells et al. (1996) and Van Leemput et al. (1999) estimate b inside EM; here b = \Phi c, where the columns of \Phi are smooth basis functions, such as low-degree polynomials, evaluated at the voxels.

Proposition 3.7 — The bias step of EM is a weighted least-squares fit Let the log-intensities of class k be Gaussian with mean \mu_k + b_i and variance \sigma_k^2, and let r_{ik} be the responsibilities. With the class parameters fixed, the M-step for c solves \Phi^\top W \Phi\, c = \Phi^\top W \rho, \qquad w_i = \sum_k \frac{r_{ik}}{\sigma_k^2}, \qquad \rho_i = \ell_i - \frac{1}{w_i} \sum_k \frac{r_{ik}\, \mu_k}{\sigma_k^2}, with W = \mathrm{diag}(w_i).

Proof. Up to terms without b, the expected complete log-likelihood is -\sum_i \sum_k \frac{r_{ik}}{2\sigma_k^2} (\ell_i - b_i - \mu_k)^2 .

Expanding in b_i, it equals -\frac12 \sum_i w_i (b_i - \rho_i)^2 plus a constant. Maximising over b = \Phi c is the weighted least-squares problem, whose normal equations are the statement (ch. 0.2). ∎

The residual \rho_i is the log-intensity minus the class mean expected at that voxel. The basis must not contain a constant, which the class means already represent. It must also be too smooth to mimic anatomy: on the concentric phantom below, a quadratic basis can reproduce the radial layout of the tissues, and the error rises to about a third of the voxels.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 128
yy, xx = np.mgrid[-1:1:n * 1j, -1:1:n * 1j]
r = np.sqrt(xx ** 2 + (yy / 1.2) ** 2)
truth = np.select([r < 0.25, r < 0.55, r < 0.8], [0, 2, 1], -1)   # 0 CSF, 1 GM, 2 WM
truth[(np.abs(xx) < 0.06) & (r < 0.55)] = 0
mask = truth >= 0
bias = np.exp(0.3 * xx + 0.15 * yy)                               # smooth multiplicative field
img = bias * np.array([0.3, 0.6, 0.9])[np.maximum(truth, 0)] + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal((n, n))
logy = np.log(np.clip(img[mask], 1e-3, None))                     # work on log-intensities
Phi = np.stack([xx[mask], yy[mask]], 1)       # bias basis: degree-1 polynomial, no constant

def em(logy, bias_basis=None, iters=60):
    K = 3; mu = np.quantile(logy, [0.1, 0.5, 0.9]); var = np.full(K, 0.05); pi = np.full(K, 1 / K)
    b = np.zeros_like(logy)
    for _ in range(iters):
        res = logy - b
        logp = -0.5 * (res[:, None] - mu) ** 2 / var - 0.5 * np.log(var) + np.log(pi)
        R = np.exp(logp - logp.max(1, keepdims=True)); R /= R.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        nk = R.sum(0); pi = nk / len(logy)
        mu = R.T @ res / nk; var = (R * (res[:, None] - mu) ** 2).sum(0) / nk
        if bias_basis is not None:                                 # Proposition 3.7
            w = (R / var).sum(1)
            target = logy - (R * mu / var).sum(1) / w
            c = np.linalg.solve(bias_basis.T @ (w[:, None] * bias_basis), bias_basis.T @ (w * target))
            b = bias_basis @ c
    return logp, R

def icm(unary, beta_mrf=1.0, sweeps=5):
    """Iterated conditional modes for the Potts prior on the 4-neighbour grid (Proposition 3.6)."""
    lab = -np.ones((n, n), int); lab[mask] = unary.argmax(1)
    U = np.zeros((n, n, 3)); U[mask] = unary
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        for i, j in zip(*np.nonzero(mask)):
            nb = [lab[a, b] for a, b in ((i-1, j), (i+1, j), (i, j-1), (i, j+1)) if lab[a, b] >= 0]
            lab[i, j] = np.argmax(U[i, j] - beta_mrf * np.array([sum(l != k for l in nb) for k in range(3)]))
    return lab[mask]

err = lambda lab: (lab != truth[mask]).mean()
logp0, R0 = em(logy)
logp1, R1 = em(logy, Phi)
print(f"EM without bias model : error {err(R0.argmax(1)):.1%}")
print(f"EM with bias model    : error {err(R1.argmax(1)):.1%}")
print(f"  + Potts prior (ICM) : error {err(icm(logp1)):.1%}")

The bias field alone raises the error of plain EM to 10.8 %. Estimating it inside EM brings the error down to 0.6 %, and the Potts prior to 0.2 %.

Atlas priors. A probabilistic atlas gives, at each position, the frequency of each tissue over a population. Once the atlas is aligned with the image, a registration problem solved in ch. 5–6, its values replace the global proportions \pi_k by voxel-wise priors \pi_{ik} (Van Leemput et al., 1999). The atlas also tells apart structures with the same intensity, such as two grey-matter nuclei.

3.5 Discriminative classifiers and evaluation

Generative models describe how intensities arise and need no training labels. Discriminative classifiers learn P(z \mid \text{features}) from labelled images instead. Typical examples are k-nearest neighbours or random forests on features computed by the filters of ch. 2 (smoothed intensities, gradients, Hessian eigenvalues), and the convolutional networks of Deep Learning for Medical Imaging. They need annotated data and may fail on a scanner they have not seen.

Definition 3.8 — Overlap and distance measures For a segmentation A and a reference B (sets of voxels), \mathrm{Dice}(A, B) = \frac{2|A \cap B|}{|A| + |B|}, \qquad \mathrm{J}(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}, \qquad \mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2\mathrm{J}}{1 + \mathrm{J}} . The Hausdorff distance between the boundaries \partial A and \partial B is d_H(\partial A, \partial B) = \max\Big(\sup_{a \in \partial A} d(a, \partial B),\ \sup_{b \in \partial B} d(b, \partial A)\Big) .

The relation between Dice and Jaccard follows from |A| + |B| = |A \cup B| + |A \cap B|. Overlap measures forgive boundary errors on large organs and punish them on small structures; the Hausdorff distance, often replaced by its 95th percentile, measures the worst boundary error in millimetres.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • The Bayes classifier thresholds the posterior; with Gaussian classes the threshold moves towards the rarer class.
  • EM maximises the free energy alternately in the class distribution and the parameters, so the likelihood never decreases.
  • A Potts prior removes isolated errors; ICM decreases its energy monotonically.
  • In the log domain, the bias step of EM is a weighted least-squares fit of smooth functions to the residuals.
  • Report Dice and a boundary distance; Dice and Jaccard are equivalent.

4. Segmentation with deformable contours and surfaces

Voxel classification ignores shape: it cannot separate two touching organs of equal intensity, nor bridge a missing edge. Deformable models move a curve or a surface under two forces, an internal one that keeps it smooth and an external one that pulls it towards the boundaries of the image.

4.1 Snakes

Definition 4.1 — Snake A snake (Kass, Witkin and Terzopoulos, 1988) is a closed curve v : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^2 minimising E(v) = \int_0^1 \Big( \frac{\alpha}{2} |v'(s)|^2 + \frac{\beta}{2} |v''(s)|^2 + P\big(v(s)\big) \Big)\, ds, where \alpha penalises stretching, \beta penalises bending, and the potential P = -|\nabla (G_\sigma * I)|^2 is low on edges.

By Theorem 0.6 applied to each coordinate, a minimiser satisfies the force balance -\alpha\, v'' + \beta\, v'''' + \nabla P(v) = 0 .

Discretise the curve into n points v_i with unit spacing. The internal energy becomes a quadratic form \frac12 v^\top A v with A = \alpha L + \beta L^2, where L is the circulant matrix of the second difference, with rows (-1, 2, -1).

Proposition 4.2 — Explicit and semi-implicit snakes For an even number of points n, the explicit gradient step v \leftarrow v - \tau (A v + \nabla P(v)) is stable for the internal forces iff \tau \leq \frac{2}{4\alpha + 16\beta} . The semi-implicit step (I + \tau A)\, v^{t+1} = v^t - \tau \nabla P(v^t) damps every mode of the internal forces for all \tau > 0.

Proof. L is circulant, with eigenvalues \mu_k = 2 - 2\cos(2\pi k / n) \in [0, 4], so A has eigenvalues \alpha \mu_k + \beta \mu_k^2 \in [0, 4\alpha + 16\beta]. For even n, the upper value is reached at k = n/2.

The explicit step multiplies mode k by 1 - \tau(\alpha\mu_k + \beta\mu_k^2), which stays in [-1, 1] iff \tau satisfies the bound. The semi-implicit step multiplies it by 1 / (1 + \tau(\alpha\mu_k + \beta\mu_k^2)) \in (0, 1]. ∎

Kass et al. use the semi-implicit scheme: one banded linear solve per step. Two extensions widen the narrow capture range of the edge potential. Cohen (1991) adds a balloon force of constant magnitude along the normal, which inflates the snake from a seed until edges stop it. Xu and Prince (1998) replace -\nabla P by a gradient vector flow field that diffuses the edge forces into homogeneous regions.

4.2 Level sets and region-based contours

Parametric contours cannot split or merge, and their points bunch up. Osher and Sethian (1988) represent the curve implicitly instead, as the zero level of a function \varphi(x, t), negative inside. Write N = -\nabla\varphi / |\nabla\varphi| for the inward unit normal.

Proposition 4.3 — Level-set equation If the curve moves with speed F along N, that is \partial_t C = F N, then \partial_t \varphi = F\, |\nabla \varphi| .

Proof. Differentiate \varphi(C(p, t), t) = 0 in t: \partial_t \varphi + \langle \nabla\varphi, \partial_t C \rangle = 0 . Since \langle \nabla\varphi, N \rangle = -|\nabla\varphi|, the second term is -F|\nabla\varphi|. ∎

The equation is solved on a fixed grid, and topology changes happen without special treatment. The curvature of the level lines is the divergence of the unit normal field, \kappa = \operatorname{div} n with n = \nabla\varphi / |\nabla\varphi|. With F = \kappa, the curve-shortening flow, a circle of radius r shrinks with \dot r = -1/r: r(t) = \sqrt{r_0^2 - 2t} .

Geodesic active contours. Caselles, Kimmel and Sapiro (1997) minimise a length weighted by an edge-stopping function, such as g = 1 / (1 + |\nabla (G_\sigma * I)|^2): L_g(C) = \int g\big(C(s)\big)\, ds .

Its gradient flow is \partial_t C = (g\kappa - \langle \nabla g, N \rangle) N, and by Proposition 4.3 the level-set form is \partial_t \varphi = |\nabla\varphi|\, \operatorname{div}\Big(g\, \frac{\nabla\varphi}{|\nabla\varphi|}\Big) .

The curve settles on the minimal paths of the metric g, which follow the edges.

Chan–Vese. Edges are weak in noisy images, so Chan and Vese (2001) use regions instead. They fit two constants c_1, c_2 inside and outside the curve: E(c_1, c_2, C) = \mu\, \mathrm{Length}(C) + \lambda_1 \int_{\text{inside}} (I - c_1)^2 + \lambda_2 \int_{\text{outside}} (I - c_2)^2 .

This is a generative model in the sense of ch. 3: two Gaussian classes of equal variance, with \lambda = 1/(2\sigma^2), plus a prior on the length of their boundary. For fixed C, c_1 and c_2 are the means inside and outside. With \varphi > 0 inside and a smoothed Dirac \delta_\epsilon, the gradient flow is \partial_t \varphi = \delta_\epsilon(\varphi) \Big[ \mu \operatorname{div}\Big(\frac{\nabla\varphi}{|\nabla\varphi|}\Big) - \lambda_1 (I - c_1)^2 + \lambda_2 (I - c_2)^2 \Big] . 

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n = 100
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:n, 0:n]
truth = ((xx - 40) ** 2 + (yy - 50) ** 2 < 18 ** 2) | ((xx - 66) ** 2 + (yy - 50) ** 2 < 12 ** 2)
img = 0.4 + 0.3 * truth + 0.25 * rng.standard_normal((n, n))     # weak contrast, strong noise

def curvature(phi):
    """div(grad phi / |grad phi|), the curvature of the level lines."""
    gy, gx = np.gradient(phi)
    norm = np.sqrt(gx ** 2 + gy ** 2) + 1e-8
    return np.gradient(gx / norm, axis=1) + np.gradient(gy / norm, axis=0)

def chan_vese(img, mu=0.5, dt=1.0, iters=300, eps=1.0):
    phi = np.sin(np.pi * xx / 8) * np.sin(np.pi * yy / 8)          # many small seeds
    for _ in range(iters):
        inside = phi > 0
        c1, c2 = img[inside].mean(), img[~inside].mean()           # region means
        delta = eps / (np.pi * (eps ** 2 + phi ** 2))              # smoothed Dirac
        phi = phi + dt * delta * (mu * curvature(phi) - (img - c1) ** 2 + (img - c2) ** 2)
    inside = phi > 0
    return inside if img[inside].mean() > img[~inside].mean() else ~inside

dice = lambda a, b: 2 * (a & b).sum() / (a.sum() + b.sum())
print(f"Dice, threshold of the raw image    : {dice(img > 0.55, truth):.3f}")
print(f"Dice, threshold of a smoothed image : {dice(gaussian_filter(img, 1.5) > 0.55, truth):.3f}")
print(f"Dice, Chan-Vese level set           : {dice(chan_vese(img), truth):.3f}")

At a noise level close to the contrast, thresholding the raw image gives a Dice of 0.44. Chan–Vese reaches 0.96 and estimates the two intensities itself, starting from many small seeds. Smoothing then thresholding does as well (0.97) only because the threshold was set at the ideal value, which requires knowing both intensities.

4.3 Deformable surfaces

In 3D the contour becomes a closed surface, discretised as a mesh. Delingette (1999) uses simplex meshes, in which every vertex has exactly three neighbours. This makes the mean curvature at a vertex computable from the vertex and its three neighbours alone, and simplex meshes are dual to triangulations. Internal forces regularise the curvature, external forces pull each vertex along its normal towards nearby edges, and the vertices follow discrete Newtonian dynamics.

The simplest internal force moves each vertex towards the average of its neighbours, the umbrella operator. It shrinks the model, as the closed polygon shows.

Proposition 4.4 — Laplacian smoothing shrinks On a closed polygon of n vertices, consider the step v_i \leftarrow v_i + \lambda\Big(\tfrac12 (v_{i-1} + v_{i+1}) - v_i\Big), \qquad 0 < \lambda \leq \tfrac12 . It keeps the centroid fixed and multiplies the k-th Fourier mode of the vertex positions by 1 - \lambda \Big(1 - \cos \frac{2\pi k}{n}\Big) . Every non-constant mode, including the overall size of the shape, shrinks.

Proof. The step is the circulant matrix I - \frac{\lambda}{2} L, diagonal in the discrete Fourier basis with the eigenvalues of L given in Proposition 4.2.

Mode 0 is the centroid, with factor 1; since 1 - \cos lies in (0, 2] for the other modes, their factors lie in [0, 1) when \lambda \leq \frac12. ∎

A circle sampled by 100 points and smoothed 1000 times with \lambda = 0.5 keeps only 0.37 of its radius. Taubin (1995) alternates a shrinking step \lambda > 0 and an inflating step \mu < -\lambda, which leaves low frequencies almost unchanged and damps high ones.

4.4 Physics-based deformable models

The internal energy can also be a physical one: the model is an elastic body. Discretised by finite elements (ch. 7.1), its displacement u obeys Lagrangian dynamics, with mass M, damping D, stiffness K and image forces f: M \ddot u + D \dot u + K u = f(u) .

Terzopoulos, Witkin and Kass (1988) introduced such models to recover 3D shape and non-rigid motion, and Cohen and Cohen (1993) solved balloon models with finite elements in 2D and 3D. The stiffness matrix then encodes a biophysical prior: soft regions deform more than stiff ones. Chapter 7 develops the elastic model, and ch. 8.4 adds a statistical prior on shapes learned from a population.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • A snake balances stretching and bending energies against an edge potential; semi-implicit steps are stable for any step size.
  • Level sets move curves by \partial_t\varphi = F|\nabla\varphi| and change topology freely; curvature motion shrinks a circle as \sqrt{r_0^2 - 2t}.
  • Geodesic active contours follow edges; Chan–Vese fits a two-region Gaussian model with a length prior and works without edges.
  • Laplacian smoothing of meshes shrinks every non-constant mode; physics-based models replace it with an elastic energy.

5. Parametric registration: the geometry of rigid and affine transformations

Registration finds the transformation that maps each point of one image onto the corresponding point of another: two scans of a patient, a scan and an atlas, two modalities. This chapter treats transformations with few parameters, starting with rotations, whose geometry is not that of a vector space.

5.1 The registration problem

Given a fixed image J and a moving image I, registration seeks a transformation T in a class \mathcal{T} minimising E(T) = \mathcal{D}(J, I \circ T) + \lambda\, \mathcal{R}(T), where \mathcal{D} measures dissimilarity and \mathcal{R} regularises. Feature-based methods match extracted points or surfaces; intensity-based methods compare voxel values directly.

The classes are groups under composition. In 3D, rigid transformations x \mapsto Rx + t have 6 parameters, similarities 7 and affine maps 12. Rigid registration suits the head, with its skull; deformable registration (ch. 6) is needed for soft organs and between subjects.

5.2 Rotations and the exponential map

Rotations form the group SO(3) = \{R : R^\top R = I,\ \det R = 1\}, a curved 3-dimensional manifold. Near the identity, R \approx I + W with W skew-symmetric. Write [\omega]_\times for the skew matrix with [\omega]_\times x = \omega \times x.

Theorem 5.1 — Rodrigues’ formula For a unit axis n and an angle \theta, with K = [n]_\times, \exp(\theta K) = I + \sin\theta\, K + (1 - \cos\theta)\, K^2 . It is the rotation of angle \theta about n; every rotation has this form, so \exp maps the vectors \omega = \theta n with |\omega| \leq \pi onto SO(3).

Proof. For a unit vector, K^2 = n n^\top - I, hence K^3 = -K. Grouping the odd and even terms of the exponential series gives \theta - \theta^3/3! + \dots = \sin\theta in front of K, and \theta^2/2! - \theta^4/4! + \dots = 1 - \cos\theta in front of K^2.

The matrix fixes n (since Kn = 0) and acts as a rotation of angle \theta in the plane orthogonal to n, where K^2 = -I. Conversely, every rotation fixes an axis (Euler’s theorem). ∎

The inverse, the logarithm, follows from the trace: \mathrm{tr}\, R = 1 + 2\cos\theta, so \theta = \arccos\big((\mathrm{tr}\, R - 1)/2\big). For 0 < \theta < \pi, the axis is read off (R - R^\top)/(2\sin\theta) = K.

The geodesic distance between two rotations is the angle of R_1^\top R_2. Unit quaternions q = (\cos\frac\theta2, \sin\frac\theta2\, n) represent the same rotations, q and -q giving the same one. They compose by the quaternion product, cheaper than a product of 3 \times 3 matrices, and avoid the singularities of Euler angles.

Example 5.2 — A quarter turn The rotation of 90° about z maps (x, y, z) to (-y, x, z). Its trace is 0 + 0 + 1 = 1, so \theta = \arccos 0 = \pi/2, and (R - R^\top)/2 = [e_z]_\times gives the axis e_z. The arithmetic mean of this rotation and the identity has determinant 1/2: it is not a rotation (ch. 8).

5.3 Point-based registration in closed form

Suppose N landmarks x_i in one image correspond to y_i in the other, such as anatomical points or fiducial markers. Write \bar x, \bar y for the centroids, and add a prime for centred points: x_i' = x_i - \bar x.

Theorem 5.3 — Orthogonal Procrustes problem Let M = \sum_i y_i' x_i'^\top = U \Sigma V^\top (SVD). A minimiser of \sum_i |R x_i + t - y_i|^2 over rotations R and translations t is R^\star = U\, \mathrm{diag}\big(1, 1, \det(U V^\top)\big)\, V^\top, \qquad t^\star = \bar y - R^\star \bar x .

Proof. For fixed R, the criterion is quadratic in t and its gradient vanishes at t = \bar y - R\bar x; substituting leaves \sum_i |R x_i' - y_i'|^2.

Expanding the squares, |R x_i'|^2 = |x_i'|^2, so it remains to maximise \sum_i y_i'^\top R x_i' = \mathrm{tr}(R M^\top) = \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma), \qquad Q = U^\top R V, where Q is orthogonal.

By Lemma 0.4, \mathrm{tr}(Q\Sigma) \leq \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma) with equality at Q = I, that is R = U V^\top. This is a rotation when \det(UV^\top) = 1.

Otherwise Q must have determinant -1, and the maximum of \mathrm{tr}(Q \Sigma) over such Q is \sigma_1 + \sigma_2 - \sigma_3, reached at Q = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1) (Umeyama, 1991). ∎

Arun, Huang and Blostein (1987) gave the SVD solution and Horn (1987) an equivalent one with quaternions. The determinant correction matters when the points are nearly coplanar or very noisy.

5.4 Predicting the registration error

Landmarks are localised with an error, the fiducial localisation error (FLE). The residual after registration is the fiducial registration error (FRE). What matters clinically is the target registration error (TRE) at a point r of interest, such as a tumour, where there is no landmark.

Theorem 5.4 — Error prediction (Fitzpatrick, West and Maurer, 1998) Assume independent, isotropic localisation errors of mean square \mathrm{FLE}^2, small compared with the extent of the configuration. To first order, \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{FRE}^2] \approx \Big(1 - \frac{2}{N}\Big) \mathrm{FLE}^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{TRE}^2(r)] \approx \frac{\mathrm{FLE}^2}{N} \Big(1 + \frac13 \sum_{k=1}^3 \frac{d_k^2}{f_k^2}\Big), where d_k is the distance from r to the k-th principal axis of the landmarks, and f_k the RMS distance of the landmarks to that axis.

Proof. Sketch. Linearise the Procrustes solution around the true transformation: the estimated translation and rotation are linear in the errors. The residual is the projection of the 3N error coordinates onto the orthogonal complement of the 6-dimensional space of infinitesimal rigid motions, whose expected squared norm is (3N - 6)/(3N) of the total. For the TRE, the translation error contributes \mathrm{FLE}^2/N, and the rotation error is amplified by the distance of r to each axis, damped by the spread f_k of the landmarks around it. ∎

Two lessons follow. First, spread the landmarks widely and place the target near their centroid. Second, the FRE does not depend on where the target is: a small residual does not prove a small error at the target.

import numpy as np

def procrustes(X, Y):
    """Rotation R and translation t minimising sum |R x_i + t - y_i|^2 (Theorem 5.3)."""
    xm, ym = X.mean(0), Y.mean(0)
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd((Y - ym).T @ (X - xm))            # M = sum y'_i x'_i^T
    D = np.diag([1.0, 1.0, np.sign(np.linalg.det(U @ Vt))])     # forbid reflections
    R = U @ D @ Vt
    return R, ym - R @ xm

def rodrigues(w):
    th = np.linalg.norm(w); K = np.cross(np.eye(3), w / th)     # K = [n]_x
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(th) * K + (1 - np.cos(th)) * K @ K

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
fid = np.array([[60, 0, 0], [-60, 0, 0], [0, 80, 0], [0, -80, 20.]])   # 4 fiducials (mm)
target = np.array([20.0, 30.0, 60.0])
R0, t0 = rodrigues(np.array([0.2, -0.1, 0.4])), np.array([5.0, -3.0, 12.0])
fle = 1.0                                                     # RMS localisation error (mm)
fre2, tre2 = [], []
for _ in range(20000):
    X = fid                                                   # exact in the first space
    Y = fid @ R0.T + t0 + fle / np.sqrt(3) * rng.standard_normal(fid.shape)
    R, t = procrustes(X, Y)
    fre2.append(np.mean(np.sum((X @ R.T + t - Y) ** 2, 1)))
    tre2.append(np.sum((R @ target + t - (R0 @ target + t0)) ** 2))

# prediction of Fitzpatrick, West and Maurer (1998), Theorem 5.4
N, c = len(fid), fid - fid.mean(0)
_, _, axes = np.linalg.svd(c)                                 # principal axes of the fiducials
dist2 = lambda P, a: np.sum(P ** 2, -1) - (P @ a) ** 2        # squared distance to an axis
f2 = np.array([dist2(c, a).mean() for a in axes])
d2 = np.array([dist2(target - fid.mean(0), a) for a in axes])
fle2 = fle ** 2
print(f"FRE^2  simulated {np.mean(fre2):.3f}   predicted {(1 - 2 / N) * fle2:.3f}")
print(f"TRE^2  simulated {np.mean(tre2):.3f}   predicted {fle2 / N * (1 + np.sum(d2 / f2) / 3):.3f}")

With four markers and an FLE of 1 mm, the simulation gives a mean square FRE of 0.500 mm² and a mean square TRE of 0.537 mm², matching the predictions 0.500 and 0.539.

5.5 Iterative closest point

Without known correspondences, for instance to align a surface extracted from CT with points digitised on the patient, Besl and McKay (1992) alternate two steps:

  1. match each point x_i to its closest point y_{c(i)} on the target surface;
  2. solve the Procrustes problem (Theorem 5.3) for these pairs, and apply the result to the x_i.

Proposition 5.5 — ICP converges monotonically The criterion e = \sum_i |R x_i + t - y_{c(i)}|^2 never increases, so it converges.

Proof. Step 1 replaces each matched point by the closest one, which cannot increase any term. Step 2 minimises e over (R, t) for fixed matches. The sequence is non-increasing and bounded below by 0. ∎

The limit is a local minimum, so ICP needs a rough initial alignment. Robust variants discard the worst matches, and Chen and Medioni (1992) minimise distances to tangent planes instead of points.

5.6 Intensity-based registration: similarity measures

The choice of \mathcal{D} encodes an assumption on how the intensities of the two images relate at corresponding points (Roche, Malandain and Ayache, 2000).

Definition 5.6 — Four similarity measures Over the overlap of J and I \circ T:

  • SSD, the sum of squared differences, assumes J = I \circ T + \varepsilon with Gaussian noise: it is the negative log-likelihood (ch. 0.3).
  • Normalised correlation assumes an affine relation J = a\, (I \circ T) + b + \varepsilon.
  • The correlation ratio, the fraction of the variance of J explained by I \circ T, assumes a functional relation J = f(I \circ T) + \varepsilon (Roche et al., 1998): \eta^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Var}(J \mid I \circ T)]}{\mathrm{Var}(J)} .
  • Mutual information, \mathrm{MI} = H(I \circ T) + H(J) - H(I \circ T, J), computed from the joint histogram, assumes only statistical dependence (Maes et al., 1997; Viola and Wells, 1997).

Proposition 5.7 — Properties of mutual information For discrete intensities, \mathrm{MI}(X, Y) \geq 0, \quad \text{with equality iff } X \text{ and } Y \text{ are independent.}

It is unchanged when X is replaced by g(X) with g one-to-one.

For a function f, the value \mathrm{MI}(X, f(X)) = H(f(X)) is the maximum over all Y with the same law as f(X).

Proof. \mathrm{MI}(X, Y) = \mathrm{KL}(p_{XY} \,\|\, p_X p_Y), which is non-negative and vanishes iff p_{XY} = p_X p_Y (Theorem 0.5). A one-to-one g only relabels the bins of the joint histogram.

Finally \mathrm{MI}(X, Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X), and H(Y \mid X) = 0 iff Y is a function of X. ∎

In words, mutual information is maximal when one image predicts the other, whatever the intensity mapping, which suits pairs such as T_1 and T_2 MRI or CT and MRI. Studholme, Hill and Hawkes (1999) normalise it to reduce its dependence on the size of the overlap.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import rotate, gaussian_filter

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n = 128
yy, xx = np.mgrid[-1:1:n * 1j, -1:1:n * 1j]
labels = (np.sqrt((xx / 0.8) ** 2 + (yy / 0.6) ** 2) < 1).astype(int)       # 1 = tissue A
labels[(xx - 0.3) ** 2 + (yy + 0.1) ** 2 < 0.08] = 2                       # 2 = tissue B
labels[(xx + 0.35) ** 2 + yy ** 2 < 0.03] = 3                              # 3 = tissue C
t1 = np.array([0.0, 0.7, 0.4, 1.0])[labels]          # "T1-like" contrast
t2 = np.array([0.9, 0.2, 1.0, 0.5])[labels]          # "T2-like": a non-monotone remapping
t1 = gaussian_filter(t1, 1) + 0.03 * rng.standard_normal((n, n))
t2 = gaussian_filter(t2, 1) + 0.03 * rng.standard_normal((n, n))

def mutual_information(a, b, bins=32):
    h, _, _ = np.histogram2d(a.ravel(), b.ravel(), bins=bins)
    p = h / h.sum(); pa, pb = p.sum(1), p.sum(0)
    nz = p > 0
    return np.sum(p[nz] * np.log(p[nz] / np.outer(pa, pb)[nz]))

true_angle = 12.0
moving = rotate(t2, true_angle, reshape=False, order=1, mode="nearest")    # T2 image, rotated
angles = np.arange(-30, 31, 1.0)
ssd = [np.mean((rotate(moving, -a, reshape=False, order=1, mode="nearest") - t1) ** 2) for a in angles]
mi = [mutual_information(rotate(moving, -a, reshape=False, order=1, mode="nearest"), t1) for a in angles]
print("SSD is minimal at", angles[np.argmin(ssd)], "degrees")
print("MI  is maximal at", angles[np.argmax(mi)], "degrees (true:", true_angle, ")")

SSD is minimal at the edge of the search range, -30°, because it tries to match bright to bright. Mutual information peaks at the true angle, 12°.

In practice, the rigid parameters are optimised by Gauss–Newton or Powell’s method over a pyramid of downsampled images (coarse to fine), with rotations updated through the exponential map, R \leftarrow R \exp([\delta\omega]_\times), and the moving image interpolated as in ch. 2.6.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Rotations form a curved group: use Rodrigues’ formula and the logarithm, not averages of matrices.
  • Point-based rigid registration has a closed form through the SVD of the cross-covariance, with a determinant correction.
  • The expected TRE grows with the distance of the target from the principal axes of the landmarks; a small FRE says nothing about the TRE.
  • ICP alternates closest-point matching and Procrustes, and decreases its criterion monotonically.
  • Choose the similarity measure from the intensity relation: SSD (identity), correlation (affine), correlation ratio (functional), mutual information (statistical).

6. Deformable registration and diffeomorphisms

Soft organs deform, and two brains differ by more than a rigid motion. Deformable registration estimates a dense displacement field. It has as many unknowns as voxels, so the regulariser carries the model of what deformations are plausible.

6.1 Non-parametric registration and regularisation

Write the transformation as \varphi(x) = x + u(x) and minimise, for SSD, E(u) = \frac12 \int \big(J(x) - I(x + u(x))\big)^2 dx + \lambda\, \mathcal{R}(u) .

Three regularisers are classical:

  • diffusion, \mathcal{R}(u) = \frac12 \sum_k \int |\nabla u_k|^2, whose Euler–Lagrange term is -\Delta u;
  • linear elasticity, a biophysical prior with Lamé coefficients \mu and \lambda_L (ch. 7.1), where \varepsilon(u) is the symmetric part of \nabla u: \mathcal{R}(u) = \int \mu\, \|\varepsilon(u)\|^2 + \frac{\lambda_L}{2} (\operatorname{div} u)^2 ;
  • curvature, \frac12 \sum_k \int (\Delta u_k)^2, which leaves affine maps unpenalised.

Bajcsy and Kovačič (1989) developed multiresolution elastic matching; Christensen, Rabbitt and Miller (1996) replaced it with a viscous fluid to allow large deformations.

Proposition 6.1 — The aperture problem Linearise the residual at one voxel: J - I(x + u) \approx d - g^\top u with d = J(x) - I(x) and g = \nabla I(x). If g \neq 0, the displacements that cancel it form a line (in 2D) or a plane (in 3D), and the one of minimal norm is u = \frac{d}{|g|^2}\, g .

Proof. The solutions of g^\top u = d are the minimal-norm solution plus any vector orthogonal to g. That solution is parallel to g, and g^\top (s g) = d gives s = d / |g|^2. ∎

Intensities thus determine motion only along the gradient, and nothing in uniform regions. The regulariser propagates the information from edges into the rest of the image.

6.2 Free-form deformations with B-splines

Rueckert et al. (1999) parametrise u by displacements c_k of control points on a grid of spacing h, combined with the cubic B-spline \beta^3 of ch. 2.6: u(x) = \sum_{k} c_k\, \beta^3\Big(\frac{x}{h} - k\Big), \qquad \beta^3(x_1, \dots, x_d) = \prod_{i=1}^d \beta^3(x_i) .

The field is C^2, and each control point influences only 4^d cells, so gradients are cheap. A coarse-to-fine sequence of grids captures large then small deformations.

The transformation folds where the Jacobian determinant \det(I + \nabla u) becomes non-positive. Choi and Lee (2000) proved that an FFD is one-to-one when every control-point displacement stays below about 0.48\, h in 2D and 0.40\, h in 3D. Rueckert et al. (2006) compose many such small FFDs to obtain large diffeomorphic deformations.

Example 6.2 — Folding in one dimension The map \varphi(x) = x + a \sin x has derivative 1 + a \cos x. It is a diffeomorphism iff |a| < 1 .

At |a| = 1 it is still one-to-one, but its inverse is not differentiable where the derivative vanishes. For |a| > 1 the derivative changes sign and the map folds the line onto itself.

6.3 The demons algorithm

Thirion (1998) imagined “demons” at the edges of J pushing the moving image along \nabla J. Pennec, Cachier and Ayache (1999) showed that the resulting update is a regularised second-order step on the SSD.

Proposition 6.3 — The demons force At voxel x, let d = J(x) - (I \circ \varphi)(x), g = \nabla J(x) and \alpha > 0. The minimiser of (d - g^\top v)^2 + \alpha |v|^2 is v = \frac{d}{|g|^2 + \alpha}\, g . With the adaptive choice \alpha = d^2 / \sigma_x^2, this is Thirion’s force, and every update satisfies |v| \leq \sigma_x / 2.

Proof. The gradient in v is -2g(d - g^\top v) + 2\alpha v. It vanishes iff (g g^\top + \alpha I) v = g d, a system with a positive definite matrix and hence a unique solution.

Trying v = s g gives s(|g|^2 + \alpha) = d, which is that solution.

For the bound, |v| = |d|\,|g| / (|g|^2 + d^2/\sigma_x^2), and |g|^2 + d^2/\sigma_x^2 \geq 2 |g|\,|d| / \sigma_x by the inequality between arithmetic and geometric means. ∎

The algorithm alternates the force computation, the update u \leftarrow u + v, and a Gaussian smoothing of the field. Smoothing the total field acts like an elastic regulariser, smoothing only the update like a fluid one; Cachier et al. (2003) interpret the alternation as minimising an energy with an auxiliary field.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter, map_coordinates

n = 96
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:n, 0:n].astype(float)
J = gaussian_filter(((xx - 48) ** 2 + (yy - 48) ** 2 < 22 ** 2) * 1.0, 1.5)           # fixed: disc
I = gaussian_filter((((xx - 44) / 26) ** 2 + ((yy - 50) / 17) ** 2 < 1) * 1.0, 1.5)  # moving: ellipse

def warp(img, u):
    """I o phi with phi(x) = x + u(x), linear interpolation (ch. 2.6)."""
    return map_coordinates(img, [yy + u[0], xx + u[1]], order=1, mode="nearest")

def jacobian_det(u):
    (u0y, u0x), (u1y, u1x) = np.gradient(u[0]), np.gradient(u[1])
    return (1 + u0y) * (1 + u1x) - u0x * u1y

gJ = np.array(np.gradient(J))                       # gradient of the fixed image
u = np.zeros((2, n, n))
sigma_x = 1.0                                       # maximal step: sigma_x / 2 (Prop. 6.3)
for it in range(150):
    diff = J - warp(I, u)
    denom = (gJ ** 2).sum(0) + diff ** 2 / sigma_x ** 2
    v = np.where(denom > 1e-9, diff * gJ / np.maximum(denom, 1e-9), 0.0)   # demons force
    u = gaussian_filter(u + v, sigma=(0, 2.0, 2.0))  # add the update, then smooth the field
mse0, mse1 = np.mean((J - I) ** 2), np.mean((J - warp(I, u)) ** 2)
print(f"mean squared difference: {mse0:.4f} before, {mse1:.5f} after demons")
print(f"min Jacobian determinant {jacobian_det(u).min():.3f}, max |u| = {np.abs(u).max():.1f} pixels")

The mean squared difference drops from 0.035 to 2 \times 10^{-5}, with displacements up to 8 pixels. The minimum Jacobian determinant, 0.55, shows a strong local compression but no folding; nothing in additive demons guarantees it.

6.4 Diffeomorphic registration

A diffeomorphism is a smooth bijection with a smooth inverse. Requiring \varphi to be one keeps the topology of the anatomy: no tearing, no folding. Diffeomorphisms do not form a vector space, but a group, and they are built by integrating velocity fields.

Definition 6.4 — Flow of a velocity field For a smooth, bounded velocity field v with bounded derivatives, the flow \phi_t solves \partial_t \phi_t(x) = v\big(\phi_t(x)\big), \qquad \phi_0(x) = x . By the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem each \phi_t is a diffeomorphism, with inverse the flow of -v. Its time-one map is the group exponential \exp(v) = \phi_1.

Proposition 6.5 — Scaling and squaring For a stationary velocity field, \phi_{s+t} = \phi_s \circ \phi_t. Consequently, \exp(v) = \Big(\exp\big(v / 2^N\big)\Big)^{2^N}, where the power denotes composition, and \exp(v / 2^N) \approx \mathrm{id} + v / 2^N for large N.

Proof. Both t \mapsto \phi_{s+t}(x) and t \mapsto \phi_t(\phi_s(x)) solve the same differential equation with the same value at t = 0, so they coincide by uniqueness; the two orders agree by symmetry. Applying the identity N times with s = t = 2^{-k} gives the formula. ∎

Arsigny et al. (2006) used this to compute exponentials with N compositions instead of 2^N integration steps. Vercauteren et al. (2009) made demons diffeomorphic by composing exponentials of the updates, \varphi \leftarrow \varphi \circ \exp(v). Their log-domain variant (2008) keeps \varphi = \exp(w) for a velocity w, so the inverse \exp(-w) comes for free and the registration can be made symmetric.

import numpy as np
from scipy.ndimage import map_coordinates

n = 128
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:n, 0:n].astype(float)
# a smooth, divergence-free "swirl" velocity field: its flow preserves areas
bump = 60.0 * np.exp(-((xx - 64) ** 2 + (yy - 64) ** 2) / (2 * 15.0 ** 2))
v = np.array([bump * (xx - 64) / 15.0, -bump * (yy - 64) / 15.0])

def compose(a, b):
    """Displacement of (id + a) o (id + b): b(x) + a(x + b(x))."""
    return b + np.array([map_coordinates(c, [yy + b[0], xx + b[1]], order=1, mode="nearest") for c in a])

def expmap(v, N=7):
    """Scaling and squaring: exp(v) = exp(v / 2^N) composed 2^N times with itself."""
    u = v / 2 ** N
    for _ in range(N):
        u = compose(u, u)
    return u

def jacobian_det(u):
    (u0y, u0x), (u1y, u1x) = np.gradient(u[0]), np.gradient(u[1])
    return (1 + u0y) * (1 + u1x) - u0x * u1y

print(f"max |v| = {np.abs(v).max():.1f} pixels")
print(f"id + v  : min Jacobian determinant {jacobian_det(v).min():.3f}")
print(f"exp(v)  : min Jacobian determinant {jacobian_det(expmap(v)).min():.3f}")
inv_err = np.abs(compose(expmap(v), expmap(-v))).max()
print(f"exp(v) o exp(-v) = id up to {inv_err:.2f} pixels")

The displacement \mathrm{id} + v folds (minimum determinant -1.16), whereas \exp(v) stays positive (0.95). The flow of this divergence-free field preserves areas exactly, so the deviation from 1 and the 1-pixel inverse error come from the discretisation: interpolation, finite differences and the first step \mathrm{id} + v/2^N.

Large deformations. LDDMM (Beg et al., 2005) lets the velocity depend on time and minimises E(v) = \int_0^1 \|v_t\|_V^2\, dt + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \big\|I \circ \phi_1^{-1} - J\big\|^2, where \|\cdot\|_V is a Sobolev norm. The first term is the energy of a path of diffeomorphisms for a right-invariant metric. Optimal paths are geodesics, whose energy is the squared geodesic distance, so they give a distance between shapes. This framework of computational anatomy is developed in Geometry and Shape Spaces.

6.5 Evaluating deformable registration

A low image dissimilarity proves little: a wild field can match intensities while scrambling anatomy. Registrations are evaluated instead by:

  • the overlap (Dice, ch. 3.5) of labels propagated by \varphi against manual labels, as in the comparison of 14 algorithms by Klein et al. (2009);
  • the distance between corresponding landmarks not used for registration (TRE, ch. 5.4);
  • the Jacobian determinant: the fraction of non-positive values (folds) and the plausibility of the volume changes;
  • inverse consistency: registering I to J and J to I should give inverse maps.

Propagating the labels of an annotated atlas to a new image is atlas-based segmentation. With several atlases, fusing the propagated labels by majority vote or by local weights is more robust than any single atlas. It also provides the aligned atlas priors of ch. 3.4.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Intensities constrain motion only along the gradient (aperture problem); the regulariser — diffusion, elastic, curvature — fills in the rest.
  • B-spline free-form deformations are smooth and local; small control-point displacements guarantee injectivity.
  • The demons force is a regularised Gauss–Newton step whose length is at most \sigma_x / 2.
  • Flows of smooth velocity fields are diffeomorphisms; scaling and squaring computes \exp(v) with a few compositions.
  • Evaluate with labels, landmarks and Jacobians, never with the image similarity alone.

7. Biophysical models: from images to simulation

Images give the geometry of organs; biophysical models describe how tissues deform, grow or conduct. Combined, they yield patient-specific models, whose parameters are estimated from images and which can then predict or simulate. This chapter presents two such models and the inverse problem of personalisation.

7.1 Linear elasticity and finite elements

Definition 7.1 — Linear elasticity For a displacement u of a body \Omega, the strain and the stress (Hooke’s law) are \varepsilon(u) = \tfrac12 \big(\nabla u + \nabla u^\top\big), \qquad \sigma = \lambda_L\, \mathrm{tr}(\varepsilon)\, I + 2\mu\, \varepsilon, with Lamé coefficients \mu = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)} and \lambda_L = \frac{E \nu}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)} for a Young modulus E and a Poisson ratio \nu. The stored energy is W(u) = \int_\Omega \mu\, \|\varepsilon(u)\|^2 + \frac{\lambda_L}{2} \big(\mathrm{tr}\, \varepsilon(u)\big)^2 \, dx . At equilibrium under a force density f, \operatorname{div} \sigma + f = 0.

Soft tissues are nearly incompressible, with \nu close to 0.5, which makes \lambda_L large. Linear elasticity is valid only for small strains; large deformations need nonlinear laws.

The finite element method writes u = \sum_j u_j N_j with piecewise linear “hat” functions N_j on a tetrahedral mesh of the organ. Minimising W(u) - \int f \cdot u over this space gives a sparse linear system K \mathbf{u} = F.

Proposition 7.2 — The stiffness matrix K is symmetric positive semi-definite. With \mu > 0 and \lambda_L \geq 0, on a connected mesh its kernel consists of the nodal values of the infinitesimal rigid motions u(x) = a + \omega \times x. Fixing three non-collinear nodes, for instance where an organ is attached, makes the reduced matrix positive definite.

Proof. For the discrete field u_h with nodal vector \mathbf{u}, we have \mathbf{u}^\top K \mathbf{u} = 2W(u_h) \geq 0. Since \mu > 0, W(u_h) = 0 forces \varepsilon(u_h) = 0 on every element.

On a tetrahedron u_h is affine, so its gradient is a constant skew-symmetric matrix: u_h(x) = a + \omega \times x there.

An infinitesimal rigid motion is fixed by its values at three non-collinear points, and neighbouring tetrahedra share three such nodes. On a connected mesh, all elements therefore carry the same motion. ∎

import numpy as np

# 1D bar of length L, Young modulus E, clamped at x = 0, free at x = L, uniform load f
L, E, f, n_el = 1.0, 2.0, 1.0, 8
x = np.linspace(0, L, n_el + 1); h = L / n_el
K = np.zeros((n_el + 1, n_el + 1)); F = np.zeros(n_el + 1)
for e in range(n_el):                                   # assemble P1 element matrices
    idx = [e, e + 1]
    K[np.ix_(idx, idx)] += E / h * np.array([[1, -1], [-1, 1]])
    F[idx] += f * h / 2
print("smallest |eigenvalue| of K before boundary conditions:", np.abs(np.linalg.eigvalsh(K)).min().round(12))
u = np.zeros(n_el + 1)
u[1:] = np.linalg.solve(K[1:, 1:], F[1:])               # clamp: remove the first node
exact = f / E * (L * x - x ** 2 / 2)                    # solution of -E u'' = f, u(0) = 0, u'(L) = 0
print("max nodal error:", np.abs(u - exact).max())
print("tip displacement:", u[-1], "exact", f * L ** 2 / (2 * E))

The assembled matrix has a zero eigenvalue (the rigid translation of the bar) until the end is clamped. In 1D, P1 elements are exact at the nodes: the tip moves by fL^2 / (2E) = 0.25.

Cotin, Delingette and Ayache (1999) used precomputed linear elastic models for real-time surgery simulation. Ferrant et al. (2001) drove a finite element model of the brain with intraoperative MRI surfaces, a biomechanical registration of the brain shift during surgery.

7.2 Tumour growth by reaction–diffusion

Diffuse gliomas infiltrate the brain beyond their visible boundary. The Fisher–Kolmogorov model describes the normalised tumour cell density u \in [0, 1] by \partial_t u = \operatorname{div}(D \nabla u) + \rho\, u (1 - u), with a diffusion D (cell motility) and a proliferation rate \rho. Swanson, Alvord and Murray (2000) made D larger in white matter than in grey matter. Jbabdi et al. (2005) made it anisotropic along the fibres seen in diffusion MRI.

Proposition 7.3 — Speed of the invasion front In 1D with constant D, a travelling front u(x, t) = U(x - ct) with U decreasing from 1 to 0 and U \geq 0 requires c \geq 2\sqrt{D \rho} . From a compactly supported initial tumour, the front moves asymptotically at this minimal speed (Kolmogorov, Petrovsky and Piskunov, 1937; Aronson and Weinberger, 1978).

Proof. Necessity. Far ahead of the front, U is small and solves the linearised equation D U'' + c U' + \rho U = 0. Its characteristic roots are r = \frac{-c \pm \sqrt{c^2 - 4D\rho}}{2D} . If c^2 < 4D\rho, they are complex, and U oscillates around 0, which contradicts U \geq 0. The selection of the minimal speed is the content of the cited theorems. ∎

import numpy as np

D, rho = 0.1, 1.0                         # diffusion (mm^2/day) and proliferation (1/day)
L, nx = 120.0, 4000
dx = L / nx; dt = 0.4 * dx ** 2 / D       # explicit scheme, stable for dt <= dx^2 / (2D)
x = np.arange(nx) * dx
u = np.where(x < 2.0, 1.0, 0.0)           # a small initial tumour at the left end
front, times, t = [], [], 0.0
while t < 150.0:
    lap = np.zeros_like(u)
    lap[1:-1] = (u[2:] - 2 * u[1:-1] + u[:-2]) / dx ** 2
    lap[0], lap[-1] = 2 * (u[1] - u[0]) / dx ** 2, 2 * (u[-2] - u[-1]) / dx ** 2  # no flux
    u = u + dt * (D * lap + rho * u * (1 - u))
    t += dt
    if t > 50.0:                          # after the transient: track the u = 0.5 front
        front.append(x[np.argmax(u < 0.5)]); times.append(t)
speed = np.polyfit(times, front, 1)[0]
print(f"measured front speed {speed:.3f} mm/day, theory 2 sqrt(D rho) = {2 * np.sqrt(D * rho):.3f}")

The simulated front moves at 0.626 against 2\sqrt{D\rho} = 0.632, in illustrative units; the small gap is the known logarithmic delay of fronts started from a compact tumour.

Imaging sees only where the density exceeds a detection threshold. Two scans give the speed of the visible boundary, hence \sqrt{D\rho}. The distance between the boundaries seen on two MRI sequences with different detection thresholds reflects the slope of the profile, of order \sqrt{D/\rho}; with the speed, it identifies D and \rho. Konukoglu et al. (2010) personalise the model from image time series with an eikonal approximation of the front. Clatz et al. (2005) coupled the growth with the elastic model of ch. 7.1 to simulate the mass effect.

7.3 Personalisation as an inverse problem

A biophysical model M_\theta predicts observables H(M_\theta), such as a tumour boundary or the activation times of the heart. Personalisation estimates \theta from image-derived data y: \hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta \ \big\|y - H(M_\theta)\big\|^2 + \mathcal{R}(\theta) .

Each evaluation of H is a simulation, so derivative-free optimisers, adjoint methods or fast surrogate models are used. The central question is identifiability: following a single visible boundary over time only gives \sqrt{D\rho}, as above.

In cardiac electrophysiology, the activation time T(x) of the myocardium approximately solves an eikonal equation c(x)\, |\nabla T| = 1, with a conduction velocity c larger along the fibres. Fitting c to measured activation maps personalises the model; Sermesant et al. (2012) used such personalised models to predict the effect of cardiac resynchronisation therapy. Ch. 9 lists the open problems of these “digital twins”.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Linear elasticity: strain, Hooke’s law with Lamé coefficients, and a stiffness matrix whose kernel is the rigid motions.
  • The Fisher–Kolmogorov model predicts an invasion front moving at 2\sqrt{D\rho}.
  • Personalising a model is an inverse problem; images often identify only combinations of parameters.

8. Geometric statistics on transformations and shapes

Self-study chapter. Averaging is the first step of any population study: a mean rotation of a bone, a mean shape of an organ, a mean deformation towards an atlas. None of these objects lives in a vector space, so the arithmetic mean is not defined, or is wrong. This chapter replaces it by the Fréchet mean on a manifold, following the framework of Pennec (2006), and previews the second-semester themes of computational anatomy.

8.1 Why the mean of rotations is not the mean of matrices

The arithmetic mean of two rotation matrices is generally not a rotation: in Example 5.2, the mean of a quarter turn and the identity has determinant 1/2. Projecting it back onto SO(3) gives a rotation, but one that depends on the embedding of SO(3) in the space of matrices, not on the geometry of rotations. The intrinsic answer uses distances measured on the group itself.

8.2 Riemannian manifolds, exponential and logarithm

A Riemannian manifold \mathcal{M} carries an inner product on each tangent space T_x \mathcal{M}, which defines the length of curves and the geodesic distance d(x, y), the length of the shortest curve.

Definition 8.1 — Exponential and logarithm maps For x \in \mathcal{M} and a tangent vector w \in T_x\mathcal{M}, \exp_x(w) is the point reached at time 1 by the geodesic leaving x with velocity w. Within the injectivity domain, its inverse is \log_x(y) \in T_x\mathcal{M}, \qquad |\log_x(y)| = d(x, y) .

On SO(3) with the metric for which the distance is the rotation angle, these maps are translated versions of the matrix exponential and logarithm of ch. 5.2: \exp_R(W) = R\, \exp(W), \qquad \log_R(S) = \log(R^\top S) .

The logarithm maps the manifold, locally, to a vector space where linear statistics can be computed; the exponential maps the results back.

8.3 The Fréchet mean

Definition 8.2 — Fréchet mean The Fréchet (or Karcher) mean of points x_1, \dots, x_n of \mathcal{M} is a minimiser of the variance \sigma^2(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n d(x, x_i)^2 . In a vector space with the Euclidean distance, it is the arithmetic mean.

Proposition 8.3 — Characterisation and fixed-point algorithm Inside the injectivity domain, the Riemannian gradient of \frac12 d(\cdot, y)^2 at x is -\log_x(y). Therefore a Fréchet mean \bar x satisfies \sum_{i=1}^n \log_{\bar x}(x_i) = 0, and the gradient descent with unit step reads x \leftarrow \exp_x\Big(\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \log_x(x_i)\Big) .

Proof. Sketch. By the Gauss lemma, moving x along a geodesic towards y decreases d(x, y) at unit rate, and moving orthogonally to it does not change d to first order.

Hence the gradient of d(\cdot, y) is the unit vector -\log_x(y) / |\log_x(y)|, and the chain rule gives the gradient of \frac12 d^2.

The mean cancels the gradient of \frac12\sigma^2, which is -\frac1n \sum_i \log_x(x_i). ∎

Karcher (1977) proved that the mean exists and is unique when the points lie in a sufficiently small geodesic ball, where the algorithm converges. Without this assumption, the mean need not be unique: on a circle, two diametrically opposite points have two means.

import numpy as np

def hat(w):
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]])

def exp_so3(w):                                   # Rodrigues' formula (Theorem 5.1)
    th = np.linalg.norm(w)
    if th < 1e-12:
        return np.eye(3)
    K = hat(w / th)
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(th) * K + (1 - np.cos(th)) * K @ K

def log_so3(R):                                   # inverse of exp for angles in [0, pi)
    th = np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R) - 1) / 2, -1, 1))
    if th < 1e-12:
        return np.zeros(3)
    W = (R - R.T) * th / (2 * np.sin(th))
    return np.array([W[2, 1], W[0, 2], W[1, 0]])

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
R_true = exp_so3(np.array([0.3, -0.5, 1.0]))
samples = [R_true @ exp_so3(0.6 * rng.standard_normal(3)) for _ in range(50)]  # noisy rotations

M = samples[0]                                    # Fréchet mean by fixed-point iteration (8.3)
for it in range(20):
    step = np.mean([log_so3(M.T @ R) for R in samples], axis=0)
    M = M @ exp_so3(step)
    if np.linalg.norm(step) < 1e-10:
        break
A = np.mean(samples, axis=0)                      # arithmetic mean of the matrices
U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
P = U @ np.diag([1, 1, np.linalg.det(U @ Vt)]) @ Vt   # its projection onto SO(3)
dist = lambda R1, R2: np.linalg.norm(log_so3(R1.T @ R2))
print(f"iterations {it + 1}; det of the arithmetic mean {np.linalg.det(A):.3f}")
print(f"distance to the true rotation: Frechet mean {dist(M, R_true):.4f} rad, "
      f"projected mean {dist(P, R_true):.4f} rad, between them {dist(M, P):.4f} rad")

The fixed-point iteration converges in 9 iterations. The arithmetic mean of the 50 matrices has determinant 0.39, far from a rotation. Projected back onto SO(3) it lands close to the Fréchet mean here (0.013 rad away), because the dispersion is moderate; the gap grows with the dispersion.

8.4 Statistical shape models

Cootes et al. (1995) represent each training shape by L corresponding landmarks, stacked into a vector s \in \mathbb{R}^{dL}. The point distribution model is built in three steps:

  1. align the shapes by generalised Procrustes analysis: alternately register every shape to the current mean with Theorem 5.3 (with a scale factor), then recompute the mean;
  2. compute the mean shape \bar s and the principal components p_k of the aligned shapes, with variances \lambda_k (ch. 0.2);
  3. model a shape with m modes as s = \bar s + \sum_{k=1}^m b_k\, p_k, \qquad |b_k| \leq 3\sqrt{\lambda_k} .

The active shape model segments a new image by alternating two steps: each landmark moves towards the strongest nearby edge along its normal, then the shape is projected back onto the model, with the coefficients b_k clipped to the bounds. Unlike the snakes of ch. 4, the regularisation is learned from the population.

Aligned shapes lie in a curved shape space (Kendall’s), so the PCA of step 2 is in fact a tangent PCA at the mean. Fletcher et al. (2004) replace it by principal geodesic analysis, which finds geodesic subspaces of maximal variance.

8.5 Diffusion tensors

Diffusion MRI estimates, at each voxel, a symmetric positive definite (SPD) 3 \times 3 matrix: the covariance of the displacement of water molecules. Interpolating, smoothing or averaging tensors needs a metric on SPD matrices.

Example 8.4 — The swelling effect Let A = \mathrm{diag}(4, 1) and B = \mathrm{diag}(1, 4): two tensors of determinant 4, elongated in orthogonal directions. Their Euclidean mean \mathrm{diag}(2.5, 2.5) has determinant 6.25 > 4, so averaging creates more diffusion than either tensor shows. The log-Euclidean mean \exp\Big(\tfrac12 (\log A + \log B)\Big) = \mathrm{diag}(2, 2) keeps the determinant 4, the geometric mean of the determinants.

Pennec, Fillard and Ayache (2006) use the affine-invariant metric, d(A, B) = \|\log(A^{-1/2} B A^{-1/2})\|, for which SPD matrices form a complete manifold of non-positive curvature: means are unique, and matrices with zero or negative eigenvalues sit at infinite distance. Arsigny et al. (2006) propose the log-Euclidean metric \|\log A - \log B\|, which gives the same type of results with closed-form means and simpler computations.

8.6 Statistics on deformations and atlases

The stationary velocity fields of ch. 6.4 play for diffeomorphisms the role of the logarithm: Arsigny et al. (2006) define log-Euclidean statistics on deformations by averaging velocities w_i with \varphi_i = \exp(w_i).

An unbiased atlas (Joshi et al., 2004) is a template image together with the deformations that map it to each subject, such that their mean is the identity. It is computed by alternating registration of all subjects to the template and update of the template. The deformations then become the data of population statistics: Jacobian determinants measure local volume changes (deformation-based morphometry), and longitudinal series of velocity fields model disease progression. These are the themes of computational anatomy that Geometry and Shape Spaces develops.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • On a manifold, replace the arithmetic mean by the Fréchet mean, characterised by \sum_i \log_{\bar x}(x_i) = 0 and computed by a fixed-point iteration.
  • Statistical shape models are a tangent PCA of Procrustes-aligned landmarks, used as a learned prior for segmentation.
  • Log-Euclidean and affine-invariant metrics avoid the swelling of Euclidean tensor averages.
  • Velocity fields linearise deformations, which makes atlases and statistics on deformations computable.

9. Research directions and open problems

Self-study chapter. The models of this course remain the backbone of medical image analysis, but learning has changed how they are fitted. This chapter surveys current directions, each with the papers to start from, and closes with open problems suitable for a project or an internship.

9.1 Learning registration

Classical registration optimises an energy for each pair of images, which takes minutes. Work by Balakrishnan and coauthors (2019) trains a network, VoxelMorph, to output the displacement field in one pass, by minimising the same energy (similarity plus regularisation) over a training set. Dalca et al. (2019) and Krebs et al. (2019) predict stationary velocity fields and integrate them by scaling and squaring (ch. 6.4), which keeps the output diffeomorphic. Hoffmann et al. (2022) train SynthMorph on synthetic images only, so that it registers contrasts never seen in training.

Open questions. A network amortises the optimisation, but nothing guarantees that its output is a minimiser for a new pair. How can one detect failures, calibrate the uncertainty of a predicted deformation, and keep accuracy across scanners?

9.2 Segmentation between models and networks

U-Net (Ronneberger, Fischer and Brox, 2015) and its self-configuring version nnU-Net (Isensee et al., 2021) are the reference segmentation methods when annotations exist; promptable foundation models such as SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023) and MedSAM (Ma et al., 2024) target the cases where they do not. The generative models of ch. 3 return in an unexpected way: SynthSeg (Billot et al., 2023) trains a network on images sampled from a Gaussian mixture conditioned on label maps, with random means, variances and bias fields, and segments brain MRI of any contrast and resolution.

Open questions. Networks give no guarantee of topology (a hole in a cortex, two components for one organ) or anatomical plausibility, which deformable and shape models enforce by construction. Combining the two, through shape priors, topological losses or registration-based segmentation, is active work.

9.3 Geometric statistics

The book edited by Pennec, Sommer and Fletcher (2020) collects the Riemannian tools of ch. 8 and their extensions. Statistics on manifolds with few samples, the bias of tangent approximations in curved spaces, and the choice of metric on diffeomorphisms remain open, as does the modelling of longitudinal shape changes in disease.

9.4 Biophysical models and digital twins

Personalised models of the heart and of tumours are now called digital twins (Corral-Acero et al., 2020; Niederer et al., 2021). Their bottlenecks are those of ch. 7.3: identifiability of the parameters from sparse, noisy images; the cost of each simulation; and validation against outcomes. Physics-informed neural networks (Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis, 2019) and learned surrogates are tried as fast approximations; Learning Physical Phenomena covers surrogates and uncertainty quantification.

9.5 Validation

Rankings of segmentation and registration challenges can change with the metric and the aggregation method (Maier-Hein et al., 2018), and recommendations for choosing metrics have been published (Maier-Hein et al., 2024). Reporting the uncertainty of a comparison, and validating on data from other centres, are part of the method.

9.6 Open problems

  • Registration with guaranteed diffeomorphism and calibrated uncertainty for a single forward pass.
  • Segmentation networks constrained by topology or by a statistical shape model, evaluated on rare anatomies.
  • Identifiability analysis of reaction–diffusion and cardiac models from the images actually acquired in the clinic.
  • Fréchet means and principal geodesic analysis on spaces of diffeomorphisms with few subjects.
  • Generative models of images conditioned on anatomy, used to train contrast-agnostic methods.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Learning amortises registration and segmentation, while the models of this course provide losses, regularisers and synthetic training data.
  • Guarantees (diffeomorphism, topology, uncertainty) and validation across centres are the main open issues.
  • Biophysical digital twins face identifiability, cost and validation problems.

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 2 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic device; four exercises, 20 points in total.

  1. Question (5 points). Course question. For CT, magnitude MRI and ultrasound, state the noise model of the reconstructed image. Why is ultrasound speckle called multiplicative, and what does it imply for filtering? Compute the mean-to-standard-deviation ratio of a Rayleigh variable.

    Expected: CT roughly Gaussian after filtered back-projection, with noise amplified by the ramp filter (ch. 1.2); Rician magnitude noise in MRI, Rayleigh in the background (ch. 1.3); Rayleigh speckle in ultrasound with a constant ratio \sqrt{\pi/(4 - \pi)} \approx 1.91, so filter its logarithm (Example 1.7).

  2. Question (5 points). Computation. Two tissue classes have intensities \mathcal{N}(100, 10^2) and \mathcal{N}(140, 10^2) and prior probabilities 0.8 and 0.2. Give the Bayes threshold and the error rate. Then write one EM update of the means from given responsibilities.

    Expected: by Proposition 3.1, t = 120 + 2.5 \log 4 \approx 123.5. The error is 0.8\, P(Z > 2.35) + 0.2\, P(Z > 1.65) \approx 0.8 \times 0.0094 + 0.2 \times 0.049 \approx 0.017 .

    The M-step means are responsibility-weighted averages (ch. 3.2).

  3. Question (5 points). Short proof. State and prove the closed-form solution of rigid point registration, assuming \det(UV^\top) = 1. Why can the solution be a reflection when the correction is omitted?

    Expected: Theorem 5.3 with its proof through Lemma 0.4: elimination of the translation, reduction to \max \mathrm{tr}(Q\Sigma), equality at Q = I. Without the correction, UV^\top can have determinant -1 for noisy or coplanar points (Exercise 11).

  4. Question (5 points). Registration. Derive the demons force as the minimiser of a regularised linearised residual and prove that its length is at most \sigma_x/2. Explain why mutual information, not SSD, is used to register T_1 with T_2 images. Give the Jacobian determinant of \varphi(x) = x + a \sin x and the values of a for which it folds.

    Expected: Proposition 6.3 and its proof; MI assumes only statistical dependence and is invariant to one-to-one intensity remappings (Proposition 5.7), whereas SSD assumes identical intensities; \varphi'(x) = 1 + a\cos x, folding for |a| > 1 (Example 6.2).
