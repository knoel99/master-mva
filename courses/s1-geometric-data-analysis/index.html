Audience: a student with an M1 in mathematics, computer science or physics who has trained a few models and wants to know which representation, distance and loss to use for vectors, graphs, surfaces and probability distributions.

Empirical first: in dimension 1000, the farthest of 1000 uniform points is only 11 % farther from a query than the nearest one (Code 1.4). Moreover, a gradient flow driven by a Gaussian kernel norm with a narrow kernel leaves every point stranded, while the same flow driven by optimal transport brings all of them to the target (Code 6.16). Both facts are geometric, and this course explains them.

The course gives one geometric viewpoint on common machine learning models. Data are vectors, points on a curved space, nodes of a graph or samples of a distribution; each choice comes with its own distances, convolutions and losses, and with its own computational cost. The course is a hub for the Santé track and for the geometry-oriented courses: Optimal Transport for Machine Learning, Geometry Processing and Geometric Deep Learning, Geometry and Shape Spaces and Graphs in Machine Learning.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the curse of dimensionality with exact computations, and say when a nearest-neighbour or kernel method can still work.
  • Encode an invariance in a distance, and compute shape distances on Kendall’s sphere of triangles.
  • Compare decision trees, nearest neighbours, linear models, neural networks and kernel methods by the geometry they impose on the feature space.
  • Choose between a squared loss and a cross-entropy, and justify the choice by gradients and by the geometry of the simplex.
  • Estimate the intrinsic dimension of a data set, measure curvature, and build graph-based embeddings (Isomap, Laplacian eigenmaps, t-SNE, UMAP).
  • Write a graph convolution and a rotation-equivariant layer from scratch, and prove their equivariance.
  • Compute distances, geodesics and Fréchet means on the sphere and in the Poincaré disk, and measure the error of graph shortest paths.
  • Compare probability distributions with divergences, the Fisher–Rao metric, kernel norms and optimal transport, and implement the Sinkhorn algorithm.
  • Relate kernel matrices, graphs, heat kernels, entropic transport plans and attention matrices to one another.
  • Predict whether a computation is memory-bound or compute-bound, and write a tiled reduction that never stores an M \times N matrix.

0. Review: linear algebra, probability and numerics

Every chapter compares data points, and every comparison is computed from inner products, eigenvalues or logarithms of sums of exponentials. This chapter states these M1 tools in the form used later, with short proofs.

0.1 Inner products and the Gram trick

Definition 0.1 — Inner product, norm, distance An inner product on \mathbb{R}^d is a symmetric bilinear form with \langle x, x \rangle > 0 for x \neq 0. It defines the norm \|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle} and the distance d(x, y) = \|x - y\|.

Every inner product reads \langle x, y \rangle_M = x^\top M y for a symmetric positive definite matrix M. Choosing M means choosing which directions of the data matter.

Proposition 0.2 — The Gram trick For x_1, \dots, x_M and y_1, \dots, y_N in \mathbb{R}^d, the matrix of squared distances is \big( \|x_i - y_j\|^2 \big)_{ij} = \big( \|x_i\|^2 \big)_i \mathbf{1}^\top + \mathbf{1} \big( \|y_j\|^2 \big)_j^\top - 2\, X Y^\top , where X and Y stack the points as rows.

Proof. Expand \|x - y\|^2 = \langle x - y, x - y \rangle by bilinearity and symmetry. ∎

All pairwise distances therefore cost one matrix product, the fastest operation on modern hardware (ch. 7).

0.2 Spectral theorem and positive matrices

Theorem 0.3 — Spectral theorem and singular value decomposition A real symmetric matrix S factors as S = U \Lambda U^\top with U orthogonal and \Lambda real diagonal.

Any real matrix factors as A = U \Sigma V^\top with U, V orthogonal and \Sigma \geq 0 diagonal.

Proof. See the linear algebra refresher; the second statement follows from the first applied to A^\top A. ∎

Definition 0.4 — Positive semi-definite matrix A symmetric matrix K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} is positive semi-definite (PSD) if c^\top K c \geq 0 for all c \in \mathbb{R}^n, that is, if its eigenvalues are non-negative.

Proposition 0.5 — PSD matrices are Gram matrices K is PSD if and only if there are vectors z_1, \dots, z_n with K_{ij} = \langle z_i, z_j \rangle .

Proof. If K_{ij} = \langle z_i, z_j \rangle, then c^\top K c = \| \sum_i c_i z_i \|^2 \geq 0.

Conversely, write K = U \Lambda U^\top with \Lambda \geq 0, and take for z_i the i-th row of U \Lambda^{1/2}. ∎

A PSD similarity matrix is therefore an inner product between hidden feature vectors (ch. 2 and 3).

0.3 Gaussian vectors

Definition 0.6 — Standard Gaussian vector X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) has independent \mathcal{N}(0, 1) coordinates and density p(x) = (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\big( -\|x\|^2 / 2 \big) . Its law is invariant under every orthogonal transformation.

Proposition 0.7 — Moments of the squared norm If X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d), then \mathbb{E} \|X\|^2 = d, \qquad \operatorname{Var} \|X\|^2 = 2d .

Proof. \|X\|^2 = \sum_k X_k^2 is a sum of d independent terms with \mathbb{E} X_k^2 = 1 and \mathbb{E} X_k^4 = 3, so each term has variance 3 - 1 = 2. ∎

0.4 Softmax, cross-entropy and the log-sum-exp

Definition 0.8 — Softmax, cross-entropy, Kullback–Leibler divergence For scores s \in \mathbb{R}^n and distributions p, q on \{1, \dots, n\}: \operatorname{softmax}(s)_i = \frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}, \qquad H(q, p) = -\sum_i q_i \log p_i, \qquad \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = \sum_i q_i \log \frac{q_i}{p_i} . The cross-entropy decomposes as H(q, p) = H(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p), where H(q) = H(q, q) is the entropy.

Proposition 0.9 — Gibbs inequality and stable log-sum-exp (a) \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) \geq 0, with equality if and only if p = q.

  1. For every s \in \mathbb{R}^n and m = \max_j s_j, \log \sum_j e^{s_j} = m + \log \sum_j e^{s_j - m} , and every exponential on the right lies in (0, 1].

Proof.

  1. By Jensen’s inequality for the concave logarithm, -\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = \sum_{i : q_i > 0} q_i \log \frac{p_i}{q_i} \leq \log \sum_{i : q_i > 0} p_i \leq 0 . Equality in both steps forces p_i / q_i to be constant on the support of q, and p to put no mass outside it: p = q.

  2. Factor e^{m} out of the sum. ∎

Code 0.10 — Distances by the Gram trick and a stable log-sum-exp

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X, Y = rng.normal(size=(5, 3)), rng.normal(size=(4, 3))
# |x - y|^2 = |x|^2 + |y|^2 - 2 <x, y>: all distances from one matrix product (Proposition 0.2)
D2 = (X**2).sum(1)[:, None] + (Y**2).sum(1)[None, :] - 2 * X @ Y.T
print("Gram trick error:", np.abs(D2 - ((X[:, None] - Y[None]) ** 2).sum(-1)).max())

def logsumexp(s):
    """log sum_j exp(s_j), computed without overflow (Proposition 0.9)."""
    m = s.max()
    return m + np.log(np.exp(s - m).sum())

s = np.array([1000.0, 999.0, 990.0])
with np.errstate(over="ignore"):
    print("naive:", np.log(np.exp(s).sum()), "  stable:", round(logsumexp(s), 6))
p = np.exp(s - logsumexp(s))                       # softmax
q = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
print("softmax:", np.round(p, 4), "  KL(q || p) =", round(float(np.sum(q * np.log(q / p))), 4))

The Gram trick agrees with the direct computation to 10^{-15}; the naive log-sum-exp overflows, the stable one returns 1000.3133. The softmax gives the third score a mass 3.3 \times 10^{-5} where q puts 20 %, hence \mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = 1.58.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • An inner product is a choice of geometry; pairwise distances cost one matrix product.
  • PSD matrices are Gram matrices; \|X\|^2 = d \pm \sqrt{2d} for a standard Gaussian.
  • Softmax, cross-entropy and KL go through the log-sum-exp, shifted by its maximum.

1. Why geometry?

Distances between raw vectors stop being informative in high dimension, yet real data are learnable. This chapter measures the problem, then shows how a well-chosen geometry, one that encodes the invariances of the data, solves it.

1.1 The curse of dimensionality

Proposition 1.1 — Volume lives near the boundary In \mathbb{R}^d, the fraction of the volume of the unit ball lying within \varepsilon of its boundary is 1 - (1 - \varepsilon)^d \geq 1 - e^{-\varepsilon d} .

Proof. The volume of the ball of radius r is c_d\, r^d, so the inner ball of radius 1 - \varepsilon holds a fraction (1 - \varepsilon)^d \leq e^{-\varepsilon d}. ∎

For d = 100 and \varepsilon = 0.05, the shell holds 99.4 % of the volume.

Proposition 1.2 — Covering numbers Covering the cube [0, 1]^d with cubes of side 2\varepsilon requires at least N(\varepsilon) \geq (2 \varepsilon)^{-d} cubes.

Proof. Each small cube has volume (2\varepsilon)^d, and their union must have volume at least 1. ∎

Local methods (k-NN, kernels of width \varepsilon) average labels over such cells: with \varepsilon = 0.1 in dimension 10, there are 5^{10} \approx 10^7 cells to fill.

Proposition 1.3 — Concentration of distances Let X and Y be independent and uniform on [0, 1]^d. Then \mathbb{E} \|X - Y\|^2 = \frac{d}{6}, \qquad \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var} \|X - Y\|^2}}{\mathbb{E} \|X - Y\|^2} = \sqrt{\frac{1.4}{d}} .

Proof. For one coordinate, T = U - V has the triangular density 1 - |t| on [-1, 1]. Hence \mathbb{E} T^2 = 2 \int_0^1 t^2 (1 - t)\, dt = \frac16, \qquad \mathbb{E} T^4 = 2 \int_0^1 t^4 (1 - t)\, dt = \frac{1}{15} .

So \operatorname{Var} T^2 = 1/15 - 1/36 = 7/180. The d coordinates are independent, so the mean and variance are multiplied by d, and the ratio is \sqrt{7d/180} \cdot 6/d = \sqrt{1.4/d}. ∎

By Chebyshev’s inequality, most squared distances from a query then lie within a few multiples of \sqrt{1.4/d} of their mean: a few percent for d = 1000.

Code 1.4 — Relative contrast of distances For 1000 uniform points and a uniform query, the code prints (\max - \min) / \min of the distances to the query.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 1000                                   # points per data set
for d in [2, 10, 100, 1000]:
    X, q = rng.random((n, d)), rng.random(d)   # uniform cloud and a query
    dist = np.linalg.norm(X - q, axis=1)
    contrast = (dist.max() - dist.min()) / dist.min()
    print(f"d = {d:4d}:  relative contrast = {contrast:6.2f},  "
          f"mean squared distance / d = {np.mean(dist**2) / d:.3f}")

The contrast falls from 134.6 (d = 2) to 3.48, 0.39 and 0.11 (d = 1000), and the mean squared distance over d tends to 1/6.

Real data escape this curse: they concentrate near low-dimensional sets (ch. 3), and their labels are invariant under known transformations, which a distance can quotient out.

1.2 Geometry as a modelling tool

Definition 1.5 — Invariant distance and quotient distance Let a group G act on a metric space (\mathcal{X}, d). The distance is G-invariant if d(g \cdot x, g \cdot y) = d(x, y) for all g, x, y. The quotient distance between orbits is \bar d([x], [y]) = \inf_{g \in G} d(x, g \cdot y) .

Proposition 1.6 — The quotient distance is a pseudo-distance If d is G-invariant, then \bar d does not depend on the chosen representatives, and it is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality.

Proof. Representatives: by invariance, d(h \cdot x, g k \cdot y) = d(x, h^{-1} g k \cdot y) , and h^{-1} g k runs over G when g does.

Symmetry: d(x, g \cdot y) = d(g^{-1} \cdot x, y).

Triangle inequality: for all g, h, d(x, g \cdot z) \leq d(x, h \cdot y) + d(y, h^{-1} g \cdot z) . Take the infimum over g, then over h. ∎

Translations of images (registration), rotations of molecules (alignment before a root-mean-square deviation), relabellings of the points of a cloud (ch. 6) and reparametrizations of curves are all quotiented this way.

1.3 The sphere of triangles

A triangle is a vector z = (z_1, z_2, z_3) \in \mathbb{C}^3 of vertices, and its shape is what remains after translations, rotations and scalings z \mapsto a z + b, with a \in \mathbb{C}^* and b \in \mathbb{C}. The Helmert coordinates u = \frac{z_2 - z_1}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad v = \frac{2 z_3 - z_1 - z_2}{\sqrt 6} remove translations isometrically. Scaling is removed by imposing |u|^2 + |v|^2 = 1, and rotations act as (u, v) \mapsto e^{i\theta} (u, v).

Theorem 1.7 — Kendall’s sphere of triangles The map h(u, v) = \big( |u|^2 - |v|^2,\ 2 \operatorname{Re}(u \bar v),\ 2 \operatorname{Im}(u \bar v) \big) identifies the shapes of triangles with the unit sphere S^2. The Procrustes distance \rho = \arccos |\langle (u, v), (u', v') \rangle| between two shapes satisfies \angle\big( h(u, v), h(u', v') \big) = 2 \rho , so the shape space is a sphere of radius 1/2. Equilateral triangles of either orientation map to the two poles, and collinear triangles to the equator.

Proof.

  1. Invariance and image: h is unchanged by a common phase e^{i\theta}, and \|h\|^2 = (|u|^2 - |v|^2)^2 + 4 |u|^2 |v|^2 = (|u|^2 + |v|^2)^2 = 1 .
  2. Fibres: h gives |u|^2, |v|^2 and u \bar v, which determine (u, v) up to a common phase. Every point of S^2 is reached.
  3. Distances: a direct expansion gives, for unit vectors a, b \in \mathbb{C}^2, h(a) \cdot h(b) = 2\, |\langle a, b \rangle|^2 - 1 = \cos(2\rho) .
  4. Poles and equator: the third coordinate equals -4\sqrt3\, A / (a^2 + b^2 + c^2), with A the signed area and a, b, c the side lengths. It is \pm 1 exactly for equilateral triangles (Weitzenböck’s inequality) and 0 for collinear ones. ∎

If the three vertices are independent standard complex Gaussians, (u, v) is a standard Gaussian vector of \mathbb{C}^2. Its normalization is uniform on S^3, and the map h pushes it to the uniform law on S^2. By Archimedes’ theorem, the third coordinate is then uniform on [-1, 1].

Code 1.8 — Triangles on the sphere The code maps triangles to S^2, checks the factor 2 of Theorem 1.7, and samples Gaussian triangles.

import numpy as np

def shape_point(z):
    """Point of the unit sphere S^2 representing the triangles z (rows of 3 complex vertices)."""
    u = (z[:, 1] - z[:, 0]) / np.sqrt(2)              # Helmert coordinates: translations removed
    v = (2 * z[:, 2] - z[:, 0] - z[:, 1]) / np.sqrt(6)
    r2 = abs(u)**2 + abs(v)**2                          # scale removed by dividing by r2
    w = u * np.conj(v)                                  # rotations z -> e^{it} z leave w unchanged
    return np.c_[(abs(u)**2 - abs(v)**2), 2 * w.real, 2 * w.imag] / r2[:, None]

def kendall(z1, z2):
    """Procrustes distance rho = arccos |<z1, z2>| between centred, normalized triangles."""
    a = np.c_[(z1[1] - z1[0]) / np.sqrt(2), (2 * z1[2] - z1[0] - z1[1]) / np.sqrt(6)].ravel()
    b = np.c_[(z2[1] - z2[0]) / np.sqrt(2), (2 * z2[2] - z2[0] - z2[1]) / np.sqrt(6)].ravel()
    c = abs(np.vdot(a, b)) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
    return np.arccos(min(c, 1.0))

equi = np.array([0, 1, np.exp(1j * np.pi / 3)])
flat = np.array([0, 1, 3 + 0j])
right = np.array([0, 1, 1j])
moved = 2.5 * np.exp(0.8j) * right + (4 - 1j)            # a similar copy of `right`
P = shape_point(np.vstack([equi, np.conj(equi), flat, right, moved]))
print("sphere points (equilateral, mirror, collinear, right, moved right):")
print(np.round(P, 4))
ang = np.arccos(np.clip(P[0] @ P[3], -1, 1))
print(f"angle on S^2 = {ang:.4f},  2 * Kendall distance = {2 * kendall(equi, right):.4f}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                            # Gaussian random triangles
n = 200_000
Z = rng.normal(size=(n, 3)) + 1j * rng.normal(size=(n, 3))
h3 = shape_point(Z)[:, 2]
print("quartiles of the third coordinate:", np.round(np.quantile(h3, [0.25, 0.5, 0.75]), 3))
s = np.sort(np.c_[abs(Z[:, 0] - Z[:, 1]), abs(Z[:, 1] - Z[:, 2]), abs(Z[:, 2] - Z[:, 0])]**2, axis=1)
print(f"fraction of obtuse triangles = {np.mean(s[:, 2] > s[:, 0] + s[:, 1]):.4f}")

The equilateral triangle and its mirror land on the poles, the collinear triangle on the equator, and the right triangle and its moved copy on the same point. The angle 0.5236 is twice the Kendall distance \pi / 12.

The quartiles of the third coordinate are close to -0.5, 0, 0.5, and 74.96 % of Gaussian triangles are obtuse, close to the exact value 3/4.

1.4 Deep Art

Style transfer (Gatys, Ecker and Bethge, 2016) optimizes an image x so that its deep features resemble those of a content image c at one layer, and those of a style image s at several layers, through Gram matrices.

Definition 1.9 — Gram matrix of features and style loss Let F^l(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{C_l \times N_l} hold the C_l channels of layer l of a fixed network at N_l positions, with columns f_1, \dots, f_{N_l}. Its Gram matrix and the transfer loss are G^l(x) = \frac{1}{N_l} F^l(x) F^l(x)^\top, \qquad \mathcal{L}(x) = \|F^L(x) - F^L(c)\|^2 + \lambda \sum_l w_l\, \|G^l(x) - G^l(s)\|_F^2 .

Proposition 1.10 — Style is a distribution of features (a) G^l is invariant under every permutation of the N_l positions.

  1. Let \mu_a = \frac1N \sum_i \delta_{f_i} and \mu_b = \frac1M \sum_j \delta_{g_j} be the empirical feature distributions of two images. With k(f, g) = \langle f, g \rangle^2, \|G_a - G_b\|_F^2 = \iint k(f, g)\, d(\mu_a - \mu_b)(f)\, d(\mu_a - \mu_b)(g) .

Proof.

  1. F F^\top = \sum_i f_i f_i^\top does not depend on the order of the columns.

  2. The Frobenius inner product of two Gram matrices is \langle G_a, G_b \rangle_F = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{i, j} \operatorname{tr}\big( f_i f_i^\top g_j g_j^\top \big) = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{i, j} \langle f_i, g_j \rangle^2 . Expand \|G_a - G_b\|_F^2 into three such terms. ∎

Content is compared as a vector and style as a histogram of features. The right-hand side of (b) is a kernel norm between distributions (ch. 6); Kolkin, Salavon and Shakhnarovich (2019) replaced it by optimal transport.

Code 1.11 — Pixel distance versus style distance A striped texture is compared with a shifted copy, a copy with shuffled 16 \times 16 tiles, and stripes of another orientation. Features come from 16 random filters and a ReLU, so nothing is downloaded.

import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
n = 64
y, x = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(n), torch.arange(n), indexing="ij")

def texture(theta):
    """Stripes of orientation theta plus a little noise: a 1 x 1 x n x n image."""
    img = torch.sin(0.6 * (x * torch.cos(theta) + y * torch.sin(theta))) + 0.3 * torch.randn(n, n)
    return img[None, None].float()

conv = torch.nn.Conv2d(1, 16, 5, padding=2, padding_mode="circular", bias=False)

def features(img):                       # 16 random filters + ReLU, flattened: 16 x n^2
    with torch.no_grad():
        return torch.relu(conv(img))[0].reshape(16, -1)

def gram(F):                             # second moments of the feature distribution
    return F @ F.T / F.shape[1]

a = texture(torch.tensor(0.4))
b = torch.roll(a, shifts=(17, 29), dims=(2, 3))                       # same texture, shifted
tiles = a.reshape(1, 1, 4, 16, 4, 16).permute(0, 1, 2, 4, 3, 5).reshape(1, 1, 16, 16, 16)
c = tiles[:, :, torch.randperm(16)].reshape(1, 1, 4, 4, 16, 16).permute(0, 1, 2, 4, 3, 5).reshape(1, 1, n, n)
d = texture(torch.tensor(1.6))                                        # other orientation

Ga = gram(features(a))
for name, img in [("shifted", b), ("tiles shuffled", c), ("other texture", d)]:
    pix = (img - a).norm() / a.norm()
    sty = (gram(features(img)) - Ga).norm() / Ga.norm()
    print(f"{name:15s}: pixel distance {pix:.3f}   Gram (style) distance {sty:.3f}")

In pixels, the three images are equally far from the original (1.44, 1.43, 1.41). The style distance separates them: 0.000 for the shift, 0.098 for the shuffled tiles (tile borders change a few features), 0.606 for the other orientation.

2. Flat vector spaces

The classical model families all work in \mathbb{R}^d, but each one imposes its own geometry. This chapter asks two questions of each model: which transformations of the features leave it unchanged, and how it decides that two points are close.

2.1 Decision trees

Definition 2.1 — Decision tree, CART A decision tree partitions \mathbb{R}^d into axis-aligned boxes by recursive tests x_k \leq t, and predicts the majority label (or the mean) of each leaf. CART chooses each split greedily, to minimize the weighted impurity of the two children, for instance the Gini impurity G(p) = 1 - \sum_c p_c^2 .

For a node with 5 samples of each class, G = 0.5. A split into children (4, 1) and (1, 4) gives G = 1 - 0.64 - 0.04 = 0.32 in each child, a gain of 0.18.

Proposition 2.2 — Trees ignore monotone changes of units If each feature is transformed by a strictly increasing map \varphi_k, CART builds the same partition of the training samples, with transformed thresholds. Rotations of the feature space, in contrast, change the tree.

Proof. The test x_k \leq t selects the same samples as \varphi_k(x_k) \leq \varphi_k(t). Impurities depend only on the induced partition of the samples, so the same splits are available with the same costs at every node.

For rotations, a boundary x_1 = x_2 is a single oblique line, but an axis-aligned tree can only approximate it by a staircase of many splits. ∎

2.2 Nearest neighbours

Definition 2.3 — k-nearest-neighbour rule Given training pairs (x_i, y_i) and a distance d, the k-NN rule predicts at a new point the majority label among the k training points closest to it for d.

Theorem 2.4 — Cover and Hart, 1967 For binary classification with \eta(x) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid X = x) continuous, the risk of the 1-NN rule tends, as n \to \infty, to R_1 = \mathbb{E}\big[ 2 \eta(X) (1 - \eta(X)) \big] \leq 2 R^\star (1 - R^\star), where R^\star = \mathbb{E} \min(\eta(X), 1 - \eta(X)) is the Bayes risk.

A proof sketch is in ch. 8.

At finite n, however, the distance decides which points are neighbours, and the sample size needed grows like \varepsilon^{-d} (Proposition 1.2). The rule is invariant under isometries of d, not under changes of units.

2.3 Linear models and losses

Definition 2.5 — Linear and logistic models A linear model scores f(x) = \langle w, x \rangle + b. Logistic regression predicts \mathbb{P}(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(f(x)), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} , and its multiclass version predicts \operatorname{softmax}(W x + b).

An invertible affine change of features, x \mapsto A x + c, maps the set of linear models onto itself through w \mapsto A^{-\top} w. Only the regularization \|w\|^2 sees the geometry of the features.

Definition 2.6 — Squared loss and cross-entropy For a predicted distribution p on the classes and a label y, with e_y the one-hot vector: \ell_2(p, y) = \|p - e_y\|^2, \qquad \ell_{\mathrm{CE}}(p, y) = -\log p_y = \mathrm{KL}(e_y \,\|\, p) .

Proposition 2.7 — Gradients of the two losses For a binary label y \in \{0, 1\} and p = \sigma(z), the cross-entropy and the binary squared loss (p - y)^2, which is half of \ell_2 for two classes, have the gradients \frac{\partial \ell_{\mathrm{CE}}}{\partial z} = p - y, \qquad \frac{\partial (p - y)^2}{\partial z} = 2 (p - y)\, p (1 - p) . The cross-entropy is convex in z; the squared loss is not, and its gradient vanishes when p saturates at the wrong value.

Proof. Use \sigma' = \sigma (1 - \sigma). For y = 1, the derivative of -\log \sigma(z) is -(1 - \sigma) = p - 1.

For y = 0, the derivative of -\log(1 - \sigma(z)) is \sigma = p.

The second derivative of the cross-entropy is p (1 - p) > 0. The squared loss gradient carries the factor p(1 - p), which tends to 0 when z \to \pm \infty. ∎

Example 2.8 — A confident mistake Take z = -5 and y = 1, so p = \sigma(-5) = 0.0067. The cross-entropy gradient is -0.9933. The squared-loss gradient is 2 \times (-0.9933) \times 0.0067 \times 0.9933 = -0.0132 , 75 times smaller: the squared loss barely corrects a confident mistake.

Geometrically, \ell_2 measures the simplex with the Euclidean metric, while \ell_{\mathrm{CE}} is a Kullback–Leibler divergence, which blows up near the faces of the simplex: confident errors cost a lot. For regression with Gaussian noise, the squared loss is the right choice, being the negative log-likelihood.

2.4 Neural networks

Proposition 2.9 — ReLU networks are piecewise affine A network that alternates affine maps and \operatorname{ReLU}(t) = \max(0, t) is continuous, and \mathbb{R}^d splits into finitely many polyhedra on each of which it is affine.

Proof. By induction on the layers. An affine map of a piecewise affine function is piecewise affine. Applying the ReLU coordinatewise refines each polyhedron by the hyperplane where the pre-activation changes sign. ∎

A ReLU network thus learns its own partition, like a tree with oblique cells. Universal approximation is the limit of this picture: Cybenko (1989) proved it for sigmoids, and Leshno, Lin, Pinkus and Schocken (1993) for every non-polynomial activation, the ReLU included. On raw vectors it treats coordinates as unrelated, and structure enters through weight sharing.

Proposition 2.10 — Translation-equivariant linear maps are convolutions Let L : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n act on signals indexed by \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} and commute with every translation (\tau_s x)_i = x_{i - s}. With h = L e_0, (L x)_i = \sum_j h_{i - j}\, x_j .

Proof. Write x = \sum_j x_j e_j = \sum_j x_j \tau_j e_0. By linearity and equivariance, L x = \sum_j x_j\, \tau_j L e_0 = \sum_j x_j\, \tau_j h . Read the i-th coordinate. ∎

Convolutions are therefore exactly the linear layers that respect translations. Replacing translations by permutations of graph nodes or by rotations of space gives the layers of ch. 4.

2.5 Kernel methods

Definition 2.11 — Positive definite kernel and RKHS A symmetric function k : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R} is a positive definite kernel if every matrix (k(x_i, x_j))_{ij} is PSD. Its reproducing kernel Hilbert space \mathcal{H}_k is the completion of the span of the functions k(\cdot, x) for the inner product \langle k(\cdot, x), k(\cdot, y) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = k(x, y) , so that f(x) = \langle f, k(\cdot, x) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}. The Gaussian kernel is k(x, y) = \exp(-\|x - y\|^2 / 2\sigma^2).

By Proposition 0.5, a kernel is an inner product between feature vectors \varphi(x) = k(\cdot, x). A kernel method is a linear method in the geometry of that feature space, where \|\varphi(x) - \varphi(y)\|^2 = k(x, x) + k(y, y) - 2 k(x, y) .

Theorem 2.12 — Representer theorem For any loss L and \lambda > 0, a minimizer over \mathcal{H}_k of \sum_{i = 1}^n L\big( y_i, f(x_i) \big) + \lambda \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 can be sought as f = \sum_i \alpha_i k(\cdot, x_i). For the squared loss, \alpha = (K + \lambda I)^{-1} y with K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j).

Proof. Split f = f_\parallel + f_\perp, with f_\parallel in the span of the k(\cdot, x_i). Then f_\perp(x_i) = \langle f_\perp, k(\cdot, x_i) \rangle = 0, so the data term only depends on f_\parallel, while \|f\|^2 = \|f_\parallel\|^2 + \|f_\perp\|^2 . A minimizer therefore has f_\perp = 0.

For the squared loss, the objective \|y - K \alpha\|^2 + \lambda\, \alpha^\top K \alpha has gradient 2 K \big( (K + \lambda I) \alpha - y \big), which vanishes at the stated \alpha. ∎

The width \sigma sets the geometry. When \sigma \to 0, the Gaussian kernel only sees the nearest points, as k-NN does; when \sigma is large, it behaves like a low-degree polynomial. A kernel matrix is also a weighted neighbourhood graph, the starting point of ch. 3.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Trees ignore changes of units but not rotations; k-NN and Gaussian kernels the reverse.
  • The cross-entropy gradient p - y never saturates; the squared loss gradient carries p(1 - p).
  • ReLU networks are piecewise affine; convolutions are the translation-equivariant linear maps.
  • Kernel methods are linear in a feature space: \alpha = (K + \lambda I)^{-1} y.

3. Graphs and embeddings

High-dimensional data are not spread uniformly: they sit near thin sets with a small intrinsic dimension and sometimes a marked curvature. This chapter measures these properties, then builds graphs that capture them and embeddings that display them.

3.1 Normal distributions are… bubbles!

Proposition 3.1 — Gaussian bubbles Let X, Y be independent \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) vectors.

  1. For every \delta > 0, \mathbb{P}\Big( \Big| \frac{\|X\|^2}{d} - 1 \Big| \geq \delta \Big) \leq \frac{2}{d\, \delta^2} .

  2. \mathbb{E} \langle X, Y \rangle = 0 and \operatorname{Var} \langle X, Y \rangle = d, so the cosine of their angle is of order 1/\sqrt d.

  3. The midpoint (X + Y)/2 is \mathcal{N}(0, I_d / 2), with norm close to \sqrt{d/2}.

Proof.

  1. is Chebyshev’s inequality with Proposition 0.7. (b) \langle X, Y \rangle = \sum_k X_k Y_k is a sum of d independent centred terms of variance 1. (c) A sum of independent Gaussian vectors is Gaussian, with the sum of the covariances. ∎

The density is largest at the origin, yet almost no sample lies near it: the mass sits on a thin shell of radius \sqrt d, a bubble. Two samples are almost orthogonal, and their straight-line midpoint is atypical. In the latent space of a generative model, one interpolates along great circles of the shell rather than along chords.

Code 3.2 — Measuring the bubble in dimension 512

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d = 512                                            # e.g. the latent space of a generative model
X, Y = rng.normal(size=(2, 10_000, d))

print(f"|X|: mean {nx.mean():.2f}, std {nx.std():.3f}   (sqrt(d) = {np.sqrt(d):.2f})")

The norms are 22.61 \pm 0.70 for \sqrt{512} = 22.63, and the cosines have standard deviation 0.0445, close to 1/\sqrt{512} = 0.0442. The midpoints have norm 15.98, 9.4 standard deviations inside the shell.

3.2 The manifold hypothesis

Definition 3.3 — Manifold hypothesis Data in \mathbb{R}^D satisfy the manifold hypothesis if they concentrate near a smooth submanifold \mathcal{M} of dimension m \ll D. Near each of its points, \mathcal{M} is the graph of a smooth function of m coordinates.

The images of one object under rotations and lighting changes form such a set, with m equal to the number of degrees of freedom. On \mathcal{M}, the relevant distance follows the manifold rather than the chord, and the covering argument of Proposition 1.2 costs \varepsilon^{-m} instead of \varepsilon^{-D}. Fefferman, Mitter and Narayanan (2016) give a statistical test of the hypothesis.

3.3 Dimension

Definition 3.4 — Correlation dimension Let C(r) be the fraction of pairs of samples at distance less than r. If C(r) \propto r^m over a range of scales, m is the correlation dimension (Grassberger and Procaccia, 1983).

The range matters: below the noise level, data look D-dimensional. Nearest-neighbour estimators avoid choosing it.

Proposition 3.5 — The TwoNN estimator Assume the samples form, near each point, a Poisson process of constant density on an m-dimensional manifold. Let r_1 < r_2 be the distances from a sample to its two nearest neighbours, and \mu = r_2 / r_1. Then \mathbb{P}(\mu > x) = x^{-m} \quad (x \geq 1), so \log \mu is exponential with rate m, and the maximum likelihood estimator from N samples is \hat m = \frac{N}{\sum_{i = 1}^N \log \mu_i} .

Proof. Given r_2, the nearest neighbour of a Poisson process is uniform in the m-dimensional ball of radius r_2, so \mathbb{P}(r_1 \leq s \mid r_2) = (s / r_2)^m. Hence \mathbb{P}(\mu > x) = \mathbb{P}(r_1 < r_2 / x) = x^{-m}.

The log-likelihood of N independent exponential variables is N \log m - m \sum_i \log \mu_i, maximal at \hat m. ∎

This estimator is due to Facco, d’Errico, Rodriguez and Laio (2017). It is invariant under isometries and under scaling, and it needs no choice of radius.

3.4 Curvature

Definition 3.6 — Gaussian curvature by circles On a surface, let C(r) be the perimeter of the geodesic circle of radius r around a point. The Gaussian curvature K at that point is defined by C(r) = 2 \pi r \Big( 1 - \frac{K r^2}{6} + o(r^2) \Big) .

On a sphere of radius R, C(r) = 2 \pi R \sin(r / R), hence K = 1 / R^2. In the hyperbolic plane of ch. 5, C(r) = 2 \pi \sinh r, hence K = -1. Positive curvature appears with normalized embeddings and directions; negative curvature appears with hierarchies, whose number of descendants grows exponentially with depth.

Proposition 3.7 — Angle defects add up to topology On a closed polyhedral surface with V vertices, E edges and F faces, let the angle defect at v be \delta_v = 2\pi - \sum (\text{angles of the faces at } v). Then \sum_v \delta_v = 2 \pi (V - E + F) .

Proof in ch. 8.

For a cube, each of the 8 vertices has defect \pi/2, for a total of 4\pi = 2\pi \times 2. The angle defect is the discrete Gaussian curvature of a mesh.

3.5 Neighbourhood graphs and embeddings

Definition 3.8 — Neighbourhood graph and Laplacian On samples x_1, \dots, x_n, a k-NN graph (or \varepsilon-graph) carries weights w_{ij} = \exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma^2) on its edges. With the degree matrix D = \operatorname{diag}(\sum_j w_{ij}), the graph Laplacian is L = D - W.

Proposition 3.9 — Laplacian eigenmaps (a) For every f \in \mathbb{R}^n, f^\top L f = \frac12 \sum_{i, j} w_{ij} (f_i - f_j)^2 \geq 0 .

  1. Let the graph be connected. Among Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} with Y^\top D Y = I and Y^\top D \mathbf{1} = 0, the trace \operatorname{tr}(Y^\top L Y) is minimized by the generalized eigenvectors L v = \lambda D v of the p smallest non-zero eigenvalues (Belkin and Niyogi, 2003).

Proof in ch. 8.

Close points get close coordinates, because (f_i - f_j)^2 is paid with weight w_{ij}.

Proposition 3.10 — Classical multidimensional scaling Let x_1, \dots, x_n be centred points of \mathbb{R}^p, \Delta_{ij} = \|x_i - x_j\|^2, and J = I - \frac1n \mathbf{1} \mathbf{1}^\top. Then B = -\tfrac12\, J \Delta J = X X^\top , so the eigenvectors of the p largest eigenvalues of B, scaled by their square roots, recover the points up to an orthogonal map.

Proof in ch. 8.

Isomap (Tenenbaum, de Silva and Langford, 2000) applies classical scaling to shortest-path distances in a k-NN graph, an estimate of geodesic distances on \mathcal{M}. When the distances are not Euclidean, B has negative eigenvalues, which measure the distortion.

Definition 3.11 — t-SNE and UMAP t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) defines Gaussian affinities p_{ij} in the data, with a width per point set by a target perplexity. It then places points y_i in the plane to minimize \mathrm{KL}(P \,\|\, Q), where q_{ij} \propto \big( 1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2 \big)^{-1} .

UMAP (McInnes, Healy and Melville, 2018) builds a weighted k-NN graph with weights w_{ij} \in [0, 1] and low-dimensional similarities v_{ij} = (1 + a \|y_i - y_j\|^{2b})^{-1}. It minimizes the edge-wise binary cross-entropy \sum_{i \neq j} \Big[ w_{ij} \log \frac{w_{ij}}{v_{ij}} + (1 - w_{ij}) \log \frac{1 - w_{ij}}{1 - v_{ij}} \Big] .

The heavy tails of q and v let clusters separate in the plane. In exchange, the sizes of clusters and the gaps between them carry no metric meaning.

4. Geometric deep learning

Convolutions are the linear layers that respect translations (Proposition 2.10). This chapter builds the layers that respect the symmetries of graphs and of point clouds, then tests them on two scientific problems.

4.1 Convolutions on graphs

Definition 4.1 — Graph signals and permutation equivariance A graph with n nodes has an adjacency matrix A and node features H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}.

A permutation matrix P relabels the nodes: A \mapsto P A P^\top and H \mapsto P H. A layer \Phi is permutation-equivariant if \Phi(P A P^\top, P H) = P\, \Phi(A, H) .

Definition 4.2 — Message passing A message-passing layer updates each node from its neighbourhood \mathcal{N}(i): h_i' = \phi\Big( h_i,\ \bigoplus_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \psi(h_i, h_j, e_{ij}) \Big), where \psi and \phi are learned functions, e_{ij} are edge features, and \bigoplus is a symmetric aggregation (sum, mean or maximum).

Proposition 4.3 — Message passing is permutation-equivariant Every layer of Definition 4.2 satisfies Definition 4.1.

Proof. Relabelling the nodes by \pi sends the neighbourhood of i to that of \pi(i), with the same features. A symmetric aggregation depends only on the multiset of messages, so the new feature of \pi(i) equals the old feature of i. ∎

Definition 4.4 — Spectral filters Let L = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2} = U \Lambda U^\top be the normalized Laplacian. The graph Fourier transform of a signal x is U^\top x, and a spectral filter is g(L)\, x = U g(\Lambda) U^\top x .

Proposition 4.5 — Polynomial filters are local For a polynomial filter p(L) = \sum_{k \leq K} \theta_k L^k, the output at node i depends only on nodes at graph distance at most K from i. Moreover, p(P L P^\top) = P\, p(L)\, P^\top.

Proof. L has the sparsity pattern of A + I. Hence the entry (L^k)_{ij} is a sum over walks of length k from node i to node j.

This sum is empty, so the entry vanishes, when the graph distance from i to j exceeds k.

The second identity holds for each power, since P^\top P = I. ∎

The graph convolutional network (GCN) of Kipf and Welling (2017) keeps degree 1 with a renormalization, H' = \sigma(\hat A H W), where \hat A = \tilde D^{-1/2} (A + I) \tilde D^{-1/2} . Stacking many such layers averages features over larger and larger regions until all nodes look alike, which is called over-smoothing (Exercise 16).

4.2 Point clouds and equivariance

Definition 4.6 — Euclidean equivariance A map \Phi from point clouds x \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 3} to point clouds is E(3)-equivariant if, for every orthogonal Q and translation t, \Phi(x Q^\top + \mathbf{1} t^\top) = \Phi(x)\, Q^\top + \mathbf{1} t^\top . A map to features is invariant if its output does not change.

Proposition 4.7 — An equivariant update For any function \phi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, the update x_i' = x_i + \frac1n \sum_j (x_i - x_j)\, \phi\big( \|x_i - x_j\|^2 \big) is E(3)-equivariant (Satorras, Hoogeboom and Welling, 2021).

Proof. Distances are invariant. Differences x_i - x_j do not see translations and rotate with Q, so the sum rotates with Q; adding x_i restores the translation. ∎

A point-cloud convolution sums g(x_j - x_i)\, f_j over the neighbours of x_i. For rotation invariance, the offset must be expressed in a local frame (normal and tangent directions), and tangent directions are defined only up to rotation.

4.3 Five major challenges

The official text announces five challenges without naming them. These notes propose the following five, each discussed elsewhere on the page.

  1. Choosing the graph. Neighbourhoods depend on the sampling density and the scale (ch. 3).
  2. Invariance. Rigid motions, local frames and orientation ambiguities (Section 4.2).
  3. Discretization. A surface can be a mesh, a point cloud or a voxel grid at any resolution; predictions should not depend on that choice (ch. 6).
  4. Scale. Shapes have 10^5 to 10^6 points, so neighbour searches and memory dominate (ch. 7).
  5. Data. Medical data sets often hold tens of labelled shapes, so priors such as invariances must carry the information.

4.4 Case study: protein docking

Proteins interact through their surfaces, where shape and chemistry must be complementary. MaSIF (Gainza and coauthors, 2020) computes a mesh of the molecular surface, cuts geodesic patches and applies learned filters in local polar coordinates. dMaSIF (Sverrisson, Feydy, Correia and Bronstein, 2021) samples points directly on a level set of a smooth distance to the atoms. It computes chemical and curvature features there and convolves them in local frames, end to end and much faster.

Definition 4.8 — Smooth distance to a molecule For atom centres a_1, \dots, a_K and a smoothing scale s > 0, let \mathrm{SD}(x) = -s \log \sum_{k = 1}^K \exp\big( -\|x - a_k\| / s \big) . It is smooth away from the centres, and \min_k \|x - a_k\| - s \log K \leq \mathrm{SD}(x) \leq \min_k \|x - a_k\| .

Proof. The sum lies between its largest term, e^{-\min_k \|x - a_k\| / s}, and K times that term; take -s \log. ∎

A surface is the level set \mathrm{SD} = r, and points are pushed onto it by Newton-like steps along \nabla \mathrm{SD}. The construction is a log-sum-exp (Proposition 0.9), the same reduction as in ch. 6 and 7.

4.5 Case study: lung registration

Comparing inhale and exhale CT scans of a patient requires a deformation matching the two vessel trees, extracted as point clouds without correspondences; branches may be missing in either scan. Registration minimizes \mathcal{D}\big( \varphi_\sharp \mu_{\text{source}}, \mu_{\text{target}} \big) + \mathcal{R}(\varphi) over deformations \varphi. Here \mathcal{D} compares measures without correspondences (a kernel norm or a transport cost, ch. 6), and \mathcal{R} penalizes irregular deformations.

Shen, Feydy and coauthors (2021) combine robust optimal transport, which allows mass to appear or vanish where branches are missing, with learned point features and smooth deformation models.

4.6 Should we learn our graphs?

A fixed geometric graph, such as a k-NN graph in space, encodes prior knowledge at a cost O(nk). Dynamic graph CNNs (Wang and coauthors, 2019) recompute the k-NN graph in feature space at every layer. Transformers go further and learn a dense graph.

Definition 4.9 — Self-attention With Q = H W_Q, K = H W_K and V = H W_V, a self-attention layer computes H' = \operatorname{softmax}_{\text{rows}}\Big( \frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \Big) V .

Proposition 4.10 — Attention is message passing on the complete graph Self-attention is a message-passing layer on the complete graph, with weights a_{ij} given by the softmax and messages v_j. It is permutation-equivariant, so without positional encodings it ignores the order of the tokens.

Proof. Row i of the output is \sum_j a_{ij} v_j, an aggregation over all nodes. Relabelling by P gives Q' = P Q, K' = P K and V' = P V.

The softmax acts row by row, so the attention matrix becomes P\, \mathcal{A}\, P^\top, and the output is P \mathcal{A} P^\top P V = P\, \mathcal{A} V . ∎

A learned dense graph is flexible but costs O(n^2) and needs data or positional encodings to recover geometry. A fixed graph is cheap and encodes geometry, but it cannot adapt to the task. Section 6.5 shows that the attention matrix is a normalized Gaussian kernel.

    diff = x[:, None, :] - x[None, :, :]                     # x_i - x_j

5. Riemannian metrics and geodesics

When data live on a curved space, straight lines leave the space and averages fall off it. This chapter replaces them by geodesics and Fréchet means, in continuous models and in their discrete approximations.

5.1 Metrics, geodesics and means

Definition 5.1 — Riemannian metric, length, distance A Riemannian metric on a manifold \mathcal{M} is a smoothly varying inner product g_x on each tangent space T_x \mathcal{M}. The length of a curve and the distance are L(\gamma) = \int_0^1 \sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}\, dt, \qquad d(x, y) = \inf_{\gamma(0) = x,\ \gamma(1) = y} L(\gamma) . Geodesics are the constant-speed curves that locally minimize length.

Definition 5.2 — Exponential and logarithm maps \exp_x(v) is the point reached at time 1 by the geodesic leaving x with velocity v, and \log_x is its local inverse. On the unit sphere, \exp_x(v) = \cos(\|v\|)\, x + \sin(\|v\|)\, \frac{v}{\|v\|}, \qquad d(x, y) = \arccos \langle x, y \rangle .

Proposition 5.3 — Fréchet mean Where the logarithms are defined, the function F(x) = \frac{1}{2n} \sum_i d(x, x_i)^2 has Riemannian gradient \operatorname{grad} F(x) = -\frac1n \sum_i \log_x(x_i) . A Fréchet mean (a minimizer of F) therefore satisfies \sum_i \log_x(x_i) = 0, and the iteration x \leftarrow \exp_x\Big( \frac1n \sum_i \log_x(x_i) \Big) is gradient descent with unit step.

Proof. The gradient of \frac12 d(\cdot, y)^2 at x is -\log_x(y), a consequence of the Gauss lemma (Lee, 2018); sum over i. In \mathbb{R}^d, \log_x(y) = y - x and the iteration returns the arithmetic mean in one step. ∎

5.2 The Poincaré disk

Definition 5.4 — Poincaré disk The Poincaré disk is \mathbb{D} = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| < 1 \} with the metric ds = 2 |dz| / (1 - |z|^2). Its distance is d(z, w) = \operatorname{arcosh}\Big( 1 + \frac{2 |z - w|^2}{(1 - |z|^2)(1 - |w|^2)} \Big) .

Proposition 5.5 — Geometry of the disk (a) d(0, r) = 2 \operatorname{artanh} r = \log \frac{1 + r}{1 - r} for 0 \leq r < 1.

  1. The maps z \mapsto e^{i\theta} (z - a) / (1 - \bar a z), for |a| < 1, are isometries. Geodesics are the diameters and the circular arcs orthogonal to the boundary.

  2. The geodesic circle of radius R has perimeter 2 \pi \sinh R, so the curvature is K = -1.

Proof.

  1. The metric depends only on |z|, and along any curve |\dot\gamma| \geq \big| \tfrac{d}{dt} |\gamma| \big|. A curve from 0 to r therefore has length at least \int_0^r \frac{2\, dt}{1 - t^2} = 2 \operatorname{artanh} r , with equality along the radius.

  2. For these maps, |\varphi'(z)| / (1 - |\varphi(z)|^2) = 1 / (1 - |z|^2), so they preserve ds. They send diameters to circles orthogonal to the boundary, and they move any point to 0, where (a) gives the distance formula.

  3. The circle is the Euclidean circle of radius \rho = \tanh(R/2), of hyperbolic perimeter 2 \pi \rho \cdot 2 / (1 - \rho^2) = 2 \pi \sinh R. Compare with Definition 3.6: 2\pi \sinh R = 2 \pi R (1 + R^2/6 + \dots). ∎

The boundary is infinitely far: d(0, 0.9) = 2.944 and d(0, 0.99) = 5.293. Perimeters grow like e^R, like the number of nodes of a tree at depth R. Trees therefore embed in the disk with arbitrarily small distortion (Sarkar, 2011), but not in a Euclidean plane, which motivates hyperbolic embeddings of hierarchies (Nickel and Kiela, 2017).

5.3 Discrete and continuous models

A continuous model gives a metric tensor and geodesic equations. A discrete model gives a graph whose edges carry lengths: a k-NN graph of samples, the edges of a mesh, or a pixel grid.

Algorithm 5.7 — Dijkstra, 1959 Let the source be s and the edge lengths \ell_{uv} \geq 0. Set \delta(s) = 0 and \delta = \infty elsewhere.

Then repeatedly settle the unsettled node u with the smallest \delta(u). For each neighbour v of u, set \delta(v) \leftarrow \min\big( \delta(v), \delta(u) + \ell_{uv} \big) .

Correctness: when u is settled, any other path to u leaves the settled set through a node whose tentative value is already at least \delta(u), and lengths are non-negative. With a binary heap, the cost is O((n + m) \log n).

Theorem 5.8 — Graph distances approximate geodesics Let samples cover a compact submanifold densely compared with its curvature radius, and connect points closer than \varepsilon. For suitable \varepsilon, the graph distances d_G satisfy, with small \lambda_1, \lambda_2, (1 - \lambda_1)\, d_{\mathcal{M}} \leq d_G \leq (1 + \lambda_2)\, d_{\mathcal{M}} .

Proof. See Bernstein, de Silva, Langford and Tenenbaum (2000), and the sketch in ch. 8. ∎

The theorem needs samples spread in all directions. On a regular grid, it fails.

Example 5.9 — Metrication error of grids On a 4-neighbour pixel grid, the graph distance is the \ell^1 norm: from (0, 0) to (40, 40) it is 80 instead of 56.57, an error of 41 %. On an 8-neighbour grid the diagonal is exact, but to (40, 20) the graph distance is 20 \sqrt 2 + 20 = 48.28 \quad \text{instead of} \quad \sqrt{2000} = 44.72 , an error of 8 % that no grid refinement removes.

5.4 Shortest paths

The distance u(x) = d(x_0, x) solves the eikonal equation \|\nabla u\| = 1/F in a medium of speed F. Fast marching (Sethian, 1996) solves it in Dijkstra’s order with a consistent local update, so the error vanishes with the grid step. The heat method (Crane, Weischedel and Wardetzky, 2013) diffuses heat briefly, normalizes its gradient and integrates it, justified by Varadhan’s formula.

Proposition 5.10 — Varadhan’s formula in \mathbb{R}^d The heat kernel k_t(x, y) = (4 \pi t)^{-d/2} \exp\big( -\|x - y\|^2 / 4t \big) satisfies \lim_{t \to 0}\ -4t \log k_t(x, y) = \|x - y\|^2 .

Proof. -4t \log k_t(x, y) = \|x - y\|^2 + 2 d\, t \log(4 \pi t), and t \log t \to 0. ∎

On a Riemannian manifold the same limit gives d_{\mathcal{M}}(x, y)^2 (Varadhan, 1967). A Gaussian kernel at small scale is a geodesic distance in disguise: kernels and manifolds meet here.

5.5 Practical applications

Hierarchies are embedded in the Poincaré disk. Directions, rotations and diffusion tensors are averaged by Fréchet means on spheres, rotation groups and positive matrices. Geodesic distances on anatomical surfaces give pose-independent descriptors. GeomStats (Miolane and coauthors, 2020) implements these spaces.

Code 5.11 — Lab: a Fréchet mean on the sphere and grid shortest paths

import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import lil_matrix
from scipy.sparse.csgraph import dijkstra

def exp_map(x, v):                            # unit sphere: follow the great circle
    t = np.linalg.norm(v)
    return x if t < 1e-12 else np.cos(t) * x + np.sin(t) * v / t

def log_map(x, y):                            # inverse of exp_map at x
    c = np.clip(x @ y, -1, 1)
    u = y - c * x
    n = np.linalg.norm(u)
    return np.zeros(3) if n < 1e-12 else np.arccos(c) * u / n

x = pts[0]
for it in range(20):                          # Riemannian gradient descent (Proposition 5.3)
    g = np.mean([log_map(x, p) for p in pts], axis=0)
    x = exp_map(x, g)
print(f"Frechet mean {np.round(x, 4)}, gradient norm {np.linalg.norm(g):.1e}")
e = np.mean(pts, axis=0)
print(f"Euclidean mean {np.round(e, 4)}, norm {np.linalg.norm(e):.4f} (off the sphere)")

n = 41                                        # grid graphs: discrete shortest paths
idx = lambda i, j: i * n + j
for name, steps in [("4-neighbour", [(0, 1), (1, 0)]),
                    ("8-neighbour", [(0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1), (1, -1)])]:
    W = lil_matrix((n * n, n * n))
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            for di, dj in steps:
                if 0 <= i + di < n and 0 <= j + dj < n:
                    W[idx(i, j), idx(i + di, j + dj)] = np.hypot(di, dj)
    D = dijkstra(W.tocsr(), directed=False, indices=idx(0, 0))
    for (i, j) in [(40, 0), (40, 40), (40, 20)]:
        print(f"{name}: to ({i:2d},{j:2d}) graph {D[idx(i, j)]:6.2f}, Euclidean {np.hypot(i, j):6.2f}")

After 20 iterations the gradient norm is 2 \times 10^{-13}. The Euclidean mean of the same points has norm 0.70: it lies inside the ball, off the sphere. The grid distances reproduce Example 5.9: 80.00 and 60.00 on the 4-neighbour grid, 56.57 and 48.28 on the 8-neighbour grid.

6. Probability distributions

Histograms, densities, point clouds and populations are probability distributions. This chapter compares them pointwise, by blurring and by moving mass, then shows that one matrix underlies kernels, graphs, transport and attention.

6.1 Geometry in statistics

Definition 6.1 — Measures and three families of discrepancies A point cloud x_1, \dots, x_n with weights a_i \geq 0 summing to 1 is the measure \alpha = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}. Discrepancies between measures fall into three families:

  • divergences, which compare densities at the same location: total variation, Hellinger, Kullback–Leibler;
  • kernel norms, which blur the measures with a kernel, then compare them;
  • transport costs, which measure the effort to move one measure onto the other.

Proposition 6.2 — Divergences ignore the ground distance If \alpha and \beta have disjoint supports, then \mathrm{TV}(\alpha, \beta) = 1 and \mathrm{KL}(\alpha \,\|\, \beta) = +\infty, however close the supports are.

Proof. Total variation is \sup_A |\alpha(A) - \beta(A)|; take A the support of \alpha. KL is infinite as soon as \alpha charges a set where \beta vanishes. ∎

For \delta_0 and \delta_t, the values are the same for t = 0.01 and t = 100, so a loss built on them gives no gradient. Point clouds, almost surely disjoint, need the other two families.

6.2 Information geometry

Definition 6.3 — Fisher information metric For a smooth family of densities p_\theta, \theta \in \Theta \subset \mathbb{R}^k, the Fisher metric is g_{ij}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\big[ \partial_i \log p_\theta(X)\ \partial_j \log p_\theta(X) \big] .

Proposition 6.4 — KL is locally the Fisher metric For a small displacement \delta, \mathrm{KL}(p_\theta \,\|\, p_{\theta + \delta}) = \tfrac12\, \delta^\top g(\theta)\, \delta + o(\|\delta\|^2) .

Proof. Expand \log p_{\theta + \delta} to second order in \delta and take the expectation under p_\theta. The first-order term vanishes, since \mathbb{E}\, \partial_i \log p_\theta = \partial_i \int p_\theta = 0 . The second-order term is -\frac12 \delta^\top H \delta, with H = \mathbb{E}[\nabla^2 \log p_\theta], and -H = g by the same differentiation under the integral. ∎

Proposition 6.5 — Gaussians form a hyperbolic plane For \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2), the Fisher metric is ds^2 = (d\mu^2 + 2\, d\sigma^2) / \sigma^2. With x = \mu / \sqrt 2, this is twice the metric of the hyperbolic half-plane, so d_{\mathrm{FR}} = \sqrt 2\, \operatorname{arcosh}\Big( 1 + \frac{(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2 / 2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2}{2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2} \Big) .

Proof in ch. 8.

Two Gaussians with the same mean and widths \sigma and 2\sigma are at distance \sqrt 2 \log 2 = 0.98, whatever \sigma.

Proposition 6.6 — The natural gradient is intrinsic Let \theta = \phi(\eta) be a reparametrization with Jacobian J. The natural gradient g(\theta)^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} (Amari, 1998) transforms like a velocity, \Delta \eta = J^{-1} \Delta \theta, so the update does not depend on the parametrization to first order.

Proof in ch. 8.

6.3 Kernel norms

Definition 6.7 — Kernel norm, maximum mean discrepancy For a positive definite kernel k, the squared kernel norm between measures is \|\alpha - \beta\|_k^2 = \iint k(x, y)\, d(\alpha - \beta)(x)\, d(\alpha - \beta)(y) . For point clouds, it is a^\top K_{xx} a + b^\top K_{yy} b - 2\, a^\top K_{xy} b.

Proposition 6.8 — Dual formula With m_\alpha = \int k(\cdot, x)\, d\alpha(x) the mean embedding in \mathcal{H}_k, \|\alpha - \beta\|_k = \|m_\alpha - m_\beta\|_{\mathcal{H}} = \sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}} \leq 1} \int f\, d(\alpha - \beta) .

Proof. By the reproducing property, \int f\, d\alpha = \langle f, m_\alpha \rangle in \mathcal{H}. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the supremum by \|m_\alpha - m_\beta\|, with equality for f proportional to m_\alpha - m_\beta. Expanding \|m_\alpha - m_\beta\|^2 gives Definition 6.7. ∎

For a characteristic kernel, such as the Gaussian, the norm vanishes only if \alpha = \beta (Gretton and coauthors, 2012). The energy distance uses the conditionally positive kernel k(x, y) = -\|x - y\|. Kernel norms are cheap and smooth, but a Gaussian kernel of width \sigma hardly sees displacements much larger than \sigma.

6.4 Optimal transport

Definition 6.9 — Optimal transport For a cost c(x, y), the transport cost between \alpha and \beta is \mathrm{OT}(\alpha, \beta) = \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\alpha, \beta)} \int c\, d\pi , where \Pi(\alpha, \beta) is the set of couplings, the measures on pairs with marginals \alpha and \beta. For c = \|x - y\|^p, W_p = \mathrm{OT}^{1/p} is the Wasserstein distance.

Proposition 6.10 — Transport on the line sorts For n points on \mathbb{R} with weights 1/n each and a convex cost c(x, y) = h(x - y), sorting both clouds gives an optimal matching. In particular, W_2^2 = \frac1n \sum_i \big( x_{(i)} - y_{(i)} \big)^2 .

Proof. For permutation matchings: if x_1 < x_2 and y_1 < y_2 are crossed, uncrossing does not increase the cost, because (x_1 - y_2)^2 + (x_2 - y_1)^2 - (x_1 - y_1)^2 - (x_2 - y_2)^2 = 2 (x_2 - x_1)(y_2 - y_1) \geq 0 . The same argument holds for convex h. Couplings with equal weights reduce to permutations by Birkhoff’s theorem. ∎

Definition 6.11 — Entropic transport and Sinkhorn divergence For \varepsilon > 0, \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) = \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\alpha, \beta)} \int c\, d\pi + \varepsilon\, \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \alpha \otimes \beta) , and the Sinkhorn divergence is S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) - \tfrac12 \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha) - \tfrac12 \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\beta, \beta) .

Proposition 6.12 — The entropic plan scales a Gibbs kernel For discrete measures with weights a, b, the optimal entropic plan has the form \pi_{ij} = a_i b_j \exp\big( (f_i + g_j - c(x_i, y_j)) / \varepsilon \big) for dual potentials f, g, and \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon = \langle a, f \rangle + \langle b, g \rangle .

Proof.

  1. Form of the plan: the objective is strictly convex in \pi, and the entropy keeps every \pi_{ij} > 0. With multipliers f_i and g_j for the marginal constraints, stationarity in \pi_{ij} reads c_{ij} + \varepsilon \log \frac{\pi_{ij}}{a_i b_j} = f_i + g_j , once the constant \varepsilon coming from the derivative of \pi \log \pi is absorbed into f. This is the stated form.
  2. Value: substituting \varepsilon \log(\pi_{ij} / a_i b_j) = f_i + g_j - c_{ij} into the objective gives \sum_{ij} \pi_{ij} (f_i + g_j). The marginal constraints turn this sum into \langle a, f \rangle + \langle b, g \rangle. ∎

Algorithm 6.13 — Sinkhorn, in the log domain Alternate the two marginal constraints, starting from g = 0: f_i \leftarrow -\varepsilon \log \sum_j b_j\, e^{(g_j - c_{ij})/\varepsilon}, \qquad g_j \leftarrow -\varepsilon \log \sum_i a_i\, e^{(f_i - c_{ij})/\varepsilon} . Each update is a log-sum-exp (Proposition 0.9); Cuturi (2013) brought these iterations to machine learning. ε-scaling starts with a large \varepsilon and decreases it geometrically, which speeds up convergence at small \varepsilon.

Each update makes one marginal exact; convergence is linear, with a rate that degrades as \varepsilon \to 0.

Code 6.14 — Lab: five discrepancies between shifted Gaussians 300 samples of \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and of \mathcal{N}(t, 1) are compared for t = 0.5, 2 and 8. The cost is \|x - y\|^2 / 2, so \sqrt{2 S_\varepsilon} estimates W_2. The official lab uses GeomLoss; here, Algorithm 6.13 is written in PyTorch.

import math
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
torch.set_default_dtype(torch.float64)

def sinkhorn(x, y, eps, n_iter=200):
    """Log-domain Sinkhorn for uniform weights and the cost |x - y|^2 / 2 (Algorithm 6.13)."""
    C = ((x[:, None, :] - y[None, :, :])**2).sum(-1) / 2
    la, lb = -math.log(len(x)), -math.log(len(y))           # log of the uniform weights
    f, g = torch.zeros(len(x)), torch.zeros(len(y))
    schedule = torch.logspace(math.log10(C.max()), math.log10(eps), 50).tolist() + [eps] * n_iter
    for e in schedule:                                      # epsilon-scaling, then iterations at eps
        f = -e * torch.logsumexp(lb + (g[None, :] - C) / e, dim=1)
        g = -e * torch.logsumexp(la + (f[:, None] - C) / e, dim=0)
    P = torch.exp(la + lb + (f[:, None] + g[None, :] - C) / eps)   # optimal plan
    return f.mean() + g.mean(), P, C                         # OT_eps = <a, f> + <b, g>

def sinkhorn_div(x, y, eps):
    return sinkhorn(x, y, eps)[0] - (sinkhorn(x, x, eps)[0] + sinkhorn(y, y, eps)[0]) / 2

def mmd2(x, y, kernel):
    return kernel(x, x).mean() + kernel(y, y).mean() - 2 * kernel(x, y).mean()

gauss = lambda x, y: torch.exp(-((x[:, None] - y[None])**2).sum(-1) / 2)    # sigma = 1
energy = lambda x, y: -((x[:, None] - y[None])**2).sum(-1).sqrt()          # k = -|x - y|

n = 300
x = torch.randn(n, 1)
print("   t |  KL   | Gauss MMD^2 | energy | W2 (sorted) | sqrt(2 S_eps)")
for t in [0.5, 2.0, 8.0]:
    y = torch.randn(n, 1) + t
    w2 = ((x.sort(0)[0] - y.sort(0)[0])**2).mean().sqrt()
    s = sinkhorn_div(x, y, eps=0.01)
    print(f"{t:4.1f} | {t**2 / 2:5.2f} | {mmd2(x, y, gauss):11.3f} | {mmd2(x, y, energy):6.3f} |"
          f" {w2:11.3f} | {(2 * s).clamp(min=0).sqrt():.3f}")
_, P, _ = sinkhorn(x, torch.randn(n, 1) + 2, eps=0.01)
print(f"marginal errors: {(P.sum(1) - 1 / n).abs().sum():.1e}, {(P.sum(0) - 1 / n).abs().sum():.1e}")

KL is the closed form t^2/2 between the Gaussian laws; between the empirical measures it is infinite (Proposition 6.2). The Gaussian kernel norm saturates (0.058, 0.611, 1.169, near its limit 2/\sqrt3), and the energy distance grows linearly.

\sqrt{2 S_\varepsilon} matches the sorted W_2 to three decimals: 0.568, 2.048 and 8.070. The marginal errors are 9 \times 10^{-4} and 10^{-14}.

Theorem 6.15 — Sinkhorn divergences For the cost c(x, y) = \|x - y\|^p with p \in \{1, 2\}, on a compact domain, the Sinkhorn divergence is non-negative, convex in each argument, and vanishes only if \alpha = \beta. Moreover, S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) \xrightarrow[\varepsilon \to 0]{} \mathrm{OT}(\alpha, \beta), \qquad S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) \xrightarrow[\varepsilon \to \infty]{} \tfrac12 \|\alpha - \beta\|_{-c}^2 .

The proof is in Feydy, Séjourné, Vialard, Amari, Trouvé and Peyré (2019); ch. 8 explains the role of the debiasing terms.

Code 6.16 — Lab: gradient flows towards a target cloud A square of 200 points flows towards two small blobs. For the kernel norm, by gradient descent; for the Sinkhorn divergence, each point moves to its barycentric target under \pi^{xy}, corrected by the debiasing plan \pi^{xx} (the gradient of S_\varepsilon, up to a factor n).

import math
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)

def sinkhorn_plan(x, y, eps, n_iter=50):
    """Optimal entropic plan for uniform weights and the cost |x - y|^2 / 2."""
    C = ((x[:, None] - y[None])**2).sum(-1) / 2
    la, lb = -math.log(len(x)), -math.log(len(y))
    f, g = torch.zeros(len(x)), torch.zeros(len(y))
    for e in torch.logspace(math.log10(C.max()), math.log10(eps), 30).tolist() + [eps] * n_iter:
        f = -e * torch.logsumexp(lb + (g[None] - C) / e, dim=1)
        g = -e * torch.logsumexp(la + (f[:, None] - C) / e, dim=0)
    return torch.exp(la + lb + (f[:, None] + g[None] - C) / eps)

def bary(P, z):                       # barycentric projection: where the plan sends each x_i
    return (P @ z) / P.sum(1, keepdim=True)

n = 200
y = torch.cat([torch.randn(n // 2, 2) * 0.15 + torch.tensor([2.0, 2.0]),     # target: two blobs
               torch.randn(n // 2, 2) * 0.15 + torch.tensor([2.0, -1.0])])
x0 = torch.rand(n, 2) * 0.8 - torch.tensor([2.0, 0.4])                      # source: a square

def flow_mmd(x, sigma=0.3, lr=2.0, steps=300):
    x = x.clone().requires_grad_(True)
    k = lambda a, b: torch.exp(-((a[:, None] - b[None])**2).sum(-1) / (2 * sigma**2))
    for _ in range(steps):
        loss = k(x, x).mean() - 2 * k(x, y).mean()          # MMD^2 up to a constant
        g, = torch.autograd.grad(loss, x)
        x = (x - lr * n * g).detach().requires_grad_(True)
    return x.detach()

def flow_sinkhorn(x, eps=0.01, lr=0.5, steps=40):
    for _ in range(steps):                                  # x_i += lr (T_xy(x_i) - T_xx(x_i))
        x = x + lr * (bary(sinkhorn_plan(x, y, eps), y) - bary(sinkhorn_plan(x, x, eps), x))
    return x

runs = [("start", x0), ("MMD, sigma=0.3", flow_mmd(x0, sigma=0.3)),
        ("MMD, sigma=2", flow_mmd(x0, sigma=2.0)), ("Sinkhorn div.", flow_sinkhorn(x0))]
for name, x in runs:
    near = torch.cdist(x, y).min(1).values                  # distance to the closest target point
    print(f"{name:14s}: median distance to target {near.median():.3f}, "
          f"points farther than 0.5: {(near > 0.5).float().mean():.0%}")

The points start at a median distance 3.38 from the target. With a narrow kernel (\sigma = 0.3), the attraction vanishes and only self-repulsion acts: the median grows to 3.97.

A wide kernel (\sigma = 2) brings the median to 0.029 but strands 6 % of the points. The Sinkhorn divergence brings every point within 0.5 of the target (median 0.021).

6.5 One matrix, five readings

For points x_i, y_j and a width \sigma, consider K_{ij} = \exp(-\|x_i - y_j\|^2 / 2\sigma^2).

  1. It is the kernel matrix of a Gaussian kernel method (ch. 2).
  2. It is the weight matrix of a neighbourhood graph (ch. 3).
  3. Up to a constant factor, it is a heat kernel at time t = \sigma^2/2, whose logarithm gives geodesic distances (Proposition 5.10).
  4. It is the Gibbs kernel of entropic transport with \varepsilon = \sigma^2 and cost \|x - y\|^2/2: Sinkhorn rescales its rows and columns (Proposition 6.12).
  5. Normalized by rows, it is an attention matrix.

Proposition 6.17 — Attention is a normalized Gaussian kernel If all keys have the same norm, then for every query q, \operatorname{softmax}_j\Big( \frac{\langle q, k_j \rangle}{\tau} \Big) = \operatorname{softmax}_j\Big( -\frac{\|q - k_j\|^2}{2 \tau} \Big) . Attention is then a Nadaraya–Watson smoother with a Gaussian kernel of width \sqrt\tau, or equivalently half a Sinkhorn step.

Proof. Expanding the square, -\frac{\|q - k_j\|^2}{2\tau} = \frac{\langle q, k_j \rangle}{\tau} - \frac{\|q\|^2 + \|k_j\|^2}{2\tau} . The last term does not depend on j, and the softmax is invariant under adding a constant. ∎

Key result — one matrix, many methods K_{ij} = e^{-\|x_i - y_j\|^2 / 2\sigma^2}: \quad \text{kernel} \;=\; \text{graph} \;=\; \text{heat kernel} \;=\; \text{Gibbs kernel of } \mathrm{OT}_{\sigma^2} \;=\; \text{attention after normalization}

Doubly stochastic attention, which runs Sinkhorn to the end, has been studied as Sinkformers (Sander, Ablin, Blondel and Peyré, 2022).

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Divergences ignore the ground distance; kernel norms blur then compare; transport moves mass.
  • The Fisher metric is the Hessian of KL; Gaussians form a hyperbolic plane.
  • Entropic transport is solved by log-sum-exp iterations; Sinkhorn divergences interpolate between transport and kernel norms.
  • The Gaussian kernel matrix is a kernel, a graph, a heat kernel, a Gibbs kernel and an attention matrix.

7. GPU programming

Every method of this course reduces to matrix products, kernel sums and log-sum-exps. Their speed depends less on the number of operations than on the movement of data.

7.1 What is a GPU?

A CPU has a few cores built for latency, with large caches. A GPU has thousands of simple arithmetic units, grouped into multiprocessors that run threads in groups executing the same instruction on different data (warps of 32 threads on NVIDIA hardware).

Registers and shared memory, on each multiprocessor, are fast but hold only tens to hundreds of kilobytes. Global memory holds tens of gigabytes at a few terabytes per second on data-centre GPUs; the host link carries tens of gigabytes per second. Peak arithmetic reaches tens of TFLOP/s in single precision.

7.2 Main rules of GPU programming

Definition 7.1 — Arithmetic intensity The arithmetic intensity I of a computation is its number of floating-point operations divided by the number of bytes it moves between global memory and the processor.

Proposition 7.2 — Roofline bound On a processor with peak throughput P_{\text{peak}} and memory bandwidth B, the attained throughput satisfies P \leq \min\big( P_{\text{peak}},\ I \cdot B \big) .

Proof. The computation lasts at least \text{flops} / P_{\text{peak}} and at least \text{bytes} / B. Divide the number of flops by the larger of the two. ∎

This is the roofline model (Williams, Waterman and Patterson, 2009). With P_{\text{peak}} = 20 TFLOP/s and B = 2 TB/s, the ridge point is I^\star = 10 flop/byte. Adding two float32 vectors does 1 flop per 12 bytes: under 1 % of the peak.

Multiplying two n \times n matrices does 2n^3 flops for 12 n^2 bytes, an intensity n/6, past the ridge for n \geq 60. The main rules follow.

  1. Keep data on the GPU: the host link is about a hundred times slower.
  2. Raise the intensity: fuse operations, recompute cheap intermediates instead of storing them.
  3. Expose parallelism: thousands of threads, small problems batched together.
  4. Read memory contiguously; avoid branches that split a warp.
  5. Use the lowest accurate precision, and stabilize reductions with the log-sum-exp.

7.3 Accelerating scientific code

Kernel sums a_i = \sum_j k(x_i, y_j)\, b_j and their log-sum-exp variants appear in ch. 2, 3, 4 and 6. Stored densely, the M \times N matrix takes 4 TB for M = N = 10^6 in float32.

Proposition 7.3 — Dense versus tiled reductions For points in \mathbb{R}^D, a Gaussian kernel sum costs about (3D + 4) flops per pair (i, j).

  1. A dense implementation writes and reads the matrix, so its intensity is at most (3D + 4)/8 flop/byte.

  2. A tiled implementation, in which each block of B queries streams the y_j through fast memory, has an intensity close to B(3D + 4) / (4(D + 1)) when M and N are large, and memory O(M + N).

Proof.

  1. MN(3D + 4) flops for 8MN bytes.

  2. Each of the M/B blocks reads the N(D + 1) floats of y and b: about 4 (M/B) N (D + 1) bytes. ∎

For D = 3, the dense version is capped at 1.6 flop/byte, memory-bound, while blocks of 256 queries reach about 200 flop/byte. This is the design of KeOps (Charlier, Feydy, Glaunès, Collin and Durif, 2021).

Proposition 7.4 — Online log-sum-exp Keep a pair (m, s) with \log \sum_{j \text{ seen}} e^{S_j} = m + \log s. When a new block of scores S_J arrives, the update m' = \max\big(m, \max_{j \in J} S_j\big), \qquad s' = s\, e^{m - m'} + \sum_{j \in J} e^{S_j - m'} keeps this invariant, and every exponential lies in (0, 1].

Proof. Multiply by e^{m'}: e^{m'} s' = e^{m} s + \sum_{j \in J} e^{S_j}, which adds the new terms to the sum. Since m' \geq m and m' \geq S_j, the exponents are non-positive. ∎

Merging pairs is associative, so the reduction splits across threads. FlashAttention (Dao and coauthors, 2022) uses the same identity to compute exact attention without storing the attention matrix.

Code 7.5 — Lab: a tiled log-sum-exp The Sinkhorn reduction \log \sum_j \exp(-\|x_i - y_j\|^2/2 + b_j) is computed densely and by tiles of 512 columns; on a GPU, move the tensors with .cuda().

import time
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
M, N, D = 4000, 6000, 3
x, y, b = torch.randn(M, D), torch.randn(N, D), torch.randn(N)

def dense_lse(x, y, b):
    """log sum_j exp(-|x_i - y_j|^2 / 2 + b_j), with the full M x N matrix in memory."""
    S = -torch.cdist(x, y)**2 / 2 + b[None, :]
    return torch.logsumexp(S, dim=1), S.numel() * 4

def tiled_lse(x, y, b, tile=512):
    """Same reduction, streamed over tiles of y with an online log-sum-exp (Proposition 7.4)."""
    m = torch.full((len(x),), -float("inf"))        # running maximum
    s = torch.zeros(len(x))                         # running sum of exp(S - m)
    for j in range(0, len(y), tile):
        S = -torch.cdist(x, y[j:j + tile])**2 / 2 + b[None, j:j + tile]
        m_new = torch.maximum(m, S.max(dim=1).values)
        s = s * torch.exp(m - m_new) + torch.exp(S - m_new[:, None]).sum(dim=1)
        m = m_new
    return m + torch.log(s), len(x) * tile * 4

for f in (dense_lse, tiled_lse):
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    out, mem = f(x, y, b)
    print(f"{f.__name__}: largest buffer {mem / 1e6:6.1f} MB, {time.perf_counter() - t0:.3f} s")
print(f"max difference: {(dense_lse(x, y, b)[0] - tiled_lse(x, y, b)[0]).abs().max():.1e}")

# Arithmetic intensity in float32 (Proposition 7.3), with blocks of B = 256 queries:
flops, B = M * N * (3 * D + 4), 256
dense = flops / (8 * M * N)                               # the matrix is written, then read
tiled = flops / (4 * (M * D + -(-M // B) * N * (D + 1) + M))  # y read once per block of queries
print(f"arithmetic intensity: dense {dense:.2f}, tiled {tiled:.0f} flop/byte")

Both versions agree to 10^{-6}, while the largest buffer falls from 96 MB to 8.2 MB; timings depend on the machine. The intensities are 1.62 flop/byte (dense) and 195 (blocks of 256 queries).

7.4 Is Moore’s law coming to an end?

Moore (1965) observed that the number of components per chip doubled every year, a pace later revised to about every two years. Dennard scaling (Dennard and coauthors, 1974) made smaller transistors faster at constant power density; it broke down in the mid-2000s, and clock frequencies have stayed at a few gigahertz since.

Transistors are now spent on more cores and specialized units. Leiserson and coauthors (2020) argue that future gains will come from performance engineering, algorithms and streamlined hardware. Here, speed-ups come from arithmetic intensity, not faster clocks.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Data movement, not arithmetic, usually limits a GPU.
  • The roofline P \leq \min(P_{\text{peak}}, I B) says whether a kernel is memory- or compute-bound.
  • Tiled kernel sums with an online log-sum-exp never store the M \times N matrix.
  • Since Dennard scaling ended, speed comes from parallelism and algorithms.

8.1 Proofs for chapters 2 and 3

Theorem 2.4 (Cover and Hart). Let X'_n be the nearest neighbour of X among n samples. On the support of the law of X, X'_n \to X almost surely, since a ball around X of any radius has positive probability and eventually contains a sample.

Given the two points, the labels Y and Y' are independent, with probabilities \eta(X) and \eta(X'_n). They disagree with probability \eta(X)\, (1 - \eta(X'_n)) + \eta(X'_n)\, (1 - \eta(X)) \longrightarrow 2\, \eta(X) (1 - \eta(X)) , by continuity of \eta; dominated convergence gives R_1. With m = \min(\eta, 1 - \eta), we have 2 \eta (1 - \eta) = 2 m (1 - m), and Jensen’s inequality for the concave map m \mapsto m (1 - m) gives R_1 \leq 2 R^\star (1 - R^\star).

Proposition 3.7 (angle defects). A face with n_f sides has angles summing to (n_f - 2)\pi, and \sum_f n_f = 2E since each edge borders two faces. All the angles of the surface therefore sum to \pi (2E - 2F), and \sum_v \delta_v = 2 \pi V - \pi (2E - 2F) = 2 \pi (V - E + F) .

Proposition 3.9 (Laplacian eigenmaps). For (a), expand (f_i - f_j)^2 and use \sum_j w_{ij} = D_{ii}. For (b), set Z = D^{1/2} Y and \tilde L = D^{-1/2} L D^{-1/2}. The problem becomes \min \operatorname{tr}(Z^\top \tilde L Z) \quad \text{subject to} \quad Z^\top Z = I, \quad Z^\top D^{1/2} \mathbf{1} = 0 . By Ky Fan’s theorem, the minimum is reached by the eigenvectors of the smallest eigenvalues of \tilde L orthogonal to D^{1/2} \mathbf{1}, which spans the kernel because the graph is connected. Going back, \tilde L z = \lambda z is L v = \lambda D v with v = D^{-1/2} z.

Proposition 3.10 (classical scaling). By Proposition 0.2, \Delta = r \mathbf{1}^\top + \mathbf{1} r^\top - 2 X X^\top with r_i = \|x_i\|^2. Since J \mathbf{1} = 0, and J X = X for centred points, -\tfrac12 J \Delta J = J X X^\top J = X X^\top . Write B = U \Lambda U^\top (Theorem 0.3): U \Lambda^{1/2} has the Gram matrix of X, hence equals X up to an orthogonal map (Proposition 0.5).

8.2 Proofs for chapters 5 and 6

Theorem 5.8 (sketch). For the upper bound, follow a minimizing geodesic and replace it by a chain of samples, each within the sampling radius of the geodesic; consecutive samples are closer than \varepsilon and the chain is barely longer than the geodesic. For the lower bound, each edge is a chord shorter than \varepsilon, much smaller than the radius of curvature, so the geodesic between its ends is at most (1 + \lambda_1') times longer. Bernstein, de Silva, Langford and Tenenbaum (2000) make the constants explicit.

Proposition 6.5 (Fisher–Rao for Gaussians). The scores are \partial_\mu \log p = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \partial_\sigma \log p = -\frac1\sigma + \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^3} . Their second moments are 1/\sigma^2 and (3 - 2 + 1)/\sigma^2 = 2/\sigma^2, using \mathbb{E}(X - \mu)^4 = 3\sigma^4; the cross moment vanishes because odd moments vanish. With x = \mu / \sqrt 2, the metric becomes 2 (dx^2 + d\sigma^2)/\sigma^2, twice the hyperbolic half-plane, whose distance is \operatorname{arcosh}\Big( 1 + \frac{(x_1 - x_2)^2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2}{2 \sigma_1 \sigma_2} \Big) . Multiplying a metric by 2 multiplies distances by \sqrt 2.

Proposition 6.6 (natural gradient). In the coordinates \eta, the chain rule multiplies the gradient by J^\top, and the metric becomes J^\top g J. Therefore (J^\top g J)^{-1} J^\top \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = J^{-1} g^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} , which is the velocity \Delta \theta = g^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} expressed in the coordinates \eta.

8.3 Curvature of graphs by optimal transport

Section 3.4 measured curvature by circles and angle defects. Ollivier (2009) defines a curvature for any metric space with a random walk, using the transport distance W_1 of ch. 6.

Definition 8.1 — Ollivier–Ricci curvature On a graph with shortest-path distance d, let m_x be the uniform measure on the neighbours of x. For an edge (x, y), \kappa(x, y) = 1 - \frac{W_1(m_x, m_y)}{d(x, y)} .

Example 8.2 — Three graphs

  • Complete graph K_5: m_x and m_y share three of their four atoms, and moving the remaining mass 1/4 costs 1, so \kappa = 1 - 1/4 = 0.75 > 0.
  • Long cycle: m_x = \frac12 (\delta_{x-1} + \delta_{x+1}) and m_y is its shift by one, so W_1 = 1 and \kappa = 0, as for a flat space.
  • Tree with degree 3: on an edge (x, y), the best plan sends y \to b_1, a_1 \to x and a_2 \to b_2, at cost (1 + 1 + 3)/3, so \kappa = 1 - 5/3 = -2/3 < 0.

Positive curvature means that neighbourhoods overlap, like on a sphere; negative curvature means that they drift apart, like in the hyperbolic plane.

8.4 Trees need negative curvature

Proposition 8.3 — Embedding a star Let a star have a centre and k leaves, with edges of length 1 and path distance d. Any embedding f in \mathbb{R}^n with d(u, v) \leq \|f(u) - f(v)\| \leq c\, d(u, v) for all nodes has c \geq k^{1/n} - 1 . In the hyperbolic plane, with edges of length R and leaves at equally spaced angles, the distortion tends to 1 as R \to \infty.

Proof. Euclidean case. The leaves are within c of the centre and at least 2 apart. The balls of radius 1 around them are disjoint and lie in the ball of radius c + 1, so k \leq (c + 1)^n by volume.

Hyperbolic case. Two leaves at angle \theta \geq 2\pi/k are at distance \ell with \cosh \ell = \cosh^2 R - \sinh^2 R \cos\theta (hyperbolic law of cosines). For large R, this gives \ell = 2R + 2 \log \sin(\theta/2) + o(1), against 2R in the tree. The ratio tends to 1. ∎

For k = 8 leaves in the plane, the distortion is at least 1.83. In the hyperbolic plane, it is 1.77 for R = 2, 1.24 for R = 5 and 1.11 for R = 10. Sarkar (2011) embeds every tree in the hyperbolic plane with distortion 1 + \varepsilon.

8.5 Convergence of the Sinkhorn algorithm

For positive vectors, Hilbert’s projective metric is d_H(u, u') = \log \max_{i, j} \frac{u_i\, u'_j}{u_j\, u'_i} . It ignores positive scalings, and the coordinatewise inversion u \mapsto a / u is an isometry for it.

Theorem 8.4 — Linear convergence of Sinkhorn Let K > 0 entrywise, and \lambda(K) = \tanh\Big( \frac14 \log \max_{i, j, k, l} \frac{K_{ik} K_{jl}}{K_{jk} K_{il}} \Big) < 1 . The scalings of the Sinkhorn iterations converge to their limit in Hilbert’s metric at the linear rate \lambda(K)^2 per iteration (Franklin and Lorenz, 1989). For K = e^{-C/\varepsilon} with C \geq 0, \lambda(K) \leq \tanh\big( \|C\|_\infty / 2\varepsilon \big) .

Proof. Sketch. Birkhoff’s theorem states that a positive matrix contracts Hilbert’s metric by the factor \lambda(K). One Sinkhorn iteration applies K, an inversion, K^\top and another inversion, hence the rate \lambda(K)^2. For the Gibbs kernel, the cross-ratio is \exp\big( (C_{jk} + C_{il} - C_{ik} - C_{jl}) / \varepsilon \big) \leq e^{2 \|C\|_\infty / \varepsilon} . ∎

For \|C\|_\infty / \varepsilon = 10, the bound gives \lambda^2 = 0.99982: about 5500 iterations to divide the error by e. The bound is pessimistic, but the trend is real, and it explains the \varepsilon-scaling of Algorithm 6.13.

8.6 Why the Sinkhorn divergence is debiased

The entropic cost \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) is not zero when \beta = \alpha, and its minimizer in \beta is not \alpha. When \varepsilon \to \infty, the optimal plan tends to the product \alpha \otimes \beta, so \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) \to \iint c(x, y)\, d\alpha(x)\, d\beta(y) . For c = \|x - y\|^2, this is minimized by a Dirac mass at the mean of \alpha: a loss based on \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon alone collapses the model onto the mean.

The two correction terms of S_\varepsilon remove this bias, so that S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha) = 0. Exercise 15 measures the effect: for two shifted Gaussians, \sqrt{2\, \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon} overestimates W_2 = 2.063 by 13 % at \varepsilon = 1, while \sqrt{2 S_\varepsilon} gives 2.062.

8.7 The graph Laplacian and the Laplace–Beltrami operator

Laplacian eigenmaps (Proposition 3.9) are justified by a limit theorem. Let x_1, \dots, x_n be uniform samples on a compact m-dimensional submanifold \mathcal{M}, and for a smooth f let L_t f(p) = \frac{1}{n\, t\, (4 \pi t)^{m/2}} \sum_{j = 1}^n e^{-\|p - x_j\|^2 / 4t} \big( f(p) - f(x_j) \big) . Belkin and Niyogi (2008) prove that, for a suitable t = t_n \to 0, L_t f(p) converges in probability to \frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{M})}\, \Delta_{\mathcal{M}} f(p) , with \Delta_{\mathcal{M}} the Laplace–Beltrami operator taken with its non-negative sign convention.

The eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian therefore approximate the eigenfunctions of \Delta_{\mathcal{M}}, the Fourier basis of the manifold. With Varadhan’s formula (Proposition 5.10), this is the second link between Gaussian kernels and the geometry of \mathcal{M}.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • The proofs of ch. 2, 3, 5 and 6 rely on Jensen’s inequality, Ky Fan’s theorem, the Gram trick and score computations.
  • Ollivier–Ricci curvature compares neighbourhoods by optimal transport: positive on complete graphs, zero on cycles, negative on trees.
  • Stars and trees embed with bounded distortion in the hyperbolic plane, not in Euclidean space.
  • Sinkhorn converges linearly at a rate close to 1 when \varepsilon is small, hence \varepsilon-scaling; debiasing removes the collapse of \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon.
  • The graph Laplacian of a Gaussian neighbourhood graph converges to the Laplace–Beltrami operator.

9. Research directions and open problems

The tools of this course are active research topics. This chapter, for self-study, points to a few directions, with references that a project can start from.

Scalable geometric computing

Kernel sums, transport plans and attention matrices are all reductions over pairs of points. Work by Charlier, Feydy, Glaunès, Collin and Durif (2021) shows that symbolic tiled reductions run on a GPU without ever storing the M \times N matrix. FlashAttention (Dao, Fu, Ermon, Rudra and Ré, 2022) applies the same online log-sum-exp to the attention of transformers.

Open problems: reductions in high dimension, where tiles of points no longer share neighbours; and multiscale or low-rank approximations with guaranteed errors, compatible with automatic differentiation.

Geometric deep learning and equivariance

Bronstein, Bruna, Cohen and Veličković (2021) organise the field around symmetry groups and the domains on which signals live: grids, groups, graphs, geodesics and gauges. Equivariant graph networks such as those of Satorras, Hoogeboom and Welling (2021) keep the cost of message passing while respecting rigid motions.

Open problems: the trade-off between expressivity and cost when features carry higher-order tensors; and the choice of local frames on surfaces, where the tangent directions are ambiguous.

Proteins and molecules

Protein structure prediction reached experimental accuracy for many targets with AlphaFold (Jumper and coauthors, 2021). Diffusion models generate new protein backbones (Watson and coauthors, 2023), and surface-based networks such as dMaSIF (Sverrisson, Feydy, Correia and Bronstein, 2021) predict interaction sites.

Open problems: representing flexibility and conformational change, and choosing between atoms, surfaces and graphs as the primary data structure. The course Algorithms and Learning for Protein Science continues this thread.

Optimal transport beyond balanced measures

Unbalanced transport (Chizat, Peyré, Schmitzer and Vialard, 2018) lets mass be created or destroyed, which robust registration needs when structures are missing. The sample complexity of Sinkhorn divergences (Genevay, Chizat, Bach, Cuturi and Peyré, 2019) interpolates between that of kernel norms and that of transport as \varepsilon varies.

Open problems: transport between measures in high dimension, where the plug-in estimator converges slowly; and fast, differentiable solvers for the very small \varepsilon needed by sharp registrations.

Intrinsic dimension and the manifold hypothesis

Fefferman, Mitter and Narayanan (2016) give a test of the manifold hypothesis with sample complexity bounds. Pope, Zhu, Abdelkader, Goldblum and Goldstein (2021) estimate the intrinsic dimension of standard image data sets and relate it to the sample complexity of learning.

Open problems: data that mix several dimensions, noise at the scale of the neighbours (Exercise 9), and the link between intrinsic dimension and generalization of deep networks.

Hyperbolic and curved representations

Poincaré embeddings (Nickel and Kiela, 2017) and hyperbolic graph networks (Chami, Ying, Ré and Leskovec, 2019) exploit negative curvature for hierarchies. Sala, De Sa, Gu and Ré (2018) analyse the trade-off between dimension, precision and distortion.

Open problems: numerical precision near the boundary of the disk, optimization on curved spaces at scale, and spaces of mixed curvature.

Transformers as interacting particles

Geshkovski, Letrouit, Polyanskiy and Rigollet (2023) model the tokens of a transformer as particles moving under attention, a normalized Gaussian kernel (Proposition 6.17), and study their clustering. Sinkformers (Sander, Ablin, Blondel and Peyré, 2022) replace the row normalization by Sinkhorn’s.

Open problems: the role of normalization layers in these dynamics, and whether doubly stochastic attention helps on geometric data.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Scalable reductions, equivariant layers and unbalanced transport are active topics with open problems in high dimension.
  • Protein science and medical registration drive many questions on surfaces, point clouds and robustness.
  • Curvature, intrinsic dimension and attention dynamics connect the geometry of this course to current deep learning research.

Exercises

The exercises are sorted by level: ★ applies the course, ★★ combines results or proves a short statement, ★★★ is open-ended. The session plan says which ones to do after each session; exercises 9 and 15 are computational.

  1. ★ Shells and cells. (a) Which fraction of the volume of the unit ball lies within 0.01 of its boundary in dimensions 10, 100 and 1000? (b) How many cubes of side 0.2 are needed to cover [0, 1]^{10}, and [0, 1]^{100}?

    Solution: (a) By Proposition 1.1, 1 - 0.99^d, that is 0.096, 0.634 and 0.99996. (b) By Proposition 1.2 with \varepsilon = 0.1, at least 5^{10} \approx 9.8 \times 10^6 and 5^{100} \approx 7.9 \times 10^{69} cubes; both bounds are reached by the regular grid.

  2. ★ Choosing a split. A node holds 8 positive and 4 negative samples. Split A sends (6+, 0-) left and (2+, 4-) right; split B sends (5+, 1-) left and (3+, 3-) right. Compute the Gini impurities and choose. Does the choice change if the feature is replaced by its logarithm?

    Solution: the parent has G = 1 - 4/9 - 1/9 = 0.444. Split A gives \frac12 \cdot 0 + \frac12 \cdot 0.444 = 0.222; split B gives \frac12 \cdot 0.278 + \frac12 \cdot 0.5 = 0.389. Split A wins, with a gain of 0.222 against 0.056. The logarithm is increasing, so by Proposition 2.2 the same partitions are available and the choice is unchanged.

  3. ★ Two losses, two predictions. For y = 1, compute the gradients of the cross-entropy and of the squared loss with respect to the score z, at z = -3 (a mistake) and z = 3 (a correct prediction).

    Solution: by Proposition 2.7, at z = -3, p = 0.0474: the cross-entropy gives -0.9526 and the squared loss -0.0861, 11 times smaller.

    At z = 3, p = 0.9526: they give -0.0474 and -0.0043. The squared loss is weak exactly where the mistake is large.

  4. ★ Is a cycle Euclidean? The 4-cycle has shortest-path distances 1 between neighbours and 2 between opposite nodes. Apply classical scaling (Proposition 3.10) and compare with the unit square, whose diagonals are \sqrt 2.

    Solution: for the cycle, B = -\frac12 J \Delta J has eigenvalues -1, 0, 2, 2; the negative eigenvalue shows that no Euclidean configuration has these distances. Indeed a node at distance 1 from two points at distance 2 is their midpoint, and both such nodes would coincide. For the square, the eigenvalues are 0, 0, 1, 1, and the two eigenvectors recover the square.

  5. ★ Angle defects of the Platonic solids. Compute the angle defects of the regular tetrahedron, octahedron and icosahedron, and check Proposition 3.7.

    Solution: three, four and five equilateral triangles meet at each vertex, with defects 2\pi - \pi = \pi, 2\pi - 4\pi/3 = 2\pi/3 and 2\pi - 5\pi/3 = \pi/3. With 4, 6 and 12 vertices, the totals are all 4\pi = 2\pi \times 2, since V - E + F = 2 for these surfaces.

  6. ★ Distances in the disk. Compute d(0, \frac12), d(-\frac12, \frac12) and d(\frac12, \frac{i}{2}) in the Poincaré disk, and compare the last one with the path through the origin.

    Solution: d(0, \frac12) = \log 3 = 1.099, and along the diameter distances add up: d(-\frac12, \frac12) = 2 \log 3 = 2.197. For the third pair, |z - w|^2 = \frac12 and (1 - \frac14)^2 = \frac{9}{16}, so d = \operatorname{arcosh}\big( 1 + \tfrac{16}{9} \big) = \operatorname{arcosh}(25/9) = 1.681 , shorter than 2 \log 3 through the origin: the geodesic is an arc bent towards the centre.

  7. ★ Two Dirac masses. For \alpha = \delta_0 and \beta = \delta_t on the real line, compute TV, KL, the Gaussian kernel norm (width \sigma), the energy distance and W_2.

    At t = 3\sigma, compare the derivatives in t of the squared kernel norm and of the squared W_2.

    Solution: TV = 1 and KL = \infty for every t \neq 0. The squared kernel norm is 2 (1 - e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}), the energy distance is 2|t|, and W_2 = |t|.

    At t = 3 with \sigma = 1, the derivative of the squared kernel norm is 6 e^{-4.5} = 0.067, against 2t = 6 for the squared W_2: a kernel loss barely pulls distant points.

  8. ★ Rooflines. A GPU has a peak of 20 TFLOP/s and a bandwidth of 2 TB/s. Bound the throughput of a float32 vector addition and of a dense Gaussian kernel sum in dimension 3. How long does a tiled kernel sum with M = N = 10^6 take at peak?

    Solution: the ridge point is 10 flop/byte. Vector addition, at 1/12 flop/byte, is bounded by 0.17 TFLOP/s. The dense kernel sum, at 13/8 flop/byte (Proposition 7.3), is bounded by 3.25 TFLOP/s. The tiled sum is compute-bound: 13 \times 10^{12} flops take 0.65 s at peak, while the dense matrix would need 4 TB.

  9. ★ Computational: noise and dimension. Sample 2000 points uniformly in the unit square, embed them in \mathbb{R}^{10}, add Gaussian noise of standard deviation s \in \{0, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1\} in all ten directions, and estimate the dimension with the two_nn function of Code 3.12.

    Hints and expected result: with one generator seeded at 0, drawing the points first and then the noise for each level in turn, the estimates are 1.98, 4.27, 6.68, 8.28 and 9.23. The nearest-neighbour distances are about 0.01, so noise of that size already fills the ambient space at the scale TwoNN uses. Subsampling the data, which increases the neighbour distances, brings the estimate back towards 2.

  10. ★★ Trees are 0-hyperbolic. A metric space is \delta-hyperbolic (Gromov) if, for all points x, y, z, w, the largest of the three sums d(x, y) + d(z, w), \quad d(x, z) + d(y, w), \quad d(x, w) + d(y, z) exceeds the second largest by at most 2\delta. Prove that a tree with its path metric is 0-hyperbolic.

    Solution: the four points span a subtree with at most two branch points. After relabelling, the paths from x and y meet at a node u, and those from z and w meet at a node v.

    Let S be the total length of the four pendant paths: S = d(x, u) + d(y, u) + d(z, v) + d(w, v) . A path from the first pair to the second crosses the segment from u to v, so d(x, y) + d(z, w) = S, \qquad d(x, z) + d(y, w) = d(x, w) + d(y, z) = S + 2\, d(u, v) . The two largest sums are equal.

  11. ★★ Style losses only see second moments. Using Proposition 1.10, find two different feature distributions with the same Gram matrix. Which kernel norm of ch. 6 would separate them?

    Solution: \mu_a = \frac12 (\delta_{e_1} + \delta_{-e_1}) and \mu_b = \delta_{e_1} both have Gram matrix e_1 e_1^\top, but different means. The kernel \langle f, g \rangle^2 is not characteristic: it only sees second moments. A Gaussian kernel norm, or a transport cost, separates them, as in the optimal-transport style losses of Kolkin and coauthors (2019).

  12. ★★ The simplex is a sphere. Show that the Fisher metric of the categorical distributions on n classes is \sum_i dp_i^2 / p_i, and that s_i = \sqrt{p_i} maps it to four times the round metric of the unit sphere. Deduce d_{\mathrm{FR}}(p, q) = 2 \arccos \sum_i \sqrt{p_i q_i} . Compare the distances between (0.9, 0.1) and (0.99, 0.01), and between (0.51, 0.49) and (0.6, 0.4).

    Solution: take a tangent vector dp with \sum_i dp_i = 0. The derivative of \log p_X along it is dp_X / p_X, whose second moment is the Fisher metric: \mathbb{E}\Big[ \Big( \frac{dp_X}{p_X} \Big)^2 \Big] = \sum_i p_i\, \frac{dp_i^2}{p_i^2} = \sum_i \frac{dp_i^2}{p_i} . With s_i = \sqrt{p_i}, we have dp_i = 2 s_i\, ds_i, so the metric is 4 \sum_i ds_i^2, four times the round metric. Since \sum_i s_i^2 = 1, the point s lies on the unit sphere, where the geodesic distance is \arccos \langle s, s' \rangle; the factor 4 doubles it.

    The two pairs, both moved by 0.09, are at distances 0.443 and 0.181: near the faces of the simplex, the same change costs 2.4 times more, which is why confident errors are expensive (Section 2.3).

  13. ★★ Attention without positions. Prove Proposition 6.17 and Proposition 4.10. Then add sinusoidal positional encodings P_i to the inputs, h_i \mapsto h_i + P_i, and show by a two-token example that the layer is no longer permutation-equivariant.

    Hints: for the second part, take h_1 = h_2; without encodings the two outputs are equal, and swapping the tokens changes nothing. With P_1 \neq P_2, the outputs differ, and swapping the inputs does not swap the outputs, because the encodings stay attached to the positions.

  14. ★★ Merging partial log-sum-exps. For pairs (m_1, s_1) and (m_2, s_2) as in Proposition 7.4, define their merge with m = \max(m_1, m_2) and s = s_1 e^{m_1 - m} + s_2 e^{m_2 - m}. Prove that the merge is associative and commutative, and deduce the depth of a parallel reduction over N blocks.

    Solution: the merge represents \log(e^{m_1} s_1 + e^{m_2} s_2), and its first component is a maximum. Both are associative and commutative, so any bracketing gives the same pair. A balanced binary tree of merges reduces N blocks in \lceil \log_2 N \rceil parallel steps.

  15. ★★ Computational: the entropic bias. With the sinkhorn function of Code 6.14, take 300 samples of \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and of \mathcal{N}(2, 1), and \varepsilon \in \{1, 0.1, 0.01\}.

    Compare \sqrt{2\, \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta)} and \sqrt{2 S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta)} with the sorted W_2, and print \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha).

    Hints and expected result: with seed 0, W_2 = 2.063. The biased estimate gives 2.333, 2.139 and 2.076, and \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha) = 0.601, 0.163 and 0.027. The debiased one gives 2.062, 2.062 and 2.063: for a translation, the debiasing of Section 8.6 removes almost all of the entropic error.

  16. ★★ Over-smoothing. Let G be connected and \hat A = \tilde D^{-1/2} (A + I) \tilde D^{-1/2}. Show that the eigenvalues of \hat A lie in (-1, 1], and that 1 is simple with eigenvector \tilde D^{1/2} \mathbf{1}.

    Deduce that \hat A^k H converges to a matrix whose rows are proportional to \sqrt{\tilde d_i} times a common vector.

    Solution: \hat A is similar to the random-walk matrix \tilde D^{-1} (A + I), which is stochastic, irreducible (connected graph) and aperiodic (self-loops). Its eigenvalue 1 is simple and the others have modulus less than 1.

    In the spectral decomposition, \hat A^k \to u u^\top with u \propto \tilde D^{1/2} \mathbf{1}, so row i of \hat A^k H tends to u_i\, u^\top H. Deep linear GCNs lose all information except the degree.

  17. ★★★ Should we learn the graph? Generate point clouds of 256 points sampled on three classes of surfaces (spheres, tori, ellipsoids), at random poses and scales. Compare (a) a message-passing network on a fixed k-NN graph in space, (b) the same network with the graph recomputed in feature space at each layer, and (c) a transformer without positional encoding.

    Hints: report the accuracy against the number of training clouds, from 50 to 5000, and the training time. Expect the fixed graph to win with few data, and the learned graphs to catch up with many; test the invariance to rotations by rotating the test set. Use Proposition 4.10 to explain why (c) needs no positional encoding here.

  18. ★★★ Hyperbolic versus Euclidean trees. Embed a complete binary tree of depth 6 in the Poincaré disk by placing the children of each node symmetrically at a fixed hyperbolic distance R, and in the Euclidean plane by classical scaling of the tree distances. Measure the distortion of both embeddings as functions of R.

    Hints: use Code 5.6 for distances and Proposition 8.3 for intuition. The Euclidean distortion grows with the depth, while the hyperbolic one decreases as R grows, until floating-point precision near the boundary becomes the limit (Sala and coauthors, 2018).

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 1 hour 30 minutes, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic devices, four exercises.

  1. Question (4 points). Explain the curse of dimensionality with two exact statements, and explain why nearest-neighbour methods still work on images of handwritten digits.

    Expected: the shell fraction 1 - (1 - \varepsilon)^d and the covering bound (2\varepsilon)^{-d}, or the concentration of distances with relative spread \sqrt{1.4/d}; then the manifold hypothesis, with an intrinsic dimension near 9 for the digits (Code 3.12), and invariances encoded in the distance.

  2. Question (6 points). (a) Compute d(0, 0.9) in the Poincaré disk. (b) Compute the Fisher–Rao distance between \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and \mathcal{N}(0, 4). (c) Compute W_2 between the uniform empirical measures of \{0, 1, 5\} and \{2, 3, 4\}.

    Expected: (a) \log 19 = 2.944. (b) Same mean, with \sigma_1 = 1 and \sigma_2 = 2 in Proposition 6.5: d_{\mathrm{FR}} = \sqrt 2\, \operatorname{arcosh}(1 + 1/4) = \sqrt 2 \log 2 = 0.980 .

    1. Sorting matches 0 \to 2, 1 \to 3 and 5 \to 4, so W_2^2 = (4 + 4 + 1)/3 = 3 and W_2 = 1.732.

  3. Question (5 points). Prove that self-attention is permutation-equivariant, and that when all keys have the same norm, the attention weights are those of a Gaussian kernel normalized by rows.

    Expected: Proposition 4.10 (relabelling multiplies the queries, keys and values by P, and the row-wise softmax gives P \mathcal{A} P^\top), then Proposition 6.17 (expand \|q - k_j\|^2; the terms constant in j cancel in the softmax).

  4. Question (5 points). A Gaussian kernel sum with M = N = 10^6 points in \mathbb{R}^3 must run on a GPU with 20 TFLOP/s and 2 TB/s. Compare a dense and a tiled implementation, and explain how to compute a stable log-sum-exp in the tiled version.

    Expected: dense storage takes 4 TB and the intensity is at most 1.6 flop/byte; tiled blocks of 256 queries reach about 200 flop/byte, compute-bound, about 0.65 s at peak (Proposition 7.3); the online pair (m, s) of Proposition 7.4, with its update and its associative merge.
