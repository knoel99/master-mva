0. Review: norms, gradients and arrays

Later chapters pick a loss and a distance. This chapter fixes the two formulas they all share, and the array layout used to compute them. The official prerequisite is linear algebra and basic Python. Nothing here goes past that level.

The Euclidean structure

Definition 0.1 — Euclidean structure

Let x and y be vectors in \mathbb{R}^d. Their inner product \langle x, y \rangle is the sum of the products of coordinates. The Euclidean norm \|x\| is the square root of \langle x, x \rangle.

Proposition 0.2 — Cauchy-Schwarz

For all x and y, |\langle x, y \rangle| \leq \|x\| \, \|y\| .

Proof. If y = 0, both sides are 0. Otherwise t \mapsto \|x + t y\|^2 is a nonnegative quadratic in the real variable t.

Expand it as \|x\|^2 + 2 t \langle x, y \rangle + t^2 \|y\|^2. A nonnegative quadratic has a nonpositive discriminant, which is the claim. ∎

Equality holds when one vector is a scalar multiple of the other. The inequality is the only reason a correlation lies in [-1, 1], and it is the step that turns an inner product into an angle.

Two losses on probability vectors

Definition 0.3 — Squared Euclidean loss and cross-entropy

Let p and q be probability vectors of the same length, with every positive coordinate of p positive in q as well. The squared Euclidean loss is \|p - q\|^2. The cross-entropy is -\sum_i p_i \log q_i.

Example 0.4 — The ranking can reverse

Take p = (0.9, 0.1), q = (0.8, 0.2) and r = (0.99, 0.01). Then \|p - q\|^2 = 0.0200 and \|p - r\|^2 = 0.0162, so the squared loss prefers r.

With log(0.8) = -0.22314 and log(0.2) = -1.60944, the cross-entropy of p relative to q is 0.3618. With log(0.99) = -0.01005 and log(0.01) = -4.60517, the cross-entropy relative to r is 0.4696. Cross-entropy prefers q.

A small coordinate that is wrong by a factor of ten is cheap for the squared norm and expensive for the logarithm. That is the whole numerical content of the second official question.

Proposition 0.5 — Gradient in the logits

Let p be a probability vector and let q = \mathrm{softmax}(z), so q_i is proportional to \exp(z_i). The gradient of the cross-entropy with respect to z is q - p.

Proof. Because \sum_i p_i = 1, the cross-entropy equals -\sum_i p_i z_i + \log \sum_k \exp(z_k).

Differentiate in z_j. The first sum contributes -p_j. The log-partition contributes q_j. ∎

Key result — cross-entropy in the logits \nabla_z \big(-\sum_i p_i \log \mathrm{softmax}(z)_i\big) = \mathrm{softmax}(z) - p .

The squared loss does not have this cancellation. Its gradient in q is 2(q - p), with no division by q_i. A coordinate near 0 is therefore a gentle penalty for the squared loss and a steep one for the cross-entropy. ch. 2 uses both gradients on classifiers.

Proposition 0.6 — Expanded squared distance \|x - y\|^2 = \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 - 2 \langle x, y \rangle .

Proof. Expand \langle x - y, x - y \rangle and use symmetry of the inner product. ∎

The identity is the one to code. It replaces a loop over pairs by two norms and one matrix product. ch. 5 explains why that form is the one a GPU finishes quickly. Exercise 2 uses it on a cloud of 40 points.

A sample of n vectors in \mathbb{R}^d is an array of shape (n, d). The pairwise matrix of inner products is the product of that array with its transpose. Subtracting a row vector from a column vector builds the differences. Those two shapes are the only array tricks the exercises need.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Cauchy-Schwarz bounds an inner product by the product of the norms.
  • On (0.9, 0.1), squared Euclidean distance and cross-entropy rank (0.8, 0.2) and (0.99, 0.01) in opposite orders.
  • In the logits, the cross-entropy gradient is \mathrm{softmax}(z) - p.
  • A squared distance expands into two squared norms and one inner product.

1. Why geometry: dimension, triangles and style

A model starts by choosing what a point is. This chapter gives two reasons to refuse the ambient Euclidean vector as a default. High dimension makes local averages empty. Some objects, triangles and images among them, already carry a symmetry that a raw vector ignores.

The curse, on a cube

Proposition 1.1 — Volume of a corner

The cube [0, 1]^d has volume 1. The corner [0, 1/2]^d has volume 2^{-d}.

Proof. Volume of a product of intervals is the product of the lengths. Each side of the corner has length 1/2, and there are d sides. ∎

Example 1.2 — One neighbour in a thousand

At d = 10 the corner has volume 1/1024. At d = 20 the volume is about one in a million. A rule that averages only the points inside a fixed corner cube has almost no points left, unless the sample size grows exponentially with d.

Bellman (1961) named this shortage the curse of dimensionality. Adding one weakly related coordinate can create a fluctuation that a small medical cohort cannot average away. Theorem 3.1 is the Gaussian form of the same shortage.

Geometry as a place to put a constraint

A distance is where expert knowledge enters. If a rotation of a protein does not change the biology, the distance ignores that rotation. If two lung images differ by a slide along a rib, the distance does not treat the slide as a change of tissue. The official page places that constraint on anatomical images, protein conformations and physical simulations.

Three storage choices come back all year.

  • A vector, when the coordinates are commensurate and a norm on those coordinates is meaningful.
  • A graph, when the object is a neighbourhood, as with a mesh, a molecule or a k-nearest-neighbour relation.
  • A histogram, when the object is a cloud of mass and the bins themselves have a geometry.

ch. 2 develops the vector. ch. 3 develops the graph. ch. 5 develops the histogram. The rest of this chapter is two examples in which the right space is neither a raw vector nor a raw pixel grid.

The sphere of triangles

Proposition 1.3 — Dimension of triangular shapes

A labeled triangle in the plane has 6 coordinates. After translation and scale are removed, the pre-shape lies on a sphere S^3. After rotation is removed, the shape has dimension 2.

Proof. Three points in \mathbb{R}^2 give 6 numbers. Forcing the centroid to the origin imposes 2 independent linear constraints, so centered triangles form a 4-dimensional space.

Deleting the zero triangle and fixing the scale puts the pre-shape on the unit sphere of that 4-dimensional space, which is S^3, of dimension 3.

The rotation group \mathrm{SO}(2) has dimension 1. Removing it leaves dimension 3 - 1 = 2. ∎

Kendall (1984) proves that this quotient is a round sphere. Two triangles are compared only after translation, scale and rotation are declared irrelevant, so the comparison lives on that sphere rather than in \mathbb{R}^6. Geometry and shape spaces builds the same quotient for curves.

Deep Art, as a Euclidean distance on Gram matrices

Definition 1.4 — Feature Gram matrix

Let F be a feature array with C channels and P spatial positions, stored as a C \times P matrix. The Gram matrix G = F F^{\mathsf T} is the C \times C matrix of channel inner products.

Gatys, Ecker and Bethge (2016) move style inside VGG-19. Content loss is squared Euclidean distance on feature arrays. Style loss is the same distance on Gram matrices.

The Gram matrix does not see the order of the spatial positions. Style, in this definition, is co-occurrence of channels. Location has been removed, as rotation was removed for triangles. The geometry sits in the two losses.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • The corner cube [0, 1/2]^d has volume 2^{-d}. Local averages run out of neighbours.
  • A vector, a graph and a histogram keep different geometries. The official page asks you to choose.
  • Triangles in the plane, modulo translation, scale and rotation, form a 2-dimensional shape space. Kendall (1984) identifies it with a sphere.
  • Neural style is a squared Euclidean distance on Gram matrices of features. Location has been removed, co-occurrence remains.

2. Flat vector spaces

This chapter asks what the norm is doing inside trees, nearest neighbours, linear scores and kernels. The official lab fits the scikit-learn versions. The comparison is about invariance, not about a leaderboard.

Axis-aligned trees

Definition 2.1 — Stump

A stump on coordinate j at threshold t is the function that returns one label when x_j > t and the other label otherwise. A decision tree is a binary hierarchy of stumps.

Proposition 2.2 — What a rotation does

The Euclidean nearest-neighbour rule is unchanged if every point is rotated by the same orthogonal matrix. The class of stumps is not.

Proof. If Q is orthogonal, then \|Qx - Qy\| = \|x - y\|. The nearest neighbour of a query is therefore the image of the old nearest neighbour.

A stump has a boundary parallel to a coordinate hyperplane. Rotate the stump 1_{x_1 > 0} by 45 degrees. The boundary becomes a diagonal line. No axis-aligned stump has a diagonal boundary. ∎

A stump is readable when each coordinate is already a lab result, an age or a dose. It is a poor default on an image, where a rotation of the axes is an accident of the file.

Nearest neighbours, and the norm as the model

Definition 2.3 — Nearest neighbour

Given points x_1, \ldots, x_n with labels, the 1-nearest neighbour of a query q is any x_i that minimises a chosen distance d(q, x_i). The predicted label is the label of that point.

Cover and Hart (1967) studied the Euclidean case. Here the distance stays a parameter, which is the official question.

Example 2.4 — L1 and L2 disagree

Let q = (1, 0), a = (0, 0) and b = (0.5, 0.6). Then \|q - a\| = 1 and \|q - b\| = \sqrt{0.61} = 0.7810, so the Euclidean neighbour is b.

The L1 distances are |1| + |0| = 1 and 0.5 + 0.6 = 1.1, so the L1 neighbour is a. One query, two norms, two answers.

Linear scores

A linear score is s(x) = \langle w, x \rangle + b. The set where s vanishes is a hyperplane with normal w. For labels in \{0, 1\}, the logistic model passes s(x) through a sigmoid and uses the cross-entropy of Proposition 0.5. The gradient in the single logit is \sigma(s) - y.

Least squares on labels \pm 1 uses the squared loss. For two spherical classes of equal weight, the population fit is the perpendicular bisector of the class means, the same boundary as the nearest centroid. Outliers move the two losses apart. Exercise 6 prints that gap.

Networks, in one paragraph

A standard network composes maps x \mapsto \sigma(W x + c). Each layer picks coordinates and then applies a linear score. The geometry deepens only when the map becomes a convolution on a grid, a graph or a group, which is ch. 3. Deep learning, in the same semester, builds the architectures.

Kernel methods

Definition 2.5 — Kernel

A kernel is a function k such that every finite Gram matrix k(x_i, x_j) is symmetric and positive semidefinite. The Gaussian kernel with width \sigma > 0 is k(x, y) = \exp\big(-\|x - y\|^2 / (2\sigma^2)\big) .

Theorem 2.6 — The Gaussian kernel is positive semidefinite

For every \sigma > 0 the Gaussian kernel is a kernel in the sense of Definition 2.5.

Proof. Write the exponent as -\|x\|^2/(2\sigma^2) - \|y\|^2/(2\sigma^2) + \langle x, y \rangle / \sigma^2 .

The factors that depend only on x and only on y multiply a kernel by a function of each argument, which preserves positive semidefiniteness.

The series of \exp(\langle x, y \rangle / \sigma^2) is a sum of polynomial kernels \langle x, y \rangle^m, each of which is positive semidefinite, with nonnegative coefficients. A convergent sum of positive semidefinite Gram matrices is positive semidefinite. ∎

A kernel predictor is a linear score in the feature space of k. Schölkopf and Smola (2002) prove the representer theorem that writes the score as a finite sum of kernels. Kernel methods is the semester-2 course on that theorem.

The scikit-learn lab

The official lab fits a tree, a nearest neighbour, a logistic regression and an RBF support-vector classifier on one synthetic two-class set. In scikit-learn the estimators are DecisionTreeClassifier, KNeighborsClassifier, LogisticRegression and SVC. Draw the decision regions.

Rotate the coordinates. Proposition 2.2 says the tree changes and the Euclidean nearest neighbour does not. Exercise 6 is the NumPy version of the norm comparison.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • A stump is tied to the coordinate axes. A Euclidean nearest neighbour is not.
  • On q = (1, 0), a = (0, 0) and b = (0.5, 0.6), the L2 neighbour is b and the L1 neighbour is a.
  • Logistic regression is cross-entropy on a linear logit. Least squares is the squared loss. They agree for equal spherical classes and diverge otherwise.
  • The Gaussian kernel is positive semidefinite because it is a rescaled exponential of an inner product.

3. Graphs, embeddings and geometric deep learning

Flat norms fail in two ways. A high-dimensional Gaussian is a thin shell, so ambient neighbours are a sampling accident. A molecule or a lung is already a neighbourhood system, and a vector of coordinates forgets it. Lectures 3 and 4 of the official list share this chapter.

Gaussian shells

Theorem 3.1 — Gaussian shell

Let X have independent standard normal coordinates in \mathbb{R}^d. Then \mathbb{E}\|X\|^2 = d and \mathrm{Var}(\|X\|^2) = 2d. In particular \|X\| / \sqrt{d} converges to 1 in probability.

Proof. The second moment of one coordinate is 1, so the squared norm has mean d. The fourth moment is 3, so one squared coordinate has variance 2. Independence adds those variances and gives 2d. Chebyshev then forces the squared norm onto d, and the norm onto the square root of d. ∎

Example 3.2 — A shell of radius 10

In dimension 100 the predicted radius is 10 and the predicted width is about 0.707.

import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(4000, 100))
r = numpy.linalg.norm(X, axis=1)
print(f"{r.mean():.4f}")
print(f"{r.std():.4f}")

The block prints 9.9894 and then 0.6998. The syllabus calls this shell a bubble. Dimension 99 is still a large manifold.

Manifolds, dimension, curvature

Definition 3.3 — Manifold hypothesis

A law on \mathbb{R}^d satisfies a manifold hypothesis of dimension m when its mass concentrates on a tubular neighbourhood of an m-dimensional set, with m fixed while d grows.

White noise concentrates on a sphere of dimension d - 1, which still grows. A useful hypothesis needs a small m that stays fixed. The data then do not fill the shell.

Proposition 3.4 — Local rank

Suppose noiseless samples span an open piece of a smooth m-dimensional submanifold, and they are not contained in a smaller affine subspace. The covariance of the centered samples then has rank m.

Proof. In a tangent chart those samples form a full-dimensional cloud in an m-dimensional linear image of the tangent space. The empirical covariance is a Gram matrix of vectors in that m-plane, so it has exactly m positive eigenvalues. ∎

Local PCA reads the proposition. Count eigenvalues of a neighbourhood covariance above a threshold you state in advance. That count is the dimension on the official list.

Definition 3.5 — Ollivier curvature of an edge

On an unweighted connected graph, let m_x be the uniform law on the neighbours of x, and let d be the path metric. The Ollivier curvature of an edge xy is 1 - W_1(m_x, m_y) / d(x, y), where W_1 is the 1-Wasserstein distance of ch. 5.

Ollivier (2009) introduced this comparison. On the triangle, half the mass of m_a and m_b is shared and the other half travels distance 1, so W_1 = 1/2 and the curvature equals 1/2. A tree does the opposite. Neighbours of two adjacent vertices point away from the edge, and the curvature is negative.

A fuzzy graph, and the UMAP lab

McInnes, Healy and Melville (2018) match two fuzzy graphs by cross-entropy. Neighbour weights in the data decay with distance. Weights in the plane depend on \|y_i - y_j\|. The lab compares the embedding with principal components on a Swiss roll. The group project asks for that cross-entropy on a small roll.

Convolutions on graphs

Definition 3.6 — Normalized graph convolution

Let \tilde A be the adjacency matrix plus the identity, and let \tilde D be its diagonal matrix of degrees. With features H and a trainable matrix W, one convolution step is H' = \tilde D^{-1/2} \tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2} H W, optionally followed by a pointwise nonlinearity. This is the layer of Kipf and Welling (2017).

Example 3.7 — A path of three nodes

Take the path 1-2-3 with self-loops, degrees 2, 3 and 2, features 1, 0 and -1, and W = 1. The outputs are 1/2, 0 and -1/2. The middle node receives opposite masses and cancels. An oriented grid filter need not.

Five challenges

The lecture lists five obstructions that keep this layer from being a drop-in replacement for a grid convolution.

  • Orientation. Definition 3.6 averages neighbours and does not see a direction. The hairy ball theorem rules out a continuous global frame on the ordinary sphere. Cohen and Welling (2016) put the group into the filter.
  • Sampling density. A voxel and a mesh triangle do not carry the same amount of tissue. Sharp, Soliman and Crane (2019) resample a surface with intrinsic triangulations.
  • Non-Euclidean connectivity. Pooling a grid drops every other pixel. A clique and a bottleneck have no such step. Alon and Yahav (2021) show that a bottleneck squashes messages. Negative Ollivier curvature marks those edges.
  • Hardware. A grid neighbourhood is a contiguous read. A graph neighbourhood is a gather. The lecture slides put the slowdown near two orders of magnitude. ch. 5 turns that gap into a programming rule.
  • The range of targets. A molecule, a lidar scan and a social graph do not share a leaderboard. The lecture keeps the geometry explicit for that reason.

Docking, the lung, and a learned graph

Protein docking, as the lecture uses it, asks for a rigid pose of one molecule against another. The unknown is an element of \mathrm{SE}(3), and the loss looks at the interface. The graph is the contact graph of atoms.

Lung registration asks for a correspondence between two scans. A squared Euclidean distance on intensities can match a rib by sliding along it. Beg, Miller, Trouvé and Younes (2005) model the correspondence as a path of diffeomorphisms with a kinetic energy. ch. 4 is the geodesic language for that path.

Proposition 3.8 — Attention is a kernel average

In a transformer layer the weight of value v_j at query i is a softmax of the scaled inner products \langle q_i, k_j \rangle. The update is the corresponding weighted average of the values.

Proof. A softmax is positive and sums to 1, so the update is a convex combination of the values. The weight depends on the pair only through an inner product. Vaswani and coauthors (2017) compute query, key and value from the same tokens. ∎

The PyG lab, Fey and Lenssen (2019), checks Definition 3.6 against a dense NumPy product. Example 3.7 is that check on a path.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • A standard Gaussian has squared norm of mean d and variance 2d, hence a shell of radius sqrt(d).
  • Local PCA estimates dimension. Ollivier curvature of the triangle equals 1/2.
  • One graph convolution normalises the adjacency, then multiplies by a matrix W.
  • The five challenges are orientation, sampling, connectivity, GPU gathers, and benchmarks that do not travel.
  • Attention is a data-dependent kernel average. Docking lives in SE(3). Lung registration is a path of diffeomorphisms.

4. Riemannian metrics and geodesics

ch. 3 put a graph on a Euclidean cloud. Here the space itself carries the inner product. Length and geodesics follow, and the Poincaré disk is the first model in the lab.

Length

Definition 4.1 — Riemannian length

A Riemannian metric on a smooth manifold assigns an inner product \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_p to each tangent space at p, depending smoothly on p. The length of a curve \gamma is the integral of \|\gamma'(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}. A geodesic is a curve that locally minimises length.

The exponential map follows the geodesic that starts at a point with a given velocity, for unit time. The logarithm is the local inverse. It returns the velocity that points at a nearby point. do Carmo (1992) treats the geodesic equation. The course uses length, this pair of maps, and one negatively curved model.

The Poincaré disk

Definition 4.2 — Poincaré distance

The open unit disk carries the distance d(u, v) = \mathrm{acosh}\!\left(1 + \frac{2\|u - v\|^2}{(1 - \|u\|^2)(1 - \|v\|^2)}\right) . The Euclidean norm in the formula is the norm of the coordinate plane. The distance itself is not that norm.

Proposition 4.3 — Distance from the origin

If \|u\| = r < 1, then d(0, u) = \mathrm{acosh}((1 + r^2) / (1 - r^2)).

Proof. Substitute v = 0 in Definition 4.2. The fraction simplifies as (1 - r^2 + 2r^2) / (1 - r^2). ∎

Example 4.4 — Two radii

For r = 1/2 the argument of \mathrm{acosh} is 5/3, and the distance is 1.0986. For r = 0.9 the argument is 1 + 2 \cdot 0.81 / 0.19 and the distance is 2.9444.

The Euclidean distances are 0.5 and 0.9. The disk stretches the outer tenth of the radius much more than the first half. Hierarchies use that stretch.

Nickel and Kiela (2017) embed taxonomies in this disk with lower distortion than a Euclidean embedding of the same dimension. The Geomstats lab, Miolane, Guigui, Le Brigant and coauthors (2020), checks Definition 4.2 against Example 4.4. Exercise 9 asks for the same comparison on a tree.

Discrete paths and continuous paths

Proposition 4.5 — Dijkstra

On a finite directed graph with nonnegative edge weights, Dijkstra’s algorithm returns a shortest path from the source to every reachable vertex.

Proof. Vertices settle in order of a candidate distance. When v is settled, every edge leaving the settled set costs at least the remaining gap. A competing path must leave the settled set, and nonnegative weights stop a later return from repairing that cost. Dijkstra (1959) is the source. ∎

A graph geodesic is a Dijkstra path. A Riemannian geodesic is a curve. Use the graph when the object is a mesh or a molecule. Use the manifold when the object is a pose or a deformation, and the mesh is only a discretisation.

Means on a manifold

Proposition 4.6 — Critical point of the Fréchet function

Let x_1, \ldots, x_n lie in a normal ball around m, and let E(m) = \sum_i d(m, x_i)^2. If m minimises E in that ball, then \sum_i \log_m(x_i) = 0.

Proof. Move m along a geodesic with initial velocity v. The derivative of \tfrac12 d(m, x_i)^2 at t = 0 is -\langle v, \log_m(x_i) \rangle_m.

Sum over i and require every directional derivative to vanish. The inner product is nondegenerate, so the sum of the logarithms is the zero tangent vector. ∎

Pennec (2006) gives the global statement, including the cut locus. The lab iterates the fixed point. Average the logarithms, step with the exponential map, and repeat. Hierarchies, shape means and registration paths are the practical applications on the official list.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Length is the integral of the metric speed. A geodesic locally minimises it.
  • In the Poincaré disk, distance from the origin to radius r is \mathrm{acosh}((1+r^2)/(1-r^2)). The values at 1/2 and 0.9 are 1.0986 and 2.9444.
  • Dijkstra returns a shortest path when edge weights are nonnegative. It is the discrete geodesic.
  • A Fréchet mean in a normal ball is a point where the logarithms of the data sum to 0.

5. Distributions, transport and the GPU

Histograms, point clouds and masks are measures. This chapter replaces squared Euclidean distance on bin heights by a Fisher metric, a kernel norm or a transport cost, and records what a GPU can evaluate. Lectures 6 and 7 share the session.

Bins are not free

Let h and h’ be histograms on ordered bins. Squared Euclidean distance charges a move between neighbours the same as a move across the grid. For lung pixels that equality is false, so the geometry has to sit on the bins.

Information geometry

Definition 5.1 — Fisher metric

For a smooth family of laws p_\theta, the Fisher information at \theta is the variance of the score \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta. It is an inner product on the tangent space of the parameter. Amari and Nagaoka (2000) develop the resulting Riemannian geometry.

Proposition 5.2 — Bernoulli line element

For a Bernoulli law of parameter \theta \in (0, 1) the Fisher information is 1 / (\theta(1 - \theta)). The induced distance is d_F(\theta_0, \theta_1) = \big|2\arcsin\sqrt{\theta_1} - 2\arcsin\sqrt{\theta_0}\big| .

Proof. One observation has variance theta(1-theta), and the mean has derivative 1, so the information is the reciprocal. The line element is the absolute increment of theta divided by the square root of theta(1-theta).

Set u = sqrt(theta). The line element becomes the derivative of 2 arcsin(u). Integrate. ∎

Example 5.3 — 0.5 against 0.9

The Fisher-Rao distance from 0.5 to 0.9 is 0.9273. The absolute difference of the parameters is 0.4. The Fisher metric stretches the end of the interval, where neighbouring Bernoulli laws are far apart in sample size, and compresses the middle.

Kernel norms

Definition 5.4 — Mean embedding and MMD

For a kernel k, the mean embedding of a discrete measure \alpha = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i} is \sum_i a_i k(x_i, \cdot). The squared maximum mean discrepancy of \alpha and \beta is the squared kernel norm of the difference of embeddings.

Gretton, Borgwardt, Rasch, Schölkopf and Smola (2012) prove that a characteristic kernel metrizes laws. Wide kernels make nearby mass cheap. MMD does not build a matching.

Optimal transport

Definition 5.5 — Wasserstein cost

Let alpha and beta be discrete probabilities with ground cost c. The Wasserstein cost is the minimum expected cost over couplings. Villani (2009) and Peyré and Cuturi (2019) are the references.

Proposition 5.6 — Two Dirac masses

The p-Wasserstein distance between \delta_x and \delta_y, for ground cost \|x - y\|^p, equals \|x - y\|.

Proof. The only coupling of two Dirac masses is the Dirac mass on the pair (x, y). Its cost is \|x - y\|^p, and the distance is the p-th root. ∎

Entropic regularisation adds \varepsilon times the entropy of P to the cost. Cuturi (2013) solves that problem by matrix scaling. With K_{ij} = \exp(-c(x_i, y_j) / \varepsilon) and positive scaling vectors u and v, the coupling P = \mathrm{diag}(u)\, K\, \mathrm{diag}(v) is the form of the solution.

Proposition 5.7 — Marginals of a scaling

Let a and b be positive marginals. Define positive scalings by the entrywise ratios u = a/(Kv) and v = b/(K transpose u). Then P = diag(u) K diag(v) has row sums a and column sums b.

Proof. The i-th row sum is u_i (Kv)_i. Substitute u_i = a_i / (Kv)_i. The product is a_i.

The column sum is the same argument with K^{\mathsf T} and v. ∎

Key result — debiased Sinkhorn divergence S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) = \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) - \tfrac12 \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha) - \tfrac12 \mathrm{OT}_\varepsilon(\beta, \beta) .

Feydy, Séjourné, Vialard, Amari, Trouvé and Peyré (2019) remove the diagonal bias. For a positive definite kernel the cost runs from transport at small blur to an MMD at large blur. GeomLoss plots the curve. The solo project repeats it in NumPy.

What a GPU is, and three rules

A GPU runs many arithmetic units on one instruction stream. On current NVIDIA hardware that group is a warp of 32 threads. A CPU is built to finish one thread with low latency. A loop over an irregular neighbourhood fights that design.

Three rules cover the official lab list.

  • Read consecutive addresses. A graph gather does not, which is challenge 4 of ch. 3.
  • Keep a warp on one branch.
  • Prefer the matrix product in Proposition 0.6 to a handwritten loop over pairs.

The lecture slides illustrate irregular gathers running about two orders of magnitude slower than a contiguous read. Cast the distance as a product, and keep the neighbourhood contiguous when the object allows it.

Is Moore’s law coming to an end?

Moore (1965) projected a doubling of components per circuit, later on a two-year clock. CPU clocks stopped following that clock in the mid-2000s. Counts of transistors kept rising inside parallel hardware.

Density still grows. Single-thread speed does not. An irregular gather does not inherit the old annual speedup. It inherits the structure in Proposition 0.6.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Squared Euclidean distance on bin heights ignores the distance between bins.
  • On the Bernoulli family the Fisher-Rao distance is a difference of 2 \arcsin\sqrt{\theta}. From 0.5 to 0.9 it equals 0.9273, against an absolute gap of 0.4.
  • MMD compares mean embeddings. Wasserstein compares couplings. Sinkhorn scaling hits the marginals, and debiasing fixes the diagonal.
  • A GPU rewards contiguous reads and matrix products. Moore’s law still describes density better than it describes single-thread speed.

6. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is self-study, about 4 hours, outside the 21 hours. It records questions on which the constructions of ch. 3, ch. 4 and ch. 5 are still moving. The voice is that of the papers, not of a team.

Equivariance beyond a known group

Cohen and Welling (2016) build convolutional layers that commute with a chosen group. The docking case study needs \mathrm{SE}(3). Jumper and coauthors (2021) show how far an equivariant architecture can go on protein structure, with a network whose updates respect three-dimensional rotations. The open problem the lecture points at is different. The group is known for docking and unknown for a raw clinical table. Learning the symmetry, rather than typing it in, does not yet have a guarantee of the kind Theorem 2.6 gives for a kernel.

Bottlenecks and curvature

Alon and Yahav (2021) show that a narrow cut in a graph forces message-passing features to forget what happened on the other side. Ollivier curvature, Definition 3.5, is negative on the edges that feed such a cut. Topping, Di Giovanni, Chamberlain, Dong and Bronstein (2022) studied the same squeezing through curvature. Rewiring the graph to raise curvature can destroy the very bottleneck that carried the signal, for example an anatomical constriction. Which rewiring keeps the prediction and removes only the numerical squeeze is open.

Divergences with a known bias

Feydy, Séjourné, Vialard, Amari, Trouvé and Peyré (2019) debias the entropic transport cost. The interpolation between transport and MMD is a theorem for a fixed positive definite kernel. It is not a theorem about which blur matches a given noise model on a CT scanner. Choosing \varepsilon from data, with a finite-sample guarantee, is open. Unbalanced transport, in which mass can be created, is the version lung masks actually need, because the two scans do not show the same air.

Hyperbolic embeddings that remain stable

Nickel and Kiela (2017) and Ganea, Bécigneul and Hofmann (2018) use the Poincaré metric of ch. 4 for hierarchies. The numerical difficulty is at the boundary, where Example 4.4 shows the distance changing fast with the Euclidean radius. Optimisers that stay accurate for deep trees, and that report a distortion with a confidence interval, are not settled. A negative result would also be useful. Some trees do not embed in the disk with low distortion in two dimensions, and the obstruction should be visible before training.

Hardware, once the neighbourhood is learned

Proposition 3.8 writes attention as a kernel on a complete graph. The complete graph is contiguous in memory only after the implementation materialises it, which is impossible for a long sequence. Sparse attention reintroduces challenge 4 of ch. 3. The open systems question is whether a learned sparsity pattern can be compiled into contiguous reads, or whether every learned graph pays the gather. Moore’s law, as left open in ch. 5, does not answer it.

Questions to carry into the project

  • For which blur \varepsilon does the debiased Sinkhorn cost on two copies of the same lung mask fall below the noise level of the scanner, and how do you estimate that level?
  • Does a one-layer convolution of Definition 3.6 beat an MLP that sees the same node features and no edges, on a graph with a known bottleneck, by a margin that survives a change of split?
  • On a tree of depth 6, is the mean edge distortion in the Poincaré disk smaller than in \mathbb{R}^2, and does the gap survive a change of the optimiser?
  • Which of the five challenges in ch. 3 is the one your project actually hits, and which theorem of these notes is the baseline you compare with?

Exercises

Levels: ★ apply the course, ★★ combine two results or write a short proof, ★★★ open-ended. Exercises 2 and 6 are computational. The printed numbers in the solutions come from the fences.

  1. ★ Two losses, opposite neighbours. Let p = (0.9, 0.1), q = (0.8, 0.2) and r = (0.99, 0.01).

    Compute the squared Euclidean distances from p to q and from p to r, and both cross-entropies of p. Use log(0.8) = -0.22314, log(0.2) = -1.60944, log(0.99) = -0.01005 and log(0.01) = -4.60517.

    Solution. The squared distances are 2 \times 0.1^2 = 0.0200 and 2 \times 0.09^2 = 0.0162. Squared Euclidean distance prefers r.

    The cross-entropy relative to q is 0.9 \times 0.22314 + 0.1 \times 1.60944 = 0.3618. Relative to r it is 0.9 \times 0.01005 + 0.1 \times 4.60517 = 0.4696. Cross-entropy prefers q. This is Example 0.4.

  2. ★ Nearest neighbours in dimension 10. Draw 40 standard Gaussian points in \mathbb{R}^{10} with the seed below. Report the mean distance from each point to its nearest other point, and the mean distance to the origin.

    import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(40, 10))
G = X @ X.T
sq = numpy.diag(G)
d2 = sq[:, None] + sq[None, :] - 2.0 * G
numpy.fill_diagonal(d2, numpy.inf)
nn = numpy.sqrt(numpy.maximum(d2, 0.0).min(axis=1))
print(f"{nn.mean():.4f}")
print(f"{numpy.linalg.norm(X, axis=1).mean():.4f}")

    Solution. The fence prints 2.6600 and then 3.0776. The neighbour is not much closer than the origin. Proposition 0.6 is the expansion in the third line of arithmetic. ch. 1 and Theorem 3.1 explain why the gap shrinks further as the dimension grows.

  3. ★ Corners of the cube. Compute the volume of [0, 1/2]^d for d = 10 and d = 20. Say what the two volumes imply for a local average that stays inside the corner.

    Solution. Proposition 1.1 gives 2^{-10} = 1/1024 and 2^{-20} = 1/1048576. A sample of a few hundred points has an empty corner at d = 10 and a still emptier one at d = 20. The average is then an average over nothing.

  4. ★ A shape dimension and a Gram matrix. Count the dimension of the shape space of a labeled triangle in the plane, after translation, scale and rotation. Then let F be the 2 \times 2 feature matrix with rows (1, 0) and (1, 1). Compute the Gram matrix F F^{\mathsf T}.

    Solution. Proposition 1.3 gives dimension 2. Six coordinates, minus 2 for the centroid, minus 1 for scale, minus 1 for rotation.

    The Gram matrix is [[1, 1], [1, 2]]. The off-diagonal entry is the inner product of the two channel rows, 1 \times 1 + 0 \times 1 = 1.

  5. ★★ Rotation. Prove that a Euclidean 1-nearest-neighbour rule is unchanged by a common orthogonal transformation of the points. Prove that the class of stumps is not closed under rotation.

    Solution. If Q is orthogonal, \|Qx - Qy\| = \|x - y\|, so neighbours are preserved. This is the first half of Proposition 2.2.

    The stump 1_{x_1 > 0} has an axis-aligned boundary. After a rotation by 45 degrees the boundary is diagonal. No stump has a diagonal boundary, so the rotated function is not a stump.

  6. ★★ Two norms, with outliers. Forty points in the plane form two classes of 20. Class 1 is shifted by 1.2 along the first axis. The first three points, which belong to class 0, are shifted by 6 along the second axis. The fence prints the leave-one-out error of the 1-nearest neighbour, Euclidean norm first and L1 norm second.

    import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
n = 40
y = numpy.repeat(numpy.array([0.0, 1.0]), n // 2)
X = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))
X[y == 1, 0] += 1.2
X[:3, 1] += 6.0
diff = X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]
d2 = (diff ** 2).sum(axis=-1)
d1 = numpy.abs(diff).sum(axis=-1)
numpy.fill_diagonal(d2, numpy.inf)
numpy.fill_diagonal(d1, numpy.inf)
pred2 = y[numpy.argmin(d2, axis=1)]
pred1 = y[numpy.argmin(d1, axis=1)]
print(f"{float((pred2 != y).mean()):.4f}")
print(f"{float((pred1 != y).mean()):.4f}")

    Solution. The fence prints 0.2750 and then 0.3000. Changing the norm changes the error by 0.025 on this sample. Example 2.4 is the same disagreement with three hand-computed points and no sample.

  7. ★★ Shell moments. Let X have independent standard normal coordinates in \mathbb{R}^d. Prove that \mathbb{E}\|X\|^2 = d and \mathrm{Var}(\|X\|^2) = 2d.

    Solution.

    • Each coordinate has second moment 1, so the squared norm has mean d.
    • The fourth moment of a standard normal is 3, so one squared coordinate has variance 3 - 1 = 2.
    • Independence adds the variances and gives 2d. This is Theorem 3.1 without the passage to the norm.

  8. ★★ A path convolution and a triangle. On the path of Example 3.7, with features 1, 0, -1 and W = 1, compute the three output coordinates. On the complete graph K_3, with m_x uniform on the two neighbours, compute the Ollivier curvature of an edge.

    Solution. The outputs are 1/2, 0 and -1/2. The middle node receives equal opposite masses and cancels.

    On the triangle, W_1 = 1/2 because half the mass is already shared and the other half travels distance 1. Definition 3.5 then gives curvature 1 - (1/2)/1 = 1/2.

  9. ★★★ Distortion in the disk. Using Proposition 4.3, compute the Poincaré distance from the origin to Euclidean radii 1/2 and 0.9. Then take a balanced tree of depth 4 and branching factor 2. Compare the mean edge distortion of a 2-dimensional Euclidean embedding with a Poincaré embedding. Distortion of an edge is embedded distance divided by graph distance.

    Hints and expected result. The two distances are \mathrm{acosh}(5/3) = 1.0986 and 2.9444, as in Example 4.4. A full-mark comparison reports both mean distortions on the same tree, with the same optimiser budget, and says which one is smaller. Nickel and Kiela (2017) found the disk smaller on hierarchies. A tie, or a win for the plane, needs a sentence on the depth and the dimension. Chapters 4 and 6.

  10. ★★★ A distance for two masks. Compute the Fisher-Rao distance of Proposition 5.2 between Bernoulli parameters 0.5 and 0.9, and compare it with |0.9 - 0.5|. Then consider two lung masks as measures on the pixel grid. Say which object you would fit, a squared Euclidean distance on intensities, a Wasserstein cost, or a diffeomorphic geodesic, and what failure each choice allows.

    Hints and expected result. The Fisher-Rao distance is 0.9273 and the absolute gap is 0.4, Example 5.3. Squared Euclidean distance on intensities permits a slide along a rib. Wasserstein moves mass but does not know a deformation of the tissue. A geodesic in a space of diffeomorphisms, Beg, Miller, Trouvé and Younes (2005), charges that slide only through the metric. A full-mark answer names the failure mode of the chosen object and the chapter that defines it.
