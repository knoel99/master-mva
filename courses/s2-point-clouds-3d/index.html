The course follows the whole chain from the sensor to the model. It explains how 3D points are acquired (ch. 1), which local operators extract normals, curvature and descriptors (ch. 2), how two scans are aligned (ch. 3), how a cloud is cut into meaningful parts (ch. 4), fitted with geometric primitives (ch. 5), turned into a surface (ch. 6) and drawn on a screen (ch. 7).

0. Review: geometry, linear algebra and probability for 3D data

Every later chapter moves points with rigid motions, extracts directions from small covariance matrices, solves least-squares problems and counts the chances of random draws. This review states these tools once, with the proofs that later chapters rely on.

Definition 0.1 — Point cloud A point cloud is a finite family \mathcal{X} = (x_1, \dots, x_n), \qquad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^3, stored without any order or connectivity.

Each point may carry attributes: an intensity (the strength of the returned signal), a colour, a timestamp, a return number, a normal n_i or a label.

We write X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 3} for the matrix whose rows are the points. Any algorithm on a cloud must give the same answer, up to the same reordering, when the rows of X are permuted.

Definition 0.2 — Rigid motions The special orthogonal group is SO(3) = \{ R \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3} : R^\top R = I,\ \det R = 1 \}.

A rigid motion is a map x \mapsto R x + t with R \in SO(3) and t \in \mathbb{R}^3. The rigid motions form the group SE(3).

In homogeneous coordinates a point is (x, 1) and the motion is the 4 \times 4 matrix T = \begin{pmatrix} R & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} R^\top & -R^\top t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

Composition becomes a matrix product, which is how a chain of sensor frames is handled in ch. 1. A rigid motion has six degrees of freedom: three for the rotation, three for the translation.

For a unit vector k, let [k]_\times be the matrix of the cross product, so that [k]_\times v = k \times v: [k]_\times = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -k_3 & k_2 \\ k_3 & 0 & -k_1 \\ -k_2 & k_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 0.3 — Rodrigues’ formula and the angle of a rotation The rotation of angle \theta about the unit axis k is R = I + \sin\theta\, [k]_\times + (1 - \cos\theta)\, [k]_\times^2 .

Conversely, every R \in SO(3) is a rotation about some axis, and its angle satisfies \cos\theta = \frac{\operatorname{tr} R - 1}{2}.

Proof. Split a vector v into its component v_\parallel = (k^\top v) k along the axis and v_\perp = v - v_\parallel.

The rotation keeps v_\parallel and turns v_\perp by \theta in the plane spanned by v_\perp and k \times v. Hence R v = v_\parallel + \cos\theta\, v_\perp + \sin\theta\, k \times v .

Since [k]_\times^2 = k k^\top - I, we have v_\perp = -[k]_\times^2 v, and the formula follows by regrouping.

For the trace: \operatorname{tr}[k]_\times = 0 and the trace of [k]_\times^2 equals -2, so the trace of R is 3 - 2(1 - \cos\theta) = 1 + 2\cos\theta.

For the converse, the eigenvalues of R have modulus 1, come in conjugate pairs and multiply to 1, so 1 is an eigenvalue; its eigenvector is the axis. ∎

Small rotations are the workhorse of iterative registration (ch. 3). For a rotation vector \omega = \theta k with small \theta, Rodrigues’ formula gives R \approx I + [\omega]_\times , \qquad R x \approx x + \omega \times x .

Theorem 0.4 — Spectral theorem and Rayleigh quotient A real symmetric matrix C \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors u_1, \dots, u_d with real eigenvalues \lambda_1 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_d. Moreover \min_{\|n\| = 1} n^\top C n = \lambda_d, \qquad \max_{\|n\| = 1} n^\top C n = \lambda_1, attained at n = u_d and n = u_1 respectively.

Proof. The decomposition C = U \Lambda U^\top is the spectral theorem of the refresher on linear algebra.

Write n = U a; since U is orthogonal, \|a\| = \|n\| = 1. Then n^\top C n = a^\top \Lambda a = \sum_{j} \lambda_j a_j^2 .

This is a convex combination of the eigenvalues with weights a_j^2 summing to 1. It is therefore at least \lambda_d and at most \lambda_1, with equality for a = e_d and a = e_1. ∎

This theorem is behind the normal of a plane fitted to points (ch. 2), the plane primitive (ch. 5) and the covariance descriptors (ch. 2).

Proposition 0.5 — Least squares and the SVD Every M \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d} has a singular value decomposition M = U \Sigma V^\top with U, V orthogonal and \Sigma diagonal with non-negative entries \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots.

For A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d} of full column rank and b \in \mathbb{R}^m, the problem \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d} \|A x - b\|^2 has the unique solution given by the normal equations A^\top A\, x^\star = A^\top b .

Proof. The objective is a convex quadratic with gradient 2 A^\top (A x - b). Full column rank makes A^\top A positive definite, so the only stationary point is the global minimum.

The SVD is the spectral theorem applied to M^\top M: its eigenvectors give V, the square roots of its eigenvalues give \Sigma, and U collects the normalised vectors M v_j / \sigma_j. ∎

In practice one calls a QR- or SVD-based solver (np.linalg.lstsq) rather than forming A^\top A, whose condition number is the square of that of A.

Proposition 0.6 — Propagation of covariance If a random vector x has mean \mu and covariance \Sigma, then y = A x + b has mean A \mu + b and covariance \operatorname{Cov}(y) = A\, \Sigma\, A^\top .

For a smooth map y = g(x) with small noise, the first-order approximation uses the Jacobian J = Dg(\mu): \operatorname{Cov}(y) \approx J\, \Sigma\, J^\top .

Proof. y - \mathbb{E} y = A (x - \mu), so the covariance is the expectation of the outer product, which equals A \Sigma A^\top. The second statement applies the first to the Taylor expansion of g at \mu, neglecting the second-order terms. ∎

Chapter 1 uses this rule to turn errors on ranges and angles into errors on points.

Proposition 0.7 — Repeated independent trials If each of N independent trials succeeds with probability q > 0, the probability that at least one succeeds is 1 - (1 - q)^N. It exceeds a target p as soon as N \geq \frac{\log(1 - p)}{\log(1 - q)} .

Proof. All trials fail with probability (1 - q)^N by independence. The inequality 1 - (1 - q)^N \geq p is equivalent to N \log(1 - q) \leq \log(1 - p); dividing by the negative number \log(1 - q) reverses it. ∎

This is the counting argument of RANSAC (ch. 3, ch. 5), where a trial succeeds when a random minimal sample contains only inliers.

Code 0.8 — Rotations in practice The code builds a rotation of 60° about the axis (1, 1, 0), checks that it lies in SO(3) and recovers its angle from the trace. It then perturbs it and projects it back onto SO(3) with the SVD, a step proved in ch. 3 (Theorem 3.2).

import numpy as np

def rodrigues(axis, theta):
    """Rotation of angle theta about the unit axis k (Rodrigues' formula)."""
    k = np.asarray(axis, float) / np.linalg.norm(axis)
    K = np.array([[0, -k[2], k[1]], [k[2], 0, -k[0]], [-k[1], k[0], 0]])
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(theta) * K + (1 - np.cos(theta)) * K @ K

R = rodrigues([1, 1, 0], np.pi / 3)
print(np.round(R, 4))
print("orthogonal:", np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(3)), "| det =", round(np.linalg.det(R), 6))
print("angle from the trace (deg):", round(np.degrees(np.arccos((np.trace(R) - 1) / 2)), 4))

# Nearest rotation to a noisy matrix: projection onto SO(3) by the SVD
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
M = R + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal((3, 3))
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M)
S = np.diag([1, 1, np.sign(np.linalg.det(U @ Vt))])
R_hat = U @ S @ Vt
print("||M - R||_F =", round(np.linalg.norm(M - R), 4),
      "| ||R_hat - R||_F =", round(np.linalg.norm(R_hat - R), 4))

The output shows the matrix with entries 0.75, 0.25 and \pm 0.6124, an orthogonal matrix of determinant 1 and an angle of exactly 60°.

The projection reduces the error: \|M - R\|_F = 0.0996 \ \longrightarrow\ \|\hat R - R\|_F = 0.069 .

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A point cloud is an unordered family of points with attributes; algorithms must not depend on the order.
  • Rigid motions x \mapsto Rx + t have six degrees of freedom; small rotations are I + [\omega]_\times to first order.
  • The extreme values of n^\top C n on the unit sphere are the extreme eigenvalues of C (Theorem 0.4).
  • Least squares are solved by the normal equations, numerically by QR or the SVD.
  • Covariances propagate through linear maps as A \Sigma A^\top, and through smooth maps through their Jacobian.
  • N independent trials of success probability q all fail with probability (1-q)^N.

1. 3D perception systems

Before processing points we must know where they come from. This chapter describes the main families of 3D sensors, derives the accuracy and the sampling density of each, and lists the artefacts that later chapters have to survive.

Sensors fall into two physical principles. Triangulation measures an angle seen from two places a known distance apart. Time of flight measures how long light takes to go to the surface and back.

Both can be passive (cameras only, as in photogrammetry and multi-view stereo) or active (the sensor emits a laser stripe, a projected pattern or light pulses). Active sensors work on textureless surfaces and in the dark, which is why they dominate 3D scanning.

Definition 1.1 — Triangulation Two rectified pinhole cameras (or a camera and a projector) with focal length f (in pixels) are separated by a baseline b along their common x axis.

A point at depth z appears at horizontal image positions u_L and u_R; the disparity is d = u_L - u_R. Similar triangles give z = \frac{f\, b}{d} .

Structured-light sensors replace the second camera by a projector whose pattern encodes the column u_R; laser-stripe scanners project a single plane of light.

Proposition 1.2 — Depth error of triangulation An error \delta d on the disparity produces, to first order, a depth error \delta z \approx \frac{z^2}{f\, b}\, \delta d .

The error grows with the square of the distance and decreases with the baseline.

Proof. Differentiate z = f b / d with respect to d: \frac{\partial z}{\partial d} = -\frac{f b}{d^2} = -\frac{z^2}{f b}, using d = f b / z. Multiply by \delta d and take absolute values. ∎

Example 1.3 — A depth camera at 1 m and at 4 m Take f = 600 px, a baseline b = 7.5 cm and a disparity accuracy of 0.1 px, typical orders of magnitude for a structured-light depth camera.

  • At z = 1 m: \delta z \approx 1 \times 0.1 / (600 \times 0.075) = 0.0022 m.
  • At z = 4 m the error is 16 times larger.

The two results are \delta z(1\ \text{m}) \approx 2.2\ \text{mm}, \qquad \delta z(4\ \text{m}) \approx 3.6\ \text{cm}.

Triangulation is excellent at short range and quickly degrades: for large scenes one switches to time of flight.

Definition 1.4 — Time of flight With c the speed of light:

  • a pulsed sensor measures the round-trip time \tau of a short laser pulse, and r = \frac{c\, \tau}{2};
  • a phase-shift (amplitude-modulated) sensor emits light modulated at frequency f_m and measures the phase delay \Delta\varphi of the return, and r = \frac{c\, \Delta\varphi}{4 \pi f_m} \quad \text{modulo the ambiguity interval } \frac{c}{2 f_m}.

A timing resolution of 100 ps gives a range resolution of c \times 10^{-10} / 2 = 1.5 cm. With f_m = 10 MHz the ambiguity interval is 15 m, and a phase resolution of 0.1° gives about 4.2 mm.

Phase-shift sensors are precise at short and medium range; pulsed sensors reach hundreds of metres. Several modulation frequencies are combined to remove the ambiguity.

Time of flight gives the range along a known direction. A laser scanner sweeps that direction with rotating mirrors or a rotating head and returns, for every shot, a range r, an azimuth \theta and an elevation \varphi.

Definition 1.5 — Scanning geometry In the frame of the scanner, the shot (r, \theta, \varphi) is the point x = r \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi \cos\theta \\ \cos\varphi \sin\theta \\ \sin\varphi \end{pmatrix}.

A terrestrial scanner stands still on a tripod and sweeps both angles finely. A rotating multi-beam LiDAR has a fixed fan of K beams at elevations \varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_K and spins in azimuth. Its scan is a K \times M range image whose rows are the beams.

The range image is a useful by-product: neighbours in the image are usually neighbours in space, which gives cheap normals and segmentation on raw scans.

Proposition 1.6 — Sampling density Consider shots separated by an angle \Delta\alpha that hit a surface at range r with incidence angle \beta (between the beam and the surface normal). The distance between neighbouring points is about s \approx \frac{r\, \Delta\alpha}{\cos\beta} .

For a sensor at height h above a flat ground, the beam of depression angle \varphi > 0 hits the ground on a ring of radius \rho(\varphi) = h / \tan\varphi. Two beams \Delta\varphi apart give rings separated by \Delta\rho \approx \frac{h\, \Delta\varphi}{\sin^2 \varphi} .

Proof. Two beams \Delta\alpha apart are r \Delta\alpha apart at range r, measured orthogonally to the beam. On a surface tilted by \beta this distance is stretched by 1 / \cos\beta.

For the rings, differentiate \rho(\varphi) = h \cot\varphi: \rho'(\varphi) = -\frac{h}{\sin^2\varphi} . ∎

Density therefore falls like 1/r^2 and collapses at grazing incidence: the ground far from a vehicle is sampled by a few sparse rings. Neighbourhoods defined by a fixed number of points (ch. 2) then have very different sizes across the scene.

Code 1.7 — A simulated scan of a street A 16-beam scanner with elevations from −15° to +15° (2° apart) and an azimuth step of 0.2° stands 2 m above the ground, in front of a wall and a sphere. Rays are intersected with the three objects, and 2 cm of Gaussian noise is added to the ranges.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# A 16-beam rotating scanner, 2 m above a flat ground, in front of a wall and a sphere.
h = 2.0
elev = np.radians(np.linspace(-15, 15, 16))           # beam elevations
azim = np.radians(np.arange(0, 360, 0.2))             # azimuth step 0.2 degrees
E, A = np.meshgrid(elev, azim, indexing="ij")
D = np.stack([np.cos(E) * np.cos(A), np.cos(E) * np.sin(A), np.sin(E)], -1).reshape(-1, 3)
o = np.array([0.0, 0.0, h])

def hit_plane(n, c):          # plane n.x = c ; returns ray parameter t (inf if missed)
    den = D @ n
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        t = (c - o @ n) / den
    return np.where((den != 0) & (t > 0), t, np.inf)

def hit_sphere(center, rad):
    oc = o - center
    b = D @ oc
    disc = b**2 - (oc @ oc - rad**2)
    t = -b - np.sqrt(np.maximum(disc, 0))
    return np.where((disc > 0) & (t > 0), t, np.inf)

t = np.minimum.reduce([hit_plane(np.array([0, 0, 1.0]), 0.0),     # ground z = 0
                       hit_plane(np.array([1.0, 0, 0]), 12.0),     # wall x = 12
                       hit_sphere(np.array([5.0, 3.0, 1.0]), 1.0)])
keep = t < 100.0                                                   # maximal range 100 m
r = t[keep] + 0.02 * rng.standard_normal(keep.sum())               # range noise, 2 cm
P = o + r[:, None] * D[keep]
print("rays:", len(D), "| returns:", len(P))

# Ground rings: beam k hits the ground at horizontal distance h / tan(-elevation)
down = -elev[elev < 0]
rings = h / np.tan(down)
print("ring radii (m):", np.round(np.sort(rings), 2))
near = np.sort(rings)[np.sort(rings) < 40]
print("gaps between consecutive rings within 40 m (m):", np.round(np.diff(near), 2))

Of the 28,800 rays, 20,073 return a point; the others go to the sky. The eight downward beams draw rings of radii 7.46,\ 8.66,\ 10.29,\ 12.63,\ 16.29,\ 22.86,\ 38.16,\ 114.58\ \text{m}.

The last ring lies beyond the 100 m range. Within 40 m the gaps between rings are 1.2, 1.63, 2.34, 3.66, 6.57 and 15.3 m, while along each ring the points are only 7.5 \times 0.0035 \approx 2.6 cm apart at 7.5 m: the sampling is extremely anisotropic.

Static scans cover one viewpoint. Mobile mapping puts the scanner on a vehicle, a backpack or a drone and places every shot in a world frame, using a satellite receiver (GNSS) and an inertial measurement unit (IMU) whose fusion gives the pose of the platform at each instant.

Definition 1.8 — Georeferencing a mobile scan Let x_s be a point in the scanner frame, measured at time t. Let (R_{bs}, l_{bs}) be the fixed rotation (boresight) and offset (lever arm) from the scanner to the platform body, found by calibration. Let (R(t), p(t)) be the pose of the body in the world. Then x_w = p(t) + R(t) \big( R_{bs}\, x_s + l_{bs} \big).

Each point gets its own pose, interpolated at its own timestamp: a scan taken while moving is not a rigid copy of the scene.

Proposition 1.9 — Error budget of a mobile point To first order, the error on x_w is the sum of:

  • the position error \delta p of the platform, transferred unchanged;
  • the attitude error \delta\psi (in radians), which moves a point at range r by about r\, \delta\psi;
  • the timing error \delta t, which moves it by v\, \delta t at speed v;
  • the range and angle noise of the sensor, whose covariance is J \Sigma J^\top with J the Jacobian of the map of Definition 1.5 (Proposition 0.6).

Proof. Linearise the formula of Definition 1.8. A small rotation error R(t) \to (I + [\delta\psi]_\times) R(t) adds \delta\psi \times (x_w - p), of norm at most r \|\delta\psi\|. A time offset shifts p(t) by \dot p\, \delta t. The sensor term is Proposition 0.6 applied to the spherical map. ∎

With a heading error of 0.01° (1.75 \times 10^{-4} rad), a point at 50 m moves by 8.7 mm. At 10 m/s, a synchronisation error of 1 ms moves every point by 1 cm.

These numbers explain why mobile maps are refined by registering overlapping passes (ch. 3) rather than trusted from GNSS and IMU alone, which can drift by decimetres when satellites are masked by buildings.

Note 1.10 — Artefacts every pipeline must expect

  • Mixed points at depth discontinuities: the beam footprint straddles a foreground edge and the background, and the sensor returns a point floating in between.
  • Specular and transparent surfaces (glass, water, polished metal) return nothing or a mirrored ghost; very dark surfaces return little energy and become noisy.
  • Multiple returns: in vegetation one pulse produces several echoes, a feature exploited by airborne LiDAR to see the ground under trees.
  • Motion distortion: a rotating scanner on a moving vehicle typically takes 0.1 s per turn, enough to bend a scan unless each point is corrected with its own pose.
  • Uneven density and occlusions, as computed above.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Triangulation: z = fb/d, depth error growing like z^2; precise at short range.
  • Time of flight: r = c\tau/2 (pulsed) or from a phase shift with an ambiguity interval c / (2 f_m).
  • A scanner returns spherical coordinates; point spacing grows like r / \cos\beta, so density is very uneven.
  • Mobile mapping composes the sensor pose, the boresight and the lever arm; attitude errors scale with range.
  • Expect mixed points, specular gaps, multiple echoes, motion distortion and occlusions.

2. Processing and operators

A raw cloud is a list of coordinates. To reason about surfaces we need, for every point, its neighbours and the local shape they describe. This chapter builds neighbourhoods efficiently, thins clouds, and computes normals, curvature and descriptors, with attention to the choice of scale.

Neighbourhoods and spatial data structures

Definition 2.1 — Neighbourhoods For a query point x and a cloud \mathcal{X}:

  • the k-nearest neighbourhood \mathcal{N}_k(x) is the set of the k points of \mathcal{X} closest to x;
  • the ball neighbourhood of radius r is \mathcal{N}_r(x) = \{ x_i \in \mathcal{X} : \|x_i - x\| \leq r \}.

A k-neighbourhood has a fixed size but a variable extent: it is tiny in dense areas and huge on sparse ground rings. A ball has a fixed extent, hence a fixed geometric meaning, but may contain one point or a hundred thousand.

Brute force costs O(n) per query, hence O(n^2) for all points: hopeless for clouds of 10^7 points. Spatial data structures reduce this cost.

Proposition 2.2 — The kd-tree A kd-tree (Bentley, 1975) splits the cloud recursively: at each node, choose a coordinate axis, cut at the median coordinate, and recurse on both halves until a leaf holds a few points.

  • Its depth is at most \lceil \log_2 n \rceil and it is built in O(n \log n) time.
  • A nearest-neighbour query descends to the leaf of x, then backtracks, visiting a sibling only if the ball around x of radius “best distance so far” crosses the splitting plane.

Proof. Cutting at the median halves the number of points at each level, which gives the depth.

A median is found in linear time by a selection algorithm, so the construction time satisfies T(n) = 2\, T(n/2) + O(n), whose solution is T(n) = O(n \log n): each of the \log_2 n levels costs O(n) in total.

The pruning rule is exact because every point beyond the splitting plane is farther from x than the plane itself. ∎

For points spread in low dimension, Friedman, Bentley and Finkel (1977) showed that the expected query time is logarithmic. In 3D this makes all k-neighbourhoods of a cloud computable in roughly O(n \log n).

Two other structures are common. An octree cuts space (not the points) into eight cubes recursively; it is the natural structure for multi-resolution processing and rendering (ch. 7). A voxel hash maps each point to the integer cell \lfloor x / s \rfloor; a ball query of radius r \le s then reads the 27 cells around the query.

Subsampling

Clouds are often far denser than needed, and denser near the sensor than far away. Subsampling reduces the cost and evens out the density.

Definition 2.3 — Subsampling schemes

  • Random: keep each point with probability q. Cheap, but it keeps the density imbalance.
  • Voxel grid: cut space into cubes of side s and keep one representative per occupied cube (the barycentre or the point closest to it). The result has spacing about s everywhere.
  • Poisson disk: keep a subset in which any two points are at least \delta apart, and which is maximal for this property.
  • Farthest point sampling (FPS): start from any point, then repeatedly add the point farthest from the points already chosen.

FPS is used inside PointNet++ (ch. 4) and has a clean guarantee. The k-center problem asks for k centres c_1, \dots, c_k minimising the covering radius \rho(C) = \max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \min_{j} \|x - c_j\| .

Proposition 2.4 — FPS is a 2-approximation of k-center Let C be the first k points chosen by FPS and \rho^\star the optimal covering radius with k centres. Then \rho(C) \leq 2 \rho^\star .

Proof. Let x^\ast be the point farthest from C, at distance \rho(C). It would be the (k+1)-st FPS point.

By construction, every FPS point was chosen at distance at least \rho(C) from the previous ones (the farthest distances never increase). So the k + 1 points C \cup \{x^\ast\} are pairwise at distance at least \rho(C).

An optimal solution has k balls of radius \rho^\star, so two of these k + 1 points fall in the same ball (pigeonhole). Their distance is at most 2\rho^\star, hence \rho(C) \le 2 \rho^\star. ∎

Normals by local PCA

Most operators need the tangent plane of the underlying surface at each point. It is estimated by fitting a plane to the neighbourhood.

Let \mathcal{N} be a neighbourhood of m points, with centroid and covariance \bar x = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{x_i \in \mathcal{N}} x_i, \qquad C = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{x_i \in \mathcal{N}} (x_i - \bar x)(x_i - \bar x)^\top .

Theorem 2.5 — The best-fitting plane Among all planes \{x : n^\top (x - c) = 0\} with \|n\| = 1, the sum of squared distances E(n, c) = \sum_{x_i \in \mathcal{N}} \big( n^\top (x_i - c) \big)^2 is minimised by a plane through the centroid, c = \bar x, whose normal is an eigenvector of C for its smallest eigenvalue \lambda_3. The minimal value is m \lambda_3.

Proof. For fixed n, E depends on c only through d = n^\top c and equals the sum of the squares of n^\top x_i - d.

This is minimised by the mean value d = n^\top \bar x, so the optimal plane passes through \bar x.

Then E(n, \bar x) = \sum_i n^\top (x_i - \bar x)(x_i - \bar x)^\top n = m\, n^\top C n , and Theorem 0.4 shows that its minimum over unit vectors is m \lambda_3, at the eigenvector of \lambda_3. ∎

Key result — normal by PCA n(x) = \text{eigenvector of } \operatorname{Cov}\big(\mathcal{N}(x)\big) \text{ for its smallest eigenvalue}

The eigenvector is defined up to sign. A consistent orientation is needed for shading (ch. 7) and for Poisson reconstruction (ch. 6).

Proposition 2.6 — Orienting normals

  • Towards the sensor: when the scanner position o is known, flip n_i so that n_i^\top (o - x_i) > 0. This is exact for every point seen from a single station.
  • By propagation (Hoppe et al., 1992): build a neighbourhood graph with edge weights 1 - |n_i^\top n_j|, compute a minimum spanning tree, orient one seed (for example the highest point, whose normal points up) and traverse the tree, flipping n_j when n_i^\top n_j < 0.

The weights favour edges between nearly parallel normals, along which the sign is unambiguous. Propagation still fails across thin structures, where both sides of a sheet share neighbours.

Curvature and the choice of scale

The other two eigenvalues measure how far the neighbourhood is from a plane.

Definition 2.7 — Surface variation With the eigenvalues \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \lambda_3 \geq 0 of the local covariance, the surface variation (Pauly, Gross & Kobbelt, 2002) is \sigma(x) = \frac{\lambda_3}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3} \in \left[0, \tfrac{1}{3}\right].

It is 0 on a plane and 1/3 for isotropically scattered points. It behaves like a curvature, but depends on the neighbourhood size.

The next computation makes that dependence explicit and gives a rule for choosing the radius.

Proposition 2.8 — Curvature, noise and the optimal radius Near a point where the surface is locally z = \frac{\kappa}{2}(x^2 + y^2), take the points of a disk of radius r uniformly, and add Gaussian noise of standard deviation \sigma along the normal. To leading order \frac{\lambda_3}{\lambda_1} \approx \frac{\kappa^2 r^2}{12} + \frac{4 \sigma^2}{r^2}.

The right-hand side is minimal for r^\star = 48^{1/4} \sqrt{\sigma / \kappa} .

Proof. For a uniform point of the disk, \rho^2 = x^2 + y^2 is uniform on [0, r^2], so \mathbb{E}[x^2] = \frac{r^2}{4}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\rho^2) = \frac{r^4}{12} .

Hence \lambda_1 \approx r^2/4, and the variance along the normal is that of \frac{\kappa}{2}\rho^2 plus the noise: \lambda_3 \approx \frac{\kappa^2 r^4}{48} + \sigma^2 .

Divide, then set the derivative 2ar - 2b/r^3 to zero, with a = \kappa^2/12 and b = 4\sigma^2. This gives r^4 = b/a = 48 \sigma^2 / \kappa^2. ∎

Small radii are dominated by noise, large radii by curvature. For \sigma = 1 cm and a radius of curvature of 0.5 m (\kappa = 2 m⁻¹), the rule gives r^\star \approx 2.63 \times \sqrt{0.005} \approx 0.19\ \text{m}.

Mitra and Nguyen (2003) derive rigorous bounds of the same nature for the error of PCA normals, with density as a third factor.

Descriptors

The eigenvalues describe the dimensionality of the neighbourhood: one dominant direction for a pole or a cable, two for a wall, three for foliage.

Definition 2.9 — Covariance features With \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \lambda_3 > 0 and n the PCA normal, define

  • linearity L = (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)/\lambda_1, planarity P = (\lambda_2 - \lambda_3)/\lambda_1, sphericity S = \lambda_3 / \lambda_1;
  • omnivariance (\lambda_1 \lambda_2 \lambda_3)^{1/3} and eigenentropy -\sum_j e_j \log e_j, where e_j are the eigenvalues divided by their sum;
  • verticality 1 - |n_z|, which is 0 on the ground and 1 on a façade.

Computed at several radii and stacked with the height, they form the classical feature vector for point classification (Demantké et al., 2011; Weinmann et al., 2015), used in ch. 4.

Proposition 2.10 — Dimensionality features The three features satisfy L + P + S = 1 and lie in [0, 1].

They take the values (1, 0, 0) on a segment, (0, 1, 0) on a uniformly sampled disk and (0, 0, 1) on a uniformly sampled ball. The sum telescopes, and the three cases have covariance spectra proportional to (1, 0, 0), (1, 1, 0) and (1, 1, 1).

Demantké and coauthors (2011) choose, for each point, the radius at which the entropy of dimensionality features of this type (built from the square roots of the eigenvalues) is minimal: the scale at which the neighbourhood looks most clearly 1D, 2D or 3D.

Covariance features ignore how normals vary inside the neighbourhood. Histogram descriptors capture this, and are used to match points between scans (ch. 3).

Definition 2.11 — Point feature histograms (PFH, FPFH) For a pair of oriented points (p_s, n_s) and (p_t, n_t) at distance d, build the Darboux frame u = n_s, \qquad v = u \times \frac{p_t - p_s}{d}, \qquad w = u \times v .

The pair is described by three angles: \alpha = v^\top n_t, \phi = u^\top (p_t - p_s) / d and \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(w^\top n_t,\, u^\top n_t).

A simplified histogram SPFH(p) bins these angles over the pairs (p, p_i) with p_i in the neighbourhood. The fast PFH of Rusu, Blodow and Beetz (2009) adds the neighbours’ histograms, weighted by inverse distance: \mathrm{FPFH}(p) = \mathrm{SPFH}(p) + \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\mathrm{SPFH}(p_i)}{\|p_i - p\|} .

The angles are dot products of vectors that turn together under a rigid motion, so the descriptor is invariant to rotations and translations.

Outliers and noise

Definition 2.12 — Outlier removal and smoothing

  • Statistical outlier removal: compute for each point the mean distance \bar d_i to its k nearest neighbours, then the mean \mu and standard deviation s of these values over the cloud. Remove the points with \bar d_i > \mu + \alpha s, typically with \alpha between 1 and 3.
  • Radius outlier removal: remove the points with fewer than m neighbours within radius r.
  • Projection smoothing: move each point onto its PCA plane, x_i \leftarrow x_i - n_i n_i^\top (x_i - \bar x_i). Moving least squares (Alexa et al., 2003) refines this with weighted fits and a polynomial height function.
  • Bilateral smoothing: move x_i along n_i by a weighted mean of the offsets n_i^\top (x_j - x_i), with weights decreasing both with \|x_j - x_i\| and with the offset itself, so that sharp edges are preserved.

Code 2.13 — Normals and features at two scales A toy street contains a noisy ground (label 0), a thin pole of radius 8 cm (label 1) and a bush (label 2). The code compares 20-nearest neighbourhoods with balls of radius 0.5 m, then subsamples the cloud on a 0.2 m grid.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# A toy street: ground (label 0), a thin pole (1), a bush (2)
g = np.c_[rng.uniform(-3, 3, (3000, 2)), 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(3000)]
th, z = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 800), rng.uniform(0, 3, 800)
p = np.c_[0.08 * np.cos(th) + 1.0, 0.08 * np.sin(th) - 1.0, z]
b = rng.normal(0, 1, (1200, 3)); b /= np.linalg.norm(b, axis=1, keepdims=True)
b = b * rng.uniform(0, 0.6, (1200, 1)) ** (1 / 3) + [-1.0, 1.0, 0.7]
X = np.r_[g, p, b]
y = np.r_[np.zeros(3000), np.ones(800), 2 * np.ones(1200)].astype(int)
tree = cKDTree(X)

def pca_features(neigh_lists):
    """Linearity, planarity, sphericity and normal from each local covariance."""
    F, N = np.zeros((len(X), 3)), np.zeros((len(X), 3))
    for i, nb in enumerate(neigh_lists):
        Q = X[nb] - X[nb].mean(0)
        lam, vec = np.linalg.eigh(Q.T @ Q / len(nb))      # ascending order
        l3, l2, l1 = np.maximum(lam, 1e-12)
        F[i] = [(l1 - l2) / l1, (l2 - l3) / l1, l3 / l1]
        N[i] = vec[:, 0]                                  # smallest eigenvector
    return F, N

for name, nbs in [("k = 20", tree.query(X, k=20)[1]),
                  ("r = 0.5 m", tree.query_ball_point(X, r=0.5))]:
    F, N = pca_features(nbs)
    print(name)
    for c, cls in enumerate(["ground", "pole", "bush"]):
        print(f"  {cls:6s} lin/plan/sph =", np.round(F[y == c].mean(0), 2))
    err = np.degrees(np.arccos(np.abs(N[y == 0] @ [0, 0, 1])))
    print("  ground normals, median angular error:", round(np.median(err), 2), "deg")

# Voxel-grid subsampling: one barycentre per occupied 0.2 m cell
keys = np.floor(X / 0.2).astype(int)
_, inv, cnt = np.unique(keys, axis=0, return_inverse=True, return_counts=True)
sub = np.zeros((len(cnt), 3)); np.add.at(sub, inv.ravel(), X); sub /= cnt[:, None]
print("points:", len(X), "-> after the 0.2 m grid:", len(sub))

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • k-neighbourhoods adapt to density, balls keep a geometric meaning; kd-trees and voxel hashes make both fast.
  • Voxel grids even out density; farthest point sampling is a 2-approximation of the k-center problem.
  • The normal is the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of the local covariance (Theorem 2.5); orientation needs the sensor position or a propagation.
  • Scale matters: noise favours large neighbourhoods, curvature small ones, with an optimum near 48^{1/4}\sqrt{\sigma/\kappa}.
  • Linearity, planarity, sphericity and verticality at several scales describe the local shape; FPFH describes it invariantly for matching.
  • Remove outliers statistically, then smooth by projection or bilateral filtering.

3. Registration

A scanner sees a scene from one place. To model a building, a street or an object we must bring many scans into one frame, and a moving robot must estimate its motion from successive scans. This chapter solves the alignment problem: first with known correspondences in closed form, then by iterating closest points, then robustly and globally.

Definition 3.1 — Rigid registration Given a source cloud \mathcal{P} = (p_i) and a target cloud \mathcal{Q}, rigid registration seeks \min_{R \in SO(3),\ t \in \mathbb{R}^3} \ \sum_{i} \operatorname{dist}\big(R p_i + t,\ \mathcal{Q}\big)^2 , where the distance is to the closest point (or to the surface) of \mathcal{Q}.

The clouds overlap only partly, are sampled at different places, and are noisy. When pairs (p_i, q_i) are known to correspond, the distance becomes \|R p_i + t - q_i\|, and the problem has a closed-form solution.

Alignment with known correspondences

Write \bar p, \bar q for the centroids, p_i' = p_i - \bar p and q_i' = q_i - \bar q for the centred points, and H = \sum_i p_i' \, q_i'^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}, \qquad H = U \Sigma V^\top \ \text{(SVD)}.

Theorem 3.2 — Closed-form rigid alignment (Arun, Huang & Blostein, 1987; Horn, 1987; Umeyama, 1991) The minimisers of E(R, t) = \sum_i \|R p_i + t - q_i\|^2 \quad \text{over } R \in SO(3),\ t \in \mathbb{R}^3 are R^\star = V S U^\top, \quad S = \operatorname{diag}\big(1, 1, \det(V U^\top)\big), \quad t^\star = \bar q - R^\star \bar p .

Proof. Translation. For fixed R, E is a convex quadratic in t with gradient 2 \sum_i (R p_i + t - q_i). It vanishes at t = \bar q - R \bar p: the optimal motion maps the centroid onto the centroid.

Reduction to a trace. Substituting, E becomes the sum of the squares of \|R p_i' - q_i'\|. Since \|R p_i'\| = \|p_i'\|, E = \sum_i \big(\|p_i'\|^2 + \|q_i'\|^2\big) - 2 \sum_i q_i'^\top R\, p_i' .

The last sum equals \operatorname{tr}(R H), so we must maximise \operatorname{tr}(R H) over rotations.

Maximisation. With H = U \Sigma V^\top, set Z = V^\top R U, an orthogonal matrix. Then \operatorname{tr}(R H) = \operatorname{tr}(Z \Sigma) = \sum_j \sigma_j Z_{jj} \leq \sum_j \sigma_j , because the entries of an orthogonal matrix are at most 1 in absolute value. Equality holds for Z = I, that is R = V U^\top.

Reflections. If \det(V U^\top) = -1, then V U^\top is a reflection, and every rotation R gives a matrix Z with \det Z = -1.

Over such matrices, with the ordering \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3, the maximum of the sum of \sigma_j Z_{jj} is \sigma_1 + \sigma_2 - \sigma_3, attained at Z = S (Umeyama, 1991). This gives R^\star = V S U^\top. ∎

Key result — rigid alignment by SVD H = \sum_i (p_i - \bar p)(q_i - \bar q)^\top = U \Sigma V^\top \ \Longrightarrow\ R^\star = V S U^\top,\quad t^\star = \bar q - R^\star \bar p

The reflection case is not exotic: it happens when the points are nearly coplanar (\sigma_3 \approx 0) or very noisy. Forgetting the correction then returns a mirror image.

Example 3.3 — Alignment in the plane, by hand In 2D the rotation by \theta gives \operatorname{tr}(R H) = \cos\theta\, (H_{xx} + H_{yy}) + \sin\theta\, (H_{xy} - H_{yx}), so the best angle is \theta^\star = \operatorname{atan2}\big(H_{xy} - H_{yx},\ H_{xx} + H_{yy}\big).

Take p = (0,0), (1,0), (0,1) and q = (1,2), (1,3), (0,2).

  • Centroids: \bar p = (1/3, 1/3) and \bar q = (2/3, 7/3).
  • Centred points: p' = (-\tfrac13, -\tfrac13),\ (\tfrac23, -\tfrac13),\ (-\tfrac13, \tfrac23), \qquad q' = (\tfrac13, -\tfrac13),\ (\tfrac13, \tfrac23),\ (-\tfrac23, -\tfrac13).
  • Entries of H: H_{xx} = \frac13, H_{yy} = -\frac13, H_{xy} = \frac23, H_{yx} = -\frac23.
  • Angle: \operatorname{atan2}(4/3, 0), a quarter turn.
  • Translation: R^\star \bar p = (-\frac13, \frac13), so t^\star = \bar q - R^\star \bar p = (1, 2).

The data were generated by exactly this motion: \theta^\star = 90°, \qquad t^\star = (1, 2).

Iterative closest point

Without correspondences, the natural idea is to guess them: match each source point to its closest target point, align, and repeat.

Definition 3.4 — ICP (Besl & McKay, 1992; Zhang, 1994) Start from an initial motion T_0 = (R_0, t_0). For k = 0, 1, \dots:

  1. Match: for each i, let j_{k+1}(i) be the index of the point of \mathcal{Q} closest to T_k p_i (kd-tree, ch. 2);
  2. Align: let T_{k+1} minimise the sum of the squares of \|T p_i - q_{j_{k+1}(i)}\| (Theorem 3.2);
  3. stop when the cost no longer decreases by more than a tolerance.

Theorem 3.5 — Monotone convergence of ICP Let e(T, j) be the sum of the squares of \|T p_i - q_{j(i)}\|. The costs e(T_k, j_k) are non-increasing and converge.

If ties in the matching step are broken by keeping the current match, ICP reaches a fixed point after finitely many iterations.

Proof. The matching step chooses, for each i separately, the closest point, so e(T_k, j_{k+1}) \leq e(T_k, j_k).

The alignment step minimises over T with j_{k+1} fixed, so e(T_{k+1}, j_{k+1}) \leq e(T_k, j_{k+1}). A non-increasing sequence of non-negative numbers converges.

For finiteness, let F(j) be the minimum of e(T, j) over T; after each alignment the cost is F(j_k). If the matching does not change, the motion does not change either: a fixed point. Otherwise some point found a strictly closer match, and F(j_{k+1}) \leq e(T_k, j_{k+1}) < e(T_k, j_k) = F(j_k).

F takes finitely many values (there are finitely many matchings), so it cannot decrease strictly forever. ∎

The theorem guarantees convergence, not correctness. ICP converges to a local minimum, and its basin of attraction is limited: a rotation of a few tens of degrees or a small overlap is enough to lock it into a wrong alignment. ICP is therefore a fine registration method, started from a coarse estimate (GNSS/IMU, odometry, or the global methods below).

Point-to-plane ICP

Matching to the closest point penalises sliding along the surface, although two samplings of the same plane never coincide. Chen and Medioni (1992) penalise only the distance to the tangent plane at the target.

Definition 3.6 — Point-to-plane cost With n_i the normal at the matched target point q_i, E_{\text{plane}}(R, t) = \sum_i \big( (R p_i + t - q_i)^\top n_i \big)^2 .

The cost is zero as soon as every source point lies on the tangent plane of its match, whatever its position within that plane.

Proposition 3.7 — The linearised point-to-plane step Write a small motion as R \approx I + [\omega]_\times and t, with unknown \xi = (\omega, t) \in \mathbb{R}^6. Then E_{\text{plane}} becomes the linear least-squares problem \min_{\xi} \sum_i \big( a_i^\top \xi - b_i \big)^2, \qquad a_i = \begin{pmatrix} p_i \times n_i \\ n_i \end{pmatrix}, \quad b_i = (q_i - p_i)^\top n_i , solved by the 6 \times 6 normal equations (Proposition 0.5).

Proof. To first order, R p_i + t - q_i = p_i - q_i + \omega \times p_i + t.

Take the inner product with n_i and use the identity (\omega \times p_i)^\top n_i = \omega^\top (p_i \times n_i).

The residual becomes a_i^\top \xi - b_i. ∎

The rotation found is turned into an exact rotation with Rodrigues’ formula (Proposition 0.3), and the step is repeated with new matches (Low, 2004).

The 6 \times 6 matrix \sum_i a_i a_i^\top shows when registration is ill-posed. On a single plane all n_i are equal: translations within the plane and rotations about its normal leave the cost unchanged, and the matrix has rank 3. In a straight corridor the translation along the axis is unobservable.

LiDAR odometry monitors the eigenvalues of this matrix to detect such degenerate places.

Code 3.8 — Point-to-point versus point-to-plane ICP A bumpy, non-symmetric patch is sampled twice (4,000 and 3,000 points, at different places). The second sample is rotated by 20°, translated by about 20 cm and given 5 mm of noise. Both variants run 30 iterations.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree
from scipy.spatial.transform import Rotation

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

def surface(n):                       # a bumpy, non-symmetric patch with its normals
    u = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (n, 2))
    z = 0.3 * np.sin(2 * u[:, 0]) * np.cos(3 * u[:, 1]) + 0.2 * u[:, 0] ** 2
    dzdx = 0.6 * np.cos(2 * u[:, 0]) * np.cos(3 * u[:, 1]) + 0.4 * u[:, 0]
    dzdy = -0.9 * np.sin(2 * u[:, 0]) * np.sin(3 * u[:, 1])
    nrm = np.c_[-dzdx, -dzdy, np.ones(n)]
    return np.c_[u, z], nrm / np.linalg.norm(nrm, axis=1, keepdims=True)

def kabsch(P, Q):
    """R, t minimising sum ||R p_i + t - q_i||^2 (Theorem 3.2)."""
    mp, mq = P.mean(0), Q.mean(0)
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd((P - mp).T @ (Q - mq))
    S = np.diag([1, 1, np.sign(np.linalg.det(Vt.T @ U.T))])
    R = Vt.T @ S @ U.T
    return R, mq - R @ mp

Q, NQ = surface(4000)                                   # reference ("model") scan
R0 = Rotation.from_rotvec(np.radians(20) * np.array([0.3, 0.2, 0.93])).as_matrix()
P = (surface(3000)[0] @ R0.T) + [0.15, -0.10, 0.05]     # new scan, other samples
P += 0.005 * rng.standard_normal(P.shape)
tree = cKDTree(Q)

def icp(P, plane, iters=30):
    R, t, rms = np.eye(3), np.zeros(3), []
    for _ in range(iters):
        X = P @ R.T + t
        d, j = tree.query(X)
        rms.append(np.sqrt(np.mean(d ** 2)))
        if plane:                                       # linearised point-to-plane step
            n = NQ[j]
            A = np.c_[np.cross(X, n), n]
            b = np.einsum("ij,ij->i", Q[j] - X, n)
            x = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)[0]
            dR = Rotation.from_rotvec(x[:3]).as_matrix()
            R, t = dR @ R, dR @ t + x[3:]
        else:                                           # point-to-point step
            dR, dt = kabsch(X, Q[j])
            R, t = dR @ R, dR @ t + dt
    return R, rms

for plane in (False, True):

    ang = np.degrees(Rotation.from_matrix(R @ R0).magnitude())
    print("point-to-plane" if plane else "point-to-point",

          f"| residual rotation error: {ang:.3f} deg")

Robust registration

Real scans contain points with no counterpart: parts seen from one station only, moving cars, noise. Their closest-point residuals are large and pull the least-squares solution away.

Definition 3.9 — Robust ICP variants

  • Rejection (Rusinkiewicz & Levoy, 2001): discard pairs farther than a threshold, pairs whose normals disagree, and pairs whose target lies on the boundary of \mathcal{Q}.
  • Trimmed ICP (Chetverikov et al., 2002): keep only the fraction \eta of pairs with the smallest residuals, \eta being an estimate of the overlap.
  • M-estimators: replace the square by a function \rho that grows more slowly, and minimise the sum of \rho(r_i). Examples are Huber’s function, quadratic up to c and linear beyond, and the Cauchy function \frac{c^2}{2} \log(1 + r^2/c^2).

Proposition 3.10 — Iteratively reweighted least squares Suppose \rho(r) = \phi(r^2) with \phi concave and non-decreasing, and let r_i(\theta) be the residuals of the parameters \theta.

Given \theta_k, set the weights w_i = \phi'(r_i(\theta_k)^2) and let \theta_{k+1} minimise the weighted sum of w_i\, r_i(\theta)^2. Then \sum_i \rho\big(r_i(\theta_{k+1})\big) \leq \sum_i \rho\big(r_i(\theta_k)\big).

Proof. Concavity gives the tangent bound \phi(s) \leq \phi(s_0) + \phi'(s_0)(s - s_0).

Applied to each s = r_i(\theta)^2 at s_0 = r_i(\theta_k)^2, it bounds the robust cost by a constant plus the weighted least-squares cost. The bound is tight at \theta_k.

Minimising the bound cannot increase it, and the robust cost lies below the bound: this is the majorise–minimise principle. ∎

For the Cauchy function the weight is 1/(1 + r^2/c^2): a pair with residual 3c counts ten times less than a perfect pair. Each robust ICP iteration is therefore a weighted Theorem 3.2 or a weighted Proposition 3.7.

Global registration

When no initial guess is available, correspondences are found by comparing descriptors instead of positions.

Definition 3.11 — Feature-based registration with RANSAC

  1. Compute a descriptor such as FPFH (Definition 2.11) at keypoints of both clouds, and match each keypoint to its nearest neighbour in descriptor space, keeping mutual matches.
  2. Repeat: draw three matches at random, compute the motion they define (Theorem 3.2), and count the matches it brings within a distance \varepsilon, called inliers.
  3. Keep the motion with the most inliers, refit it on all its inliers, then refine with ICP.

Most descriptor matches are wrong, so the number of draws matters.

Theorem 3.12 — How many RANSAC iterations If a fraction w of the matches are correct and a minimal sample has s elements, then N independent samples contain at least one all-inlier sample with probability at least p as soon as N \geq \frac{\log(1 - p)}{\log(1 - w^s)} .

Proof. A sample of s matches drawn independently is all-inlier with probability q = w^s; apply Proposition 0.7. Drawing without replacement from a large set changes q only slightly. ∎

For p = 0.99 and s = 3 the required number of draws explodes as w decreases:

Inlier ratio w 0.5 0.3 0.1 0.05
Iterations N 35 169 4,603 36,840

Since w is unknown, implementations start with a large N and decrease it each time a better consensus raises the estimate of w (the adaptive rule used in Code 5.10).

Note 3.13 — Beyond two scans

  • Variants of the cost: Generalized-ICP (Segal, Haehnel & Thrun, 2009) models both clouds as local Gaussians and contains point-to-point and point-to-plane as special cases. The normal distributions transform (Biber & Straßer, 2003) registers a cloud to a voxel grid of Gaussians.
  • Other global methods: 4PCS (Aiger, Mitra & Cohen-Or, 2008) matches coplanar four-point bases, and TEASER (Yang, Shi & Carlone, 2021) gives certifiable solutions under many outliers.
  • Many scans: pairwise motions are combined in a pose graph and adjusted jointly so that loops close consistently.
  • LiDAR odometry registers each new scan to a map built from the previous ones, in real time; see ch. 8.
  • Evaluation: rotation error (the angle of the relative rotation, via the trace formula), translation error, and RMS of point-to-point or point-to-plane distances on the overlap.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • With known correspondences: centre both sets, take the SVD of the cross-covariance, R^\star = V S U^\top, t^\star = \bar q - R^\star \bar p; the matrix S removes reflections.
  • ICP alternates closest-point matching and closed-form alignment; its cost decreases monotonically to a local minimum.
  • Point-to-plane ICP linearises to a 6 \times 6 system and converges much faster; its rank reveals degenerate scenes.
  • Outliers are handled by rejection, trimming or M-estimators solved by reweighted least squares.
  • Global registration matches descriptors and needs about \log(1-p)/\log(1-w^3) RANSAC draws.

4. Segmentation of point clouds

A registered cloud is still a single list of points. Segmentation cuts it into meaningful parts: smooth surface patches, objects, or semantic classes such as ground, building, vegetation and car. This chapter goes from geometric criteria to learned classifiers, and ends with neural networks that consume raw points.

Definition 4.1 — Segmentation tasks and their metrics

  • Geometric segmentation partitions the cloud into connected regions that are smooth or belong to one primitive.
  • Semantic segmentation assigns a class label y_i \in \{1, \dots, C\} to every point.
  • Instance segmentation also separates objects of the same class (this car, that car).

For semantic segmentation, with \mathrm{TP}_c, \mathrm{FP}_c, \mathrm{FN}_c the true positives, false positives and false negatives of class c, \mathrm{IoU}_c = \frac{\mathrm{TP}_c}{\mathrm{TP}_c + \mathrm{FP}_c + \mathrm{FN}_c}, \qquad \mathrm{mIoU} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_c \mathrm{IoU}_c .

The overall accuracy is dominated by large classes such as the ground; the mean IoU weighs every class equally and is the standard benchmark score.

Geometric segmentation

Definition 4.2 — Region growing with a smoothness constraint (Rabbani, van den Heuvel & Vosselman, 2006) Compute normals and the residual of each local plane fit, or the surface variation (ch. 2). Sort the points by increasing residual. While some point is unlabelled:

  1. start a new region from the flattest unlabelled point, and put it in a queue of seeds;
  2. pop a seed; add each unlabelled neighbour whose normal makes an angle below \theta_{\max} with the seed’s normal;
  3. among the added neighbours, those with a residual below a threshold become seeds;
  4. when the queue is empty, close the region.

Each point enters at most once, so the cost is O(nk) for k neighbours per point after the neighbour search.

Region growing returns smooth patches: planes of a façade, a road surface, the cylinder of a pole. A simpler variant, Euclidean clustering, keeps the connected components of the graph that joins points closer than r. After the ground has been removed it separates isolated objects such as cars and pedestrians.

Both methods decide locally. A global formulation instead balances how well each point fits a label against how coherent the labels of neighbours are.

Definition 4.3 — Labelling energy with a Potts prior On a neighbourhood graph (V, E) of the cloud, a labelling y = (y_i) has energy \mathcal{E}(y) = \sum_{i \in V} U_i(y_i) + \beta \sum_{(i,j) \in E} \mathbb{1}[y_i \neq y_j] , where the data term U_i(c) is the cost of giving label c to point i (for example minus the log-probability returned by a classifier) and \beta > 0 penalises label changes between neighbours.

Theorem 4.4 — Exact binary labelling by a minimum cut (Greig, Porteous & Seheult, 1989) For two labels, the minimum of \mathcal{E} is computed exactly by a minimum s–t cut, hence in polynomial time.

For more labels and the Potts prior, minimisation is NP-hard. The α-expansion algorithm of Boykov, Veksler and Zabih (2001) solves a sequence of binary cuts and returns a labelling whose energy is at most twice the minimum.

Proof. Sketch for two labels \{0, 1\}. Build a graph with a source s, a sink t and one node per point.

Add an edge s \to i of capacity U_i(1), an edge i \to t of capacity U_i(0), and edges i \leftrightarrow j of capacity \beta for every pair of neighbours.

An s–t cut puts each node on the source side (label 0) or on the sink side (label 1). Its cost is the sum of the capacities it severs: U_i(1) for the points labelled 1, U_i(0) for the points labelled 0, and \beta for each pair of neighbours with different labels.

That is exactly \mathcal{E}(y). A minimum cut, computable by max-flow, therefore minimises the energy. ∎

Classification with hand-crafted features

The classical supervised pipeline computes, for every point, the features of Definition 2.9 at several scales and trains a standard classifier on labelled points.

Definition 4.5 — Multi-scale feature classification

  1. Choose radii r_1 < \dots < r_S, or a pyramid of voxel grids as in Hackel, Wegner and Schindler (2016), which keeps the neighbour search cheap on large scenes.
  2. For every point and scale compute linearity, planarity, sphericity, verticality and the other features of Definition 2.9; add the height above the ground and, when present, intensity and colour.
  3. Train a random forest (Breiman, 2001): an average of decision trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample with random subsets of features at each split.
  4. Optionally regularise the predicted labels with the energy of Definition 4.3.

Weinmann, Jutzi, Hinz and Mallet (2015) compare neighbourhood definitions, feature sets and classifiers systematically in this pipeline.

Code 4.6 — One scale or three? Two toy street scans with four classes (ground, pole, bush, façade) are generated with different layouts. A random forest is trained on the first and tested on the second, with features at one radius, at three radii, and at three radii plus the height.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

def scene(seed):
    """Toy street with labels: ground 0, pole 1, bush 2, facade 3."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    g = np.c_[rng.uniform(-3, 3, (3000, 2)), 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(3000)]
    th, z = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 600), rng.uniform(0, 3, 600)
    c = rng.uniform(-2, 2, 2)
    p = np.c_[0.08 * np.cos(th) + c[0], 0.08 * np.sin(th) + c[1], z]
    b = rng.normal(0, 1, (1200, 3)); b /= np.linalg.norm(b, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    b = b * rng.uniform(0, 0.6, (1200, 1)) ** (1 / 3) + [*rng.uniform(-2, 2, 2), 0.7]
    f = np.c_[np.full(1500, 3.0), rng.uniform(-3, 3, 1500), rng.uniform(0, 4, 1500)]
    f[:, 0] += 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(1500)
    y = np.repeat([0, 1, 2, 3], [3000, 600, 1200, 1500])
    return np.r_[g, p, b, f], y

def features(X, radii=(0.25, 0.5, 1.0)):
    """Per radius: linearity, planarity, sphericity, verticality; then the height."""
    tree, F = cKDTree(X), []
    for r in radii:
        out = np.zeros((len(X), 4))
        for i, nb in enumerate(tree.query_ball_point(X, r)):
            Q = X[nb] - X[nb].mean(0)
            lam, vec = np.linalg.eigh(Q.T @ Q / len(nb))
            l3, l2, l1 = np.maximum(lam, 1e-12)
            out[i] = [(l1 - l2) / l1, (l2 - l3) / l1, l3 / l1, 1 - abs(vec[2, 0])]
        F.append(out)
    return np.hstack(F + [X[:, 2:3]])

(Xtr, ytr), (Xte, yte) = scene(0), scene(1)       # train on one scan, test on another
Ftr, Fte = features(Xtr), features(Xte)
for name, cols in [("one scale (0.25 m)", slice(0, 4)),
                   ("three scales", slice(0, 12)),
                   ("three scales + height", slice(0, 13))]:
    clf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, random_state=0).fit(Ftr[:, cols], ytr)
    pred = clf.predict(Fte[:, cols])
    iou = [np.sum((pred == c) & (yte == c)) / np.sum((pred == c) | (yte == c)) for c in range(4)]
    print(f"{name:22s} IoU ground/pole/bush/facade = {np.round(iou, 3)}  mIoU = {np.mean(iou):.3f}")

Learning directly on points

Hand-crafted features fix the description in advance. Deep networks learn it, but a cloud is neither a grid (no convolution) nor a sequence (no order). The architecture must be insensitive to the order of the points.

Definition 4.7 — Symmetric set functions and PointNet A function f(x_1, \dots, x_n) is permutation invariant if f(x_{\pi(1)}, \dots, x_{\pi(n)}) = f(x_1, \dots, x_n) for every permutation \pi.

PointNet (Qi, Su, Mo & Guibas, 2017) computes f(\mathcal{X}) = \gamma\Big( \max_{i = 1, \dots, n} h(x_i) \Big), where h : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^K is a multilayer perceptron applied to every point with shared weights, the maximum is taken coordinate by coordinate, and \gamma is another perceptron.

For segmentation, the global vector \max_i h(x_i) is concatenated to each point’s own features and a third shared perceptron predicts one label per point.

The maximum is symmetric, so f is permutation invariant by construction. The next theorem shows that nothing essential is lost.

Theorem 4.8 — Universal approximation of set functions (Qi et al., 2017) Let f be a function on the sets of n points of [0, 1]^3, continuous for the Hausdorff distance d_H.

For every \varepsilon > 0 there exist a continuous h and a continuous \gamma such that, for every such set \mathcal{X}, \Big| f(\mathcal{X}) - \gamma\big( \max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} h(x) \big) \Big| < \varepsilon .

Proof. Sketch. The domain is compact for d_H, so f is uniformly continuous: there is \delta > 0 such that sets at Hausdorff distance below \delta have values closer than \varepsilon.

Cut the cube into K small cells of diameter below \delta, and let h_k be a continuous approximation of the indicator of cell k. Then v = \max_i h(x_i) \in [0, 1]^K approximately records which cells are occupied.

Any n-point set that meets every occupied cell and stays inside their union is within \delta of \mathcal{X} in Hausdorff distance, so f differs by less than \varepsilon on it.

Defining \gamma(v) as f of such a set for each occupancy pattern, extended continuously between the vertices of [0, 1]^K, gives the bound. ∎

Zaheer and coauthors (2017, “Deep Sets”) prove a sum-decomposition \rho(\sum_i \phi(x_i)) of the same kind. The max version has an extra property: robustness to missing points.

Proposition 4.9 — Critical points Let v = \max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} h(x) \in \mathbb{R}^K. There is a subset \mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{X} with at most K points, the critical set, such that every set \mathcal{T} with \mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{T} \subseteq \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : h(x) \leq v \text{ coordinatewise} \} has the same global feature v, hence the same output.

Proof. For each coordinate k, choose a point of \mathcal{X} where the k-th coordinate of h reaches its maximum v_k.

Let \mathcal{C} be the set of these at most K points.

Any such \mathcal{T} contains a point reaching v_k for each k, and no point exceeding it. So its coordinatewise maximum is v. ∎

Removing non-critical points, or adding points below v, does not change the output: PointNet tolerates sparse or partial clouds better than a dense-grid network. Its weakness is the opposite: each point is processed alone, so local geometry enters only through the final maximum.

Code 4.10 — A minimal PointNet Four classes of shapes (sphere, cube, cylinder, cone), 128 noisy points each, randomly rotated about the vertical axis and scaled. A PointNet with a shared perceptron 3 \to 32 \to 64 \to 128 is trained for 20 epochs on 600 shapes and tested on 300.

import numpy as np, torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.set_num_threads(1); torch.manual_seed(0); rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def sample(kind, n=128):
    """n noisy points on a sphere, cube, cylinder or cone, randomly turned and scaled."""
    if kind == 0:
        x = rng.normal(size=(n, 3)); x /= np.linalg.norm(x, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    elif kind == 1:
        x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (n, 3)); f = rng.integers(0, 3, n)
        x[np.arange(n), f] = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], n)
    else:
        th, z = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, n), rng.uniform(-1, 1, n)
        rad = np.ones(n) if kind == 2 else (1 - z) / 2
        x = np.c_[rad * np.cos(th), rad * np.sin(th), z]
    a = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi)                        # random rotation about z
    Rz = np.array([[np.cos(a), -np.sin(a), 0], [np.sin(a), np.cos(a), 0], [0, 0, 1]])
    return (x @ Rz.T) * rng.uniform(0.7, 1.3) + 0.02 * rng.normal(size=(n, 3))

def dataset(m):
    y = rng.integers(0, 4, m)
    X = np.stack([sample(k) for k in y])
    return torch.tensor(X, dtype=torch.float32), torch.tensor(y)

class PointNet(nn.Module):
    """Shared per-point MLP h, symmetric max-pooling, then a classifier gamma."""
    def __init__(self, k=4):
        super().__init__()
        self.h = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(3, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, 64),
                               nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 128))
        self.gamma = nn.Sequential(nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, k))
    def forward(self, x):                                # x: (batch, n_points, 3)
        return self.gamma(self.h(x).max(dim=1).values)

Xtr, ytr = dataset(600); Xte, yte = dataset(300)
net = PointNet(); opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
for epoch in range(20):
    perm = torch.randperm(len(Xtr))
    for i in range(0, len(Xtr), 50):
        b = perm[i:i + 50]
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(net(Xtr[b]), ytr[b])
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    acc = (net(Xte).argmax(1) == yte).float().mean().item()
    same = torch.allclose(net(Xte), net(Xte[:, torch.randperm(128)]), atol=1e-5)
print(f"test accuracy (sphere/cube/cylinder/cone): {acc:.3f}")
print("output unchanged when the points are reordered:", same)

The network classifies all 300 test shapes correctly (accuracy 1.000), and shuffling the points leaves its outputs unchanged, as Definition 4.7 guarantees. Training takes a few seconds on one CPU core.

Local structure: hierarchies and point convolutions

Definition 4.11 — PointNet++ (Qi, Yi, Su & Guibas, 2017) A set abstraction layer maps a set of points with features to a smaller set:

  1. sample m centres by farthest point sampling (Proposition 2.4);
  2. group the points within radius r of each centre (ball query);
  3. apply a small PointNet to each group, on coordinates relative to the centre, which gives one feature vector per centre.

Stacking layers with growing radii builds a hierarchy, like the pyramid of a convolutional network. For segmentation, features are propagated back to all points by inverse-distance interpolation from the three nearest centres.

Grouping with several radii at once (“multi-scale grouping”) makes the network robust to density changes, the learned counterpart of the multi-scale features of Definition 4.5.

Definition 4.12 — Kernel point convolution, KPConv (Thomas et al., 2019) A kernel is a set of K kernel points \tilde x_1, \dots, \tilde x_K in a ball of radius r, each carrying a weight matrix W_k. The convolution of the input features f_i at a point x is (\mathcal{F} * g)(x) = \sum_{x_i \in \mathcal{N}_r(x)} g(x_i - x)\, f_i, \qquad g(y) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \max\Big(0,\ 1 - \frac{\|y - \tilde x_k\|}{\sigma}\Big) W_k .

Each neighbour contributes through the kernel points close to its relative position, with a linear correlation of influence \sigma. In the deformable version, the network also predicts shifts of the kernel points at each location. Between layers the cloud is subsampled on voxel grids of growing size (Definition 2.3).

KPConv is a genuine convolution: the same filter is applied at every location, and it depends on relative positions only, so it is translation equivariant.

Note 4.13 — Other architectures and benchmark data

  • Graph-based: DGCNN (Wang et al., 2019) recomputes k-nearest-neighbour graphs in feature space at every layer (EdgeConv).
  • Sparse voxels: networks such as MinkowskiNet (Choy, Gwak & Savarese, 2019) voxelise the cloud and convolve only on occupied voxels.
  • Attention: point transformers (Zhao et al., 2021) replace the fixed kernel by attention weights within local neighbourhoods.
  • Data sets: ModelNet40 for shape classification (Wu et al., 2015); S3DIS for indoor scenes (Armeni et al., 2016); Semantic3D (Hackel et al., 2017), Paris-Lille-3D (Roynard, Deschaud & Goulette, 2018) and SemanticKITTI (Behley et al., 2019) for outdoor LiDAR.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Report per-class IoU and mIoU; overall accuracy hides the small classes.
  • Region growing on normals and curvature gives smooth patches; Euclidean clustering separates objects once the ground is removed.
  • A Potts energy balances data fit and coherence; two labels are solved exactly by a minimum cut, several within a factor 2 by α-expansion.
  • Multi-scale covariance features with a random forest are a strong, fast baseline.
  • PointNet is a shared perceptron followed by a max: permutation invariant, universal for continuous set functions, determined by at most K critical points.
  • PointNet++ adds a sampling–grouping hierarchy; KPConv defines true convolutions on points with kernel points.

5. Modelling by primitives

Man-made scenes are mostly planes, cylinders, spheres and cones: walls, pipes, tanks, columns. Describing a scan by a few such primitives compresses it by orders of magnitude and turns points into an editable model. This chapter fits primitives, detects them in clutter with RANSAC and the Hough transform, and chooses between them.

Definition 5.1 — Primitives and their distances

  • Plane \{x : n^\top x = d\}, 3 parameters; distance |n^\top x - d|. Least-squares fit: Theorem 2.5.
  • Sphere of centre c and radius r, 4 parameters; distance \big|\, \|x - c\| - r \,\big|.
  • Cylinder of unit axis a through c and radius r, 5 parameters; distance \big|\, \|(I - a a^\top)(x - c)\| - r \,\big|.
  • Cone (apex, axis, angle, 6 parameters) and torus (7 parameters) complete the usual list.

A point is an inlier of a primitive if its distance is below a threshold \varepsilon and, when normals are available, its normal deviates from the primitive’s normal by less than an angle \alpha.

The geometric distance makes sphere and cylinder fitting non-linear. A change of unknowns makes the sphere linear.

Proposition 5.2 — Algebraic sphere fit The condition \|x - c\|^2 = r^2 is equivalent to \|x\|^2 = 2 c^\top x + d, \qquad d = r^2 - \|c\|^2 , which is linear in the unknowns (c, d). Least squares over all points (Proposition 0.5) gives c and d, then r = \sqrt{d + \|c\|^2}.

Proof. Expand \|x - c\|^2 = \|x\|^2 - 2 c^\top x + \|c\|^2 and rearrange. ∎

The algebraic residual \|x\|^2 - 2c^\top x - d equals (\|x - c\| - r)(\|x - c\| + r), about 2r times the geometric one near the sphere. The fit is excellent on well-covered spheres but biased on small caps; it then serves as the start of a Gauss–Newton refinement of the geometric distance.

Proposition 5.3 — A cylinder from two oriented points Let (p_1, n_1) and (p_2, n_2) be two points of a cylinder with non-parallel normals. Then:

  • the axis direction is a = n_1 \times n_2 / \|n_1 \times n_2\|;
  • after projection on the plane orthogonal to a, the axis is the intersection point of the two lines p_k + s\, n_k;
  • the radius is the distance from p_1 to the axis.

Proof. Every normal of a cylinder is orthogonal to its axis, so a is orthogonal to n_1 and n_2.

The normal line at a point of a cylinder meets the axis orthogonally. In the cross-section plane the cylinder is a circle, and its normal lines all pass through the centre. ∎

Two oriented points also determine a sphere (where the normal lines meet). Oriented samples therefore make minimal samples smaller, which matters for RANSAC.

Detection with RANSAC

Definition 5.4 — RANSAC shape detection (Fischler & Bolles, 1981)

  1. Draw a minimal sample: 3 points for a plane, 2 oriented points for a cylinder or a sphere.
  2. Compute the candidate primitive; its score is its number of inliers.
  3. Repeat N times, with N updated by Theorem 3.12 using the best inlier ratio found so far.
  4. Refit the best candidate on its inliers by least squares, keep it, remove its inliers, and start again (sequential RANSAC) until no candidate has enough support.

On large scenes a single primitive holds a tiny fraction of the points, and Theorem 3.12 becomes prohibitive. Schnabel, Wahl and Klein (2007) draw the samples locally.

Proposition 5.5 — Localised sampling Suppose the first point is drawn uniformly, and the other s - 1 points are drawn in the octree cell of the first. If a shape holds a fraction w of the points, and fills a fraction \eta of each cell it crosses, the probability that a sample is all-inlier becomes q = w\, \eta^{s - 1} \quad \text{instead of} \quad w^s .

Proof. The first point is an inlier with probability w. Given that, its cell is one the shape crosses, so each of the other draws is an inlier with probability \eta, independently. ∎

Take 10^6 points, a plane of 1{,}000 points and samples of size 3. Uniform sampling needs about 4.6 \times 10^9 draws for p = 0.99. With cells in which the plane holds half of the points, q = 10^{-3} \times 0.25 and N \approx \frac{4.6}{2.5 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 18{,}400 \text{ draws}.

Efficient RANSAC also estimates scores on random subsets of the cloud, and keeps only the largest connected component of the inliers, so that two separate walls in the same plane become two primitives.

The Hough transform

RANSAC samples the data and tests parameters. The Hough transform does the reverse: every point votes for all the primitives that pass through it, in a discretised parameter space.

Definition 5.6 — Hough transform for planes Parametrise a plane by its unit normal in spherical angles and its distance \rho to the origin: n(\theta, \phi) = (\cos\theta \sin\phi,\ \sin\theta \sin\phi,\ \cos\phi), \qquad n(\theta, \phi)^\top x = \rho .

Discretise (\theta, \phi, \rho) into an accumulator. Each point x adds one vote, for every angle cell, to the \rho-cell containing n(\theta, \phi)^\top x. Planes are the peaks of the accumulator.

Proposition 5.7 — Cost and resolution of the Hough transform With N_\theta \times N_\phi angle cells, n points cast n N_\theta N_\phi votes.

If the angle cells have width \Delta\alpha, the points of a plane of diameter D vote, in the cell nearest to the true normal, for values of \rho spread over an interval of width at most D\, \Delta\alpha / 2, plus the noise.

Proof. The first claim is a count. For the second, let n be the true normal, n' the normal of the cell, at an angle of at most \Delta\alpha / 2, and c the centre of the plane patch. For a point x of the patch, n^\top(x - c) = 0, so |n'^\top x - n'^\top c| = |(n' - n)^\top (x - c)| \leq \|n' - n\| \, \frac{D}{2} \leq \frac{\Delta\alpha}{2} \cdot \frac{D}{2} . The interval of values has twice this half-width. ∎

With 1° cells, a 10 m façade spreads its votes over 8.7 cm of \rho: finer \rho-cells split the peak, coarser ones merge nearby planes. Cells of 1° also make 360 \times 90 = 32{,}400 angle cells, hence 3 \times 10^{10} votes for 10^6 points. Randomised variants and better accumulator designs (Borrmann et al., 2011) reduce both problems.

Choosing between primitives

Definition 5.8 — Penalised model selection Inlier counts favour complex shapes: a sphere of radius 100 m fits a 2 m wall patch as well as a plane does. Among candidate models M with k_M parameters and residuals r_i on the same points, choose the one minimising \operatorname{cost}(M) = \sum_i \frac{r_i^2}{\sigma^2} + k_M \log m , with \sigma the noise level and m the number of points (a BIC-type penalty). When residuals are equal, the plane (3 parameters) beats the sphere (4).

Note 5.9 — From primitives to models Detected primitives are linked into an adjacency graph (two primitives are adjacent if their inliers are close). Intersections of adjacent planes give edges and corners, from which building or CAD models are assembled.

Examples are the hybrid city models of Lafarge and Mallet (2012) and the polygonal surface reconstruction of PolyFit (Nan & Wonka, 2017), which selects faces from the arrangement of detected planes by integer programming.

Code 5.10 — Sequential RANSAC on a room corner, and a sphere fit A floor and two walls (2,000, 1,500 and 1,000 points with 1 cm noise) are mixed with 1,900 clutter points, 30 % of the cloud. Three planes are extracted in turn with the adaptive number of iterations. Then a sphere is fitted algebraically to a noisy half sphere.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# A room corner: floor z=0, two walls x=0 and y=0, plus 30 % clutter
def patch(axis, n):
    P = rng.uniform(0, 4, (n, 3)); P[:, axis] = 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(n); return P
X = np.r_[patch(2, 2000), patch(0, 1500), patch(1, 1000), rng.uniform(0, 4, (1900, 3))]

def ransac_plane(X, eps=0.03, p=0.99, max_iter=10_000):
    """Plane with the largest consensus set; iteration count updated adaptively."""
    best, N, k = np.zeros(len(X), bool), max_iter, 0
    while k < N:
        a, b, c = X[rng.choice(len(X), 3, replace=False)]
        n = np.cross(b - a, c - a)
        if np.linalg.norm(n) < 1e-9:
            k += 1; continue
        n /= np.linalg.norm(n)
        inl = np.abs((X - a) @ n) < eps
        if inl.sum() > best.sum():
            best = inl
            w = inl.mean()                                    # current inlier ratio
            N = min(max_iter, int(np.ceil(np.log(1 - p) / np.log(1 - w ** 3))))
        k += 1
    Q = X[best] - X[best].mean(0)                             # least-squares refit
    n = np.linalg.svd(Q, full_matrices=False)[2][-1]
    return n, best, k

rest = X.copy()
for found in range(3):                                        # sequential RANSAC
    n, inl, k = ransac_plane(rest)
    print(f"plane {found + 1}: normal {np.round(np.abs(n), 3)}, "
          f"{inl.sum()} inliers, {k} iterations")
    rest = rest[~inl]
print("points left:", len(rest))

# Algebraic sphere fit: |x|^2 = 2 c.x + (r^2 - |c|^2) is linear in (c, d)
S = rng.normal(size=(500, 3)); S /= np.linalg.norm(S, axis=1, keepdims=True)
S = 0.5 * S[S[:, 2] > 0] + [1, 2, 3]                       # upper half only
S += 0.005 * rng.standard_normal(S.shape)
A = np.c_[2 * S, np.ones(len(S))]
sol = np.linalg.lstsq(A, (S ** 2).sum(1), rcond=None)[0]
c = sol[:3]; r = np.sqrt(sol[3] + c @ c)
print("sphere centre", np.round(c, 3), "radius", round(r, 3))

The floor is found first (2,006 inliers, with a few clutter points within 3 cm), after 148 iterations: its inlier ratio w \approx 0.31 gives N = 147 by Theorem 3.12. The walls follow with 1,490 and 989 inliers in 116 and 115 iterations; 1,915 points remain.

The sphere fit returns centre (1.000, 1.999, 3.001) and radius 0.500 from half a sphere.

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Planes are fitted by PCA, spheres linearly in (c, \, r^2 - \|c\|^2), cylinders from two oriented points.
  • RANSAC detects primitives in clutter; sequential RANSAC extracts them one by one.
  • Localised sampling turns the success probability w^s into w \eta^{s-1}: the key to efficient RANSAC on large scenes.
  • The Hough transform votes in parameter space; its cost and its resolution trade-offs grow with the number of angle cells.
  • Penalise the number of parameters when choosing between primitives.

6. Reconstruction of curves and surfaces

Points are samples; most applications need the continuous object they come from: a mesh to render, measure or print, or a function whose zero set is the surface. This chapter asks when samples determine a curve or a surface, and builds reconstructions from Delaunay triangulations and from implicit functions, up to Poisson reconstruction.

Sampling conditions and curve reconstruction

Definition 6.1 — Medial axis, local feature size, ε-sample (Amenta & Bern, 1999) Let \Gamma be a smooth closed curve or surface.

  • Its medial axis is the closure of the set of points of space with at least two closest points on \Gamma.
  • The local feature size \operatorname{lfs}(x) of x \in \Gamma is the distance from x to the medial axis.
  • A finite set S \subset \Gamma is an \varepsilon-sample if every x \in \Gamma has a sample within distance \varepsilon \operatorname{lfs}(x).

The local feature size is small near sharp bends and thin parts, where dense sampling is needed, and large on flat regions, where sparse sampling suffices.

The Voronoi cell of a sample is the set of points closer to it than to any other sample. The Delaunay triangulation is the dual structure: it joins two samples when their cells touch, and no sample lies inside the circumscribed circle of any of its triangles.

Definition 6.2 — The crust (Amenta, Bern & Eppstein, 1998) Given samples S of a curve in the plane:

  1. compute the vertices V of the Voronoi diagram of S;
  2. compute the Delaunay triangulation of S \cup V;
  3. keep the Delaunay edges whose two ends both belong to S.

For a dense sample the Voronoi vertices lie near the medial axis. Adding them destroys every Delaunay edge that cuts across the shape, while edges between neighbours along the curve survive. Amenta, Bern and Eppstein prove that for an \varepsilon-sample with \varepsilon below an explicit constant, the crust joins exactly the samples that are adjacent along the curve.

Code 6.3 — Crust versus nearest neighbours Two hundred random samples of the flower curve r = 1 + 0.3 \cos 5\theta are reconstructed by the crust and by the naive rule “join each sample to its two nearest neighbours”.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import Delaunay, Voronoi

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# 200 random samples of the flower r = 1 + 0.3 cos(5 theta)
th = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 200)               # random positions on the curve
S = np.c_[(1 + 0.3 * np.cos(5 * th)) * np.cos(th), (1 + 0.3 * np.cos(5 * th)) * np.sin(th)]

def crust(S):
    """2D crust: Delaunay edges between samples that survive adding the Voronoi vertices."""
    V = Voronoi(S).vertices
    T = Delaunay(np.r_[S, V]).simplices
    E = set()
    for tri in T:
        for a, b in [(tri[0], tri[1]), (tri[1], tri[2]), (tri[0], tri[2])]:
            if a < len(S) and b < len(S):
                E.add((min(a, b), max(a, b)))
    return E

E = crust(S)
deg = np.bincount(np.array(list(E)).ravel(), minlength=len(S))
order = np.argsort(th)                           # true cyclic order along the curve
pos = np.empty(len(S), int); pos[order] = np.arange(len(S))
good = sum((pos[a] - pos[b]) % len(S) in (1, len(S) - 1) for a, b in E)
print(f"{len(E)} crust edges, all degrees 2: {np.all(deg == 2)}, "
      f"edges joining consecutive samples: {good}")

# The naive alternative: join each sample to its 2 nearest neighbours
D = np.linalg.norm(S[:, None] - S[None], axis=-1); np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)
nn2 = np.argsort(D, axis=1)[:, :2]
bad = sum((pos[i] - pos[j]) % len(S) not in (1, len(S) - 1) for i in range(len(S)) for j in nn2[i])
print("2-nearest-neighbour graph: wrong links =", bad)

The crust returns 200 edges, every sample has degree 2, and all 200 edges join consecutive samples: a correct closed polygon.

The nearest-neighbour rule makes 98 wrong links out of 400. With random sampling, both nearest neighbours of a point often lie on the same side of it along the curve.

Meshes

A surface reconstruction is usually delivered as a triangle mesh.

Definition 6.4 — Triangle mesh A triangle mesh is a set of vertices in \mathbb{R}^3 and of triangles on these vertices, glued along shared edges.

It is a closed 2-manifold when every edge belongs to exactly two triangles and the triangles around each vertex form a single fan. It is orientable when its triangles can be ordered consistently, so that each shared edge is traversed in opposite directions by its two triangles. A closed orientable surface has a genus g, its number of handles: 0 for a sphere, 1 for a torus.

Proposition 6.5 — Counting with the Euler formula A closed orientable triangle mesh with V vertices, E edges and F triangles satisfies the Euler formula V - E + F = 2 - 2g. Consequently E = 3V - 6 + 6g, \qquad F = 2V - 4 + 4g , and the average vertex degree 2E / V tends to 6 when V is large.

Proof. Each triangle has three edges and each edge borders two triangles, so 3F = 2E.

Substitute F = 2E/3 in the Euler formula to get V - E/3 = 2 - 2g, that is E = 3V - 6 + 6g. Then F = 2E/3 gives the second formula. ∎

The icosahedron (V = 12, g = 0) has E = 30 and F = 20. A mesh of a million vertices has about two million triangles: a useful rule for budgeting memory.

Note 6.6 — Delaunay-based surface reconstruction In 3D the crust generalises to surfaces: the Voronoi cells of a dense sample are long and thin along the normal, and their extreme vertices, the poles, approximate the medial axis.

  • The cocone algorithm (Amenta, Choi, Dey & Leekha, 2002) and the power crust (Amenta, Choi & Kolluri, 2001) select Delaunay triangles or Voronoi faces using these poles, with guarantees for \varepsilon-samples.
  • α-shapes (Edelsbrunner & Mücke, 1994) keep the Delaunay simplices with an empty circumscribed ball of radius at most \alpha.
  • Ball pivoting (Bernardini et al., 1999) rolls a ball of radius \rho over the samples and creates a triangle whenever it rests on three of them.

These methods interpolate the samples. This is accurate on clean data, but noise and holes produce rough or incomplete meshes, which motivates the implicit methods below.

Implicit surfaces

An implicit method computes a function f : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R} that is negative inside and positive outside, and extracts the surface as its zero set. Noise is averaged and holes are filled by the smoothness of f.

Definition 6.7 — Signed distance to tangent planes (Hoppe et al., 1992) Estimate an oriented tangent plane (o_i, n_i) at each sample by PCA, and orient the normals by propagation (Theorem 2.5, Proposition 2.6). For a query x, let o_i be the closest plane centre and set f(x) = n_i^\top (x - o_i) .

This approximates the signed distance near the surface; it is left undefined far from the samples, so that holes are not closed arbitrarily.

Definition 6.8 — Marching cubes (Lorensen & Cline, 1987) Sample f at the vertices of a regular grid. In each cube, the signs of f at its 8 corners select one of 2^8 = 256 cases (15 up to symmetries) from a table of triangle patterns. The vertices of the triangles lie on the cube edges where the sign changes, at the zero of the linear interpolation: x = x_a + \frac{f(x_a)}{f(x_a) - f(x_b)}\, (x_b - x_a).

Some face configurations are ambiguous and can leave holes; the asymptotic decider (Nielson & Hamann, 1991) or a split of each cube into tetrahedra removes them.

Definition 6.9 — RBF and MLS implicit surfaces

  • Radial basis functions (Carr et al., 2001): f(x) = \sum_j w_j\, \varphi(\|x - c_j\|) + \pi(x) with \pi a polynomial of low degree. The weights solve the interpolation conditions f(x_i) = 0 at the samples and f(x_i \pm \delta n_i) = \pm \delta at off-surface points.
  • Implicit moving least squares: with a Gaussian weight \theta of width h, f(x) = \frac{\sum_i \theta(\|x - x_i\|)\, n_i^\top (x - x_i)}{\sum_i \theta(\|x - x_i\|)} , a smooth blend of the tangent planes of Definition 6.7 (Kolluri, 2008). It is the surface model of the IMLS-SLAM odometry (Deschaud, 2018).

Poisson surface reconstruction

Kazhdan, Bolitho and Hoppe (2006) reconstruct the indicator function \chi of the solid (1 inside, 0 outside) from oriented samples. The key observation is that the gradient of a smoothed indicator is a smoothed field of normals.

Proposition 6.10 — The gradient of the smoothed indicator Let M be a solid with smooth boundary \partial M, inward unit normal N(p), and let \tilde F be a smooth, rapidly decaying filter. Then \nabla \big( \chi_M * \tilde F \big)(q) = \int_{\partial M} \tilde F(q - p)\, N(p)\, \mathrm{d}p .

Proof. Differentiate under the integral, \nabla_q (\chi_M * \tilde F)(q) = \int_M \nabla_q \tilde F(q - p)\, \mathrm{d}p , and use \nabla_q \tilde F(q - p) = -\nabla_p \tilde F(q - p).

The divergence theorem, applied to each component, turns the integral of -\nabla_p \tilde F(q - p) over M into the boundary integral of -\tilde F(q - p) times the outward normal. The outward normal is -N. ∎

The boundary integral is approximated from the samples s_i with normals N_i and area weights a_i (the surface each sample represents): V(q) = \sum_i a_i\, \tilde F(q - s_i)\, N_i \approx \nabla(\chi_M * \tilde F)(q) .

The field V is in general not a gradient, so we look for the function whose gradient is closest to it.

Theorem 6.11 — Poisson equation On a domain \Omega, the minimisers of J(\chi) = \int_\Omega \|\nabla \chi - V\|^2 over functions with prescribed boundary values are exactly the solutions of \Delta \chi = \nabla \cdot V \ \text{ in } \Omega .

Proof. J is convex. For a test function \varphi vanishing on \partial\Omega, expand J(\chi + t\varphi) and differentiate at t = 0: \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} J(\chi + t \varphi)\Big|_{t=0} = 2 \int_\Omega (\nabla\chi - V) \cdot \nabla\varphi = -2 \int_\Omega \varphi\, \big( \Delta \chi - \nabla \cdot V \big), by integration by parts, the boundary term vanishing with \varphi.

A convex functional is minimal exactly where this derivative is zero for every \varphi, that is where \Delta\chi - \nabla\cdot V = 0. ∎

Key result — Poisson reconstruction \Delta \chi = \nabla \cdot V, \qquad \text{surface} = \{ \chi = \gamma \}, \quad \gamma = \text{mean of } \chi \text{ at the samples}

In practice the equation is discretised on an adaptive octree with B-spline bases, which makes the linear system sparse and its size proportional to the surface, not the volume. The iso-value \gamma is the average of \chi at the samples, which absorbs any global scaling of V.

Screened Poisson reconstruction (Kazhdan & Hoppe, 2013) adds a term pulling \chi towards \gamma at the samples, which reduces the over-smoothing of thin details.

Code 6.12 — Poisson reconstruction in 2D Four hundred noisy oriented samples of the flower curve are splatted with a Gaussian filter on a 161 \times 161 grid. The Poisson equation is solved with zero boundary values by a sparse solver, and the curve is extracted at the iso-value.

import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp
from scipy.sparse.linalg import spsolve
import contourpy

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Oriented samples of the "flower" r = 1 + 0.3 cos(5 theta), with noise
th = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 400))
r, dr = 1 + 0.3 * np.cos(5 * th), -1.5 * np.sin(5 * th)
P = np.c_[r * np.cos(th), r * np.sin(th)]
T = np.c_[dr * np.cos(th) - r * np.sin(th), dr * np.sin(th) + r * np.cos(th)]
Nout = np.c_[T[:, 1], -T[:, 0]] / np.linalg.norm(T, axis=1, keepdims=True)
P += 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(P.shape)
ds = 0.5 * (np.linalg.norm(np.roll(P, -1, 0) - P, axis=1)
            + np.linalg.norm(np.roll(P, 1, 0) - P, axis=1))   # arc length per sample

# 1. Splat the inward normals on a grid: V ~ grad(indicator)
m, L = 161, 2.0
g = np.linspace(-L, L, m); h = g[1] - g[0]
XX, YY = np.meshgrid(g, g, indexing="ij")
sig = 1.5 * h
V = np.zeros((m, m, 2))
for p, n, w in zip(P, -Nout, ds):
    G = np.exp(-((XX - p[0]) ** 2 + (YY - p[1]) ** 2) / (2 * sig ** 2)) / (2 * np.pi * sig ** 2)
    V += w * G[..., None] * n

# 2. Solve the Poisson equation  Laplacian(chi) = div V, chi = 0 on the border
div = np.gradient(V[..., 0], h, axis=0) + np.gradient(V[..., 1], h, axis=1)
I = sp.identity(m - 2)
D2 = sp.diags([1, -2, 1], [-1, 0, 1], shape=(m - 2, m - 2), dtype=float) / h ** 2
Lap = sp.kron(D2, I) + sp.kron(I, D2)
chi = np.zeros((m, m))
chi[1:-1, 1:-1] = spsolve(Lap.tocsc(), div[1:-1, 1:-1].ravel()).reshape(m - 2, m - 2)

# 3. Iso-value = mean of chi at the samples, then extract the curve
ij = np.clip(np.round((P + L) / h).astype(int), 0, m - 1)
iso = chi[ij[:, 0], ij[:, 1]].mean()
line = max(contourpy.contour_generator(XX, YY, chi).lines(iso), key=len)
x, y = line[:, 0], line[:, 1]
area = 0.5 * abs(np.dot(x, np.roll(y, -1)) - np.dot(y, np.roll(x, -1)))
print(f"chi inside ~ {chi[m // 2, m // 2]:.3f}, outside ~ {chi[5, 5]:.3f}, iso = {iso:.3f}")
print(f"area of the reconstruction {area:.4f} vs exact {np.pi * 1.045:.4f}")
rho = np.hypot(x, y); phi = np.arctan2(y, x)
print(f"max radial error: {np.max(np.abs(rho - (1 + 0.3 * np.cos(5 * phi)))):.4f}")

The solution is about 1.38 inside and 0 outside, not 1: noise lengthens the sample spacing and so inflates the weights a_i. Choosing the iso-value at the samples (0.651, about half the jump) cancels this global scale.

The reconstruction is accurate: \text{area } 3.2832 \ \text{vs exact } 3.2830, \qquad \text{max radial error } 0.027 .

The radial error is below three times the noise level of 1 cm.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Sampling density must follow the local feature size; \varepsilon-samples make reconstruction provable.
  • The crust keeps the Delaunay edges that survive the insertion of the Voronoi vertices, and recovers curves exactly from dense samples.
  • Closed triangle meshes satisfy V - E + F = 2 - 2g, hence about 2V triangles.
  • Delaunay-based methods interpolate; implicit methods (signed distances, RBF, MLS) approximate, and marching cubes extracts the zero set.
  • Poisson reconstruction solves \Delta\chi = \nabla\cdot V from a field of splatted normals and extracts the iso-value at the samples.

7. Rendering point clouds and meshes

Every step of the pipeline is checked by looking at the data, and many applications end with an image: inspection, virtual visits, simulation. This chapter explains how a mesh or a cloud becomes pixels, why a naive point display fails, and how splatting and level-of-detail hierarchies fix it.

Definition 7.1 — Pinhole projection A camera with rotation R, translation t, focal length f (in pixels) and principal point (u_0, v_0) maps a world point x to camera coordinates (X, Y, Z) = R x + t, then to the pixel u = f \frac{X}{Z} + u_0, \qquad v = f \frac{Y}{Z} + v_0 .

The depth Z > 0 is kept for visibility. In matrix form this is K [R \mid t] followed by division by Z, as in 3D Computer Vision.

Proposition 7.2 — Projected size A small disk of radius \rho at depth Z, facing the camera, projects to a disk of radius about r_{\text{px}} \approx \frac{f \rho}{Z} pixels. A disk tilted by an angle \beta from the viewing direction projects to an ellipse with axes f\rho/Z and f\rho \cos\beta / Z.

Proof. Two points at distance \rho in a plane of constant depth Z are mapped f\rho/Z pixels apart by Definition 7.1. Tilting the disk shortens one of its diameters by \cos\beta in the direction of view, to first order in \rho / Z. ∎

Definition 7.3 — The z-buffer (Catmull, 1974) Keep, for every pixel, the smallest depth drawn so far and the corresponding colour. Each primitive is rasterised into the pixels it covers; a pixel is overwritten only if the new depth is smaller.

The result does not depend on the drawing order, and the cost is proportional to the number of pixels covered by all primitives.

Meshes: rasterisation and shading

A triangle is rasterised by testing pixel centres against its three edges. Inside the triangle, attributes such as depth, colour and normal are interpolated from the vertices.

Proposition 7.4 — Perspective-correct interpolation On the image of a planar triangle, 1/Z is an affine function of the pixel coordinates (u, v). So is a/Z for any attribute a that is affine on the triangle in space. The attribute at a pixel is therefore the ratio of two screen-space interpolations: a = \frac{\operatorname{interp}(a / Z)}{\operatorname{interp}(1 / Z)} .

Proof. The plane of the triangle satisfies \alpha X + \beta Y + \gamma Z = \delta with \delta \neq 0 when the plane avoids the camera centre.

Substitute X = (u - u_0) Z / f and Y = (v - v_0) Z / f, then divide by \delta Z: 1/Z is affine in (u, v).

An attribute affine in space is affine in (X, Y, Z); divided by Z, it becomes affine in X/Z, Y/Z and 1/Z, each of which is affine in (u, v). ∎

Interpolating a itself linearly on the screen makes textures swim on slanted surfaces.

Definition 7.5 — Local shading With unit normal n, unit direction to the light l, unit direction to the viewer e and the half-vector h = (l + e)/\|l + e\|, the Blinn–Phong model gives the intensity I = k_a + k_d \max(0, n^\top l) + k_s \max(0, n^\top h)^{s} .

The three terms are the ambient, diffuse (Lambert) and specular parts; s controls the size of highlights. Phong (1975) introduced the specular term with the reflected light direction, and Blinn (1977) replaced it by the half-vector. Gouraud shading evaluates I at the vertices and interpolates it; Phong shading interpolates the normal and evaluates I at each pixel, which keeps highlights sharp.

Shading needs oriented normals: this is why the orientation step of ch. 2 matters even when no surface is reconstructed.

Rendering points directly

A cloud can be rendered without a mesh by giving each point an extent on the surface.

Definition 7.6 — Surfels and splatting (Pfister et al., 2000; Zwicker et al., 2001) A surfel is a point with a normal, a radius \rho and a colour: a small oriented disk approximating the surface around the sample.

Splatting draws each surfel as its projected footprint (Proposition 7.2) into the z-buffer. To cover the surface without holes, the disks must overlap: \rho is chosen from the local spacing, for example the distance to the k-th nearest neighbour.

Code 7.7 — Holes and show-through Three thousand random points on a unit sphere (normal = position) are seen from 4 m with f = 300 px on a 200 \times 200 image. Three renderings are compared: one pixel per point, square splats of a fixed radius based on the mean spacing, and splats whose radius is the distance to the 4th nearest neighbour.

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 3000 surfels on the unit sphere (position = normal), seen by a pinhole camera
N = 3000
X = rng.normal(size=(N, 3)); X /= np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
W, f, cam = 200, 300.0, np.array([0.0, 0.0, -4.0])   # image size, focal (px), centre
Z = X[:, 2] - cam[2]                                  # depth along the optical axis
u, v = f * X[:, 0] / Z + W / 2, f * X[:, 1] / Z + W / 2
back = ((X - cam) * X).sum(1) > 0                     # normal points away from the camera

def splat(radius_px):
    """Z-buffer: each point covers a square of half-width radius_px (in pixels)."""
    depth, who = np.full((W, W), np.inf), np.full((W, W), -1)
    for k in range(N):
        r = int(np.ceil(radius_px[k]))
        i0, i1 = max(int(v[k]) - r, 0), min(int(v[k]) + r + 1, W)
        j0, j1 = max(int(u[k]) - r, 0), min(int(u[k]) + r + 1, W)
        closer = Z[k] < depth[i0:i1, j0:j1]
        depth[i0:i1, j0:j1][closer] = Z[k]
        who[i0:i1, j0:j1][closer] = k
    return who

# True silhouette: pixels whose viewing ray meets the sphere
jj, ii = np.meshgrid(np.arange(W) + 0.5, np.arange(W) + 0.5)
d = np.stack([(jj - W / 2) / f, (ii - W / 2) / f, np.ones_like(jj)], -1)
d /= np.linalg.norm(d, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
inside = (d @ cam) ** 2 - (cam @ cam - 1) > 0

s = np.sqrt(4 * np.pi / N)                            # mean spacing if samples were regular
d4 = cKDTree(X).query(X, k=5)[0][:, -1]               # distance to the 4th neighbour
for name, rad in [("1-pixel points", np.zeros(N)),
                  ("fixed r = f s / 2z", f * s / (2 * Z)),
                  ("adaptive r = f d4 / z", f * d4 / Z)]:
    who = splat(rad)
    holes = np.mean(who[inside] < 0)
    seen = who[inside & (who >= 0)]
    print(f"{name:22s} holes {100 * holes:5.1f} % | pixels showing a back-facing "
          f"point {100 * np.mean(back[seen]):5.1f} %")

With one pixel per point most of the sphere is empty, and most visible pixels belong to its back, seen through the gaps. A fixed radius based on the mean spacing still leaves random gaps through which the back shows. Radii adapted to the local spacing close the surface.

Square splats of constant colour give a blocky image and aliasing at a distance. Gaussian kernels give smooth blending, and their projection has a closed form.

Proposition 7.8 — Projection of Gaussian splats (EWA, Zwicker et al., 2001) Let G_V be the Gaussian density of covariance V.

  • Under an affine map u = J x + c with J invertible, the image of G_V is, up to the constant factor |\det J|^{-1}, the Gaussian G_{J V J^\top} centred at c.
  • Gaussians compose under convolution: G_A * G_B = G_{A + B}.

Hence a surfel with a Gaussian kernel of covariance V, mapped to the screen by the local affine approximation J of the projection and filtered by a pixel low-pass filter G_I, is drawn with the Gaussian of covariance J V J^\top + I .

Proof. If x has density G_V then u = Jx + c is Gaussian with covariance J V J^\top (Proposition 0.6), and the change of variables gives the factor |\det J|^{-1}.

The convolution of two densities is the density of the sum of independent variables, and covariances of independent variables add. ∎

The term I guarantees a footprint of at least one pixel, which removes aliasing when many surfels fall in one pixel. The same formula, with learned Gaussians, is the core of 3D Gaussian splatting (ch. 8).

Level of detail

Large clouds hold billions of points, far more than the pixels of a screen.

Definition 7.9 — Bounding-sphere hierarchy (QSplat, Rusinkiewicz & Levoy, 2000) Build a tree whose leaves are the points and whose internal nodes store a bounding sphere of their children, with an average normal and colour. To render, traverse the tree from the root:

  • skip a node whose sphere is outside the view (frustum culling) or entirely back-facing;
  • draw a node as one splat when its projected radius is below a threshold of \tau pixels, or when it is a leaf;
  • otherwise visit its children.

The number of splats drawn depends on the screen area covered and on \tau, not on the size of the cloud. Lowering \tau while the camera is still refines the image progressively. Octree-based viewers stream the nodes from disk in the same way.

Note 7.10 — Quality and current practice

  • Overlapping splats are best blended: accumulate kernel-weighted colours and normals, then normalise and shade per pixel (deferred shading; Botsch et al., 2005).
  • Coarse meshes can be refined at render time; Phong tessellation (Boubekeur & Alexa, 2008) curves each triangle using its vertex normals, so silhouettes look smooth.
  • Neural rendering optimises the splats themselves against photographs; see ch. 8.

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Projection divides by depth: a splat of radius \rho covers about f\rho/Z pixels.
  • The z-buffer resolves visibility pixel by pixel, independently of the drawing order.
  • Perspective-correct interpolation interpolates a/Z and 1/Z; shading needs oriented normals.
  • Points drawn as pixels leave holes and show hidden surfaces; surfels with radii adapted to the local spacing close the surface.
  • Gaussian splats project to Gaussians of covariance JVJ^\top, plus I after the anti-aliasing filter.
  • Hierarchies of bounding spheres render huge clouds at a cost set by the screen, not by the data.

8. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It points to active research lines that extend each chapter of the course, with the questions that remain open. It is also a reservoir of papers for the course project.

Learned and certifiable registration, LiDAR odometry

Learning has entered registration at two levels. Learned descriptors replace FPFH in the pipeline of Definition 3.11. End-to-end networks go further: Deep Closest Point (Wang & Solomon, 2019) computes soft correspondences with attention and back-propagates through the SVD of Theorem 3.2.

Predator (Huang et al., 2021) focuses on scans with low overlap, and GeoTransformer (Qin et al., 2022) encodes pairwise distances and angles, which are invariant to rigid motions.

At the other end, TEASER (Yang, Shi & Carlone, 2021) minimises a truncated least-squares cost, robust to a large majority of outliers. A semidefinite relaxation can then certify that the solution found is globally optimal.

For mobile robots and mapping vehicles, registration becomes LiDAR odometry. LOAM (Zhang & Singh, 2014) registers edge and plane features. IMLS-SLAM (Deschaud, 2018) registers each scan to an implicit MLS surface of the map (Definition 6.9).

CT-ICP (Dellenbach, Deschaud, Jacquet & Goulette, 2022) estimates a pose at the beginning and at the end of each scan, which corrects the motion distortion of Note 1.10. KISS-ICP (Vizzo et al., 2023) shows that a carefully tuned point-to-point ICP with an adaptive threshold is competitive with far more complex systems.

Open questions include:

  • degenerate places (tunnels, corridors, open fields), detected through the rank of the matrix of Proposition 3.7 but not yet handled reliably;
  • moving objects, which violate the rigidity assumption;
  • learned descriptors that transfer between sensors of different resolutions (Proposition 1.6);
  • certifiable methods fast enough for real time.

Large-scale semantic segmentation

Outdoor scans contain billions of points. Superpoint graphs (Landrieu & Simonovsky, 2018) first partition the cloud into geometrically homogeneous parts, then classify the parts with a graph network. RandLA-Net (Hu et al., 2020) shows that random subsampling, combined with local feature aggregation, is enough to process large scenes efficiently.

Transformer architectures now lead most benchmarks; Point Transformer V3 (Wu et al., 2024) trades exact neighbourhoods for serialised point orderings to scale up.

Annotation is the bottleneck: labelling a scan point by point takes hours. Self-supervised pre-training on unlabelled clouds, by contrastive learning (PointContrast, Xie et al., 2020) or masked auto-encoding (Point-MAE, Pang et al., 2022), reduces the labels needed.

Domain shift between sensors, cities and seasons, long-tailed classes and calibrated uncertainty remain largely open. Work by Thomas, Deschaud, Goulette and coauthors (KPConv, 2019) and the Paris-Lille-3D data set (Roynard, Deschaud & Goulette, 2018) are reference points for this line.

Neural implicit surfaces

Occupancy Networks (Mescheder et al., 2019) and DeepSDF (Park et al., 2019) represent a shape by a neural network f_\theta(x), an occupancy probability or a signed distance, learned over collections of shapes. The surface is extracted by marching cubes (Definition 6.8).

A true signed distance satisfies the eikonal equation \|\nabla f\| = 1. Gropp and coauthors (2020) fit a network to a single raw cloud by asking f to vanish at the samples while penalising the deviation from this equation: \min_\theta \ \sum_i |f_\theta(x_i)| + \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_x \big( \|\nabla f_\theta(x)\| - 1 \big)^2 .

Shape As Points (Peng et al., 2021) makes Poisson reconstruction (Theorem 6.11) differentiable by solving it spectrally, so that the oriented points themselves can be learned.

Open questions: theoretical guarantees comparable to those of \varepsilon-samples, sharp features, and very large scenes.

Neural rendering and Gaussian splatting

NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020) represents a scene by a network mapping a position and a direction to a density and a colour, and renders it by integrating along rays. It produces photorealistic views but is slow to train and to render.

3D Gaussian splatting (Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler & Drettakis, 2023) returns to points. The scene is a cloud of anisotropic 3D Gaussians, initialised from the sparse cloud of structure-from-motion and projected with the EWA formula of Proposition 7.8. Each Gaussian i has a projected opacity \alpha_i and a colour c_i.

Definition 8.1 — Alpha compositing of sorted splats With the splats covering a pixel sorted from front to back, the colour of the pixel is C = \sum_{i} c_i\, \alpha_i \prod_{j < i} (1 - \alpha_j) .

This is differentiable in all the parameters of the Gaussians (position, covariance, opacity, colour), which are optimised by gradient descent so that the rendered images match the photographs.

Gaussian splatting renders in real time at high quality. Extracting accurate surfaces from it, controlling its memory footprint, and relighting are active questions; the point-based graphics of ch. 7 is its direct ancestor.

Generating and comparing point clouds

Generative models of point clouds, based on normalising flows (PointFlow, Yang et al., 2019) or diffusion (Luo & Hu, 2021), need a distance between clouds to train and to evaluate. Optimal transport gives the earth mover’s distance; the cheaper Chamfer distance is the most common choice.

Definition 8.2 — Chamfer distance For two finite clouds \mathcal{X} and \mathcal{Y}, d_{\mathrm{CD}}(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \min_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \|x - y\|^2 + \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \|x - y\|^2 .

It is computed with two kd-tree queries, but it is not a metric (no triangle inequality) and it is insensitive to how points are distributed within a region. The tools of Optimal Transport for Machine Learning and Geometric Data Analysis give finer comparisons.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Registration research splits between learned correspondences and certifiable robust estimation; LiDAR odometry adds continuous-time motion and scan-to-map registration.
  • Large-scale segmentation relies on efficient sampling, transformers and self-supervised pre-training; domain shift and labels are the open problems.
  • Neural implicit surfaces learn signed distances with eikonal regularisation; differentiable Poisson solvers bridge them with ch. 6.
  • Gaussian splatting is EWA splatting with learned Gaussians and alpha compositing.
  • Chamfer and transport distances compare clouds for generation and evaluation.

  1. ★ Depth error of a stereo rig. A stereo rig has f = 1000 px and baseline b = 0.3 m, and matches disparities to 0.25 px. Compute the depth error at 10 m and at 20 m. Which baseline gives 5 cm at 20 m?

    Solution: by Proposition 1.2, \delta z = z^2 \delta d / (f b), which gives 100 \times 0.25 / 300 \approx 8.3 cm at 10 m and four times more, 33 cm, at 20 m.

    Solving for the baseline: b = \frac{z^2\, \delta d}{f\, \delta z} = \frac{400 \times 0.25}{1000 \times 0.05} = 2\ \text{m}.

  2. ★ Phase-shift ambiguity. A phase-shift scanner modulates at 5 MHz. What is its ambiguity interval? It adds a second frequency of 4.5 MHz: over which range can the two phases together identify the distance?

    Solution: the interval is c / (2 f_m) = 3 \times 10^8 / 10^7 = 30 m. The pair of phases repeats only when both do, which happens with the beat frequency f_1 - f_2 = 0.5 MHz: the combined interval is \frac{c}{2 (f_1 - f_2)} = 300\ \text{m}.

  3. ★ Farthest point sampling by hand. On the line, take the points 0, 1, 2, 3, 10. Run FPS from 0 with k = 3 and compute the covering radius. Compare with the best possible radius with 3 centres anywhere on the line.

    Solution: FPS picks 0, then 10 (the farthest), then 3 (at distance 3 from the set \{0, 10\}). The points 1 and 2 are then at distance 1, so \rho = 1.

    Centres at 0.5, 2.5 and 10 give \rho^\star = 0.5. The ratio is exactly 2: the bound of Proposition 2.4 is tight.

  4. ★ A normal by hand. Compute the PCA normal and the surface variation of the four points (1, 0, 0), (-1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0.1), (0, -1, -0.1).

    Solution: the centroid is 0. The covariance has C_{xx} = C_{yy} = 0.5, C_{zz} = 0.005, C_{yz} = 0.05, and 0 elsewhere.

    The (y, z) block has trace 0.505 and determinant 0, so the eigenvalues are 0.5, 0.505, 0. The eigenvector of 0 solves 0.5 y + 0.05 z = 0: n = \frac{(0, -0.1, 1)}{\sqrt{1.01}}, \qquad \sigma = 0 . The four points lie exactly on the plane z = 0.1\, y.

  5. ★ RANSAC budgets. With p = 0.99, how many iterations are needed to detect a plane holding 20 % of the points with 3-point samples? A cylinder holding 20 % with samples of 2 oriented points? A cylinder holding 5 %?

    Solution: Theorem 3.12 gives N = 574 for the plane (w^3 = 0.008), N = 113 for the cylinder (w^2 = 0.04), and N = 1{,}840 for the cylinder at 5 %. Smaller minimal samples, made possible by normals, pay off quickly.

  6. ★ Pooling and invariance. For a shared map h, compare the pooled features \max_i h(x_i), \sum_i h(x_i) and \frac1n \sum_i h(x_i). Which are permutation invariant? Which are unchanged when every point is duplicated?

    Solution: all three are permutation invariant, since max, sum and mean are symmetric. Duplicating every point leaves the max and the mean unchanged and doubles the sum. Max and mean are therefore insensitive to the sampling density, which suits clouds whose density varies (ch. 1).

  7. ★ Euler characteristic. A closed orientable triangle mesh has V = 10{,}000 and F = 20{,}000. What is its genus? Same question with V = 5{,}000 and F = 9{,}996.

    Solution: E = 3F/2 = 30{,}000, so V - E + F = 0 = 2 - 2g and g = 1, a torus. In the second case E = 14{,}994 and V - E + F = 2, so g = 0, a sphere (Proposition 6.5).

  8. ★ Perspective-correct interpolation. A segment goes from a point A at depth 1 (attribute a = 0) to a point B at depth 3 (a = 1). Which value of a appears at the midpoint of its image?

    Solution: interpolate 1/Z and a/Z on the screen (Proposition 7.4): 1/Z = (1 + 1/3)/2 = 2/3 and a/Z = (0 + 1/3)/2 = 1/6, so a = \frac{1/6}{2/3} = 0.25 . The image midpoint shows the point a quarter of the way along the segment, at depth 1.5, not the true midpoint.

  9. ★★ Optimal neighbourhood radius. Using Proposition 2.8, compute the optimal radius for a scanner noise of 5 mm on a surface with radius of curvature 2 m, and the corresponding value of \lambda_3 / \lambda_1. Why does a real implementation also need a lower bound on the number of points?

    Solution: with \kappa = 0.5 m⁻¹ and \sigma = 0.005 m, r^\star = 48^{1/4} \sqrt{0.01} \approx 0.26 m.

    At the optimum, ar^2 = b/r^2, so the ratio is 2\sqrt{ab} = 2 \kappa \sigma / \sqrt{3} \approx 0.0029. The proposition assumes uniform dense sampling; with few points the eigenvalues are themselves noisy, so the radius must also contain enough points (Mitra & Nguyen, 2003).

  10. ★★ Statistical outlier removal (Python). Generate a 2 m × 2 m noisy plane of 4,750 points and 250 uniform outliers in a box around it. Apply the filter of Definition 2.12 with 8 neighbours and \alpha \in \{1, 2, 3\}, and report precision and recall.

    Solution:

    import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
plane = np.c_[rng.uniform(0, 2, (4750, 2)), 0.005 * rng.standard_normal(4750)]
noise = rng.uniform([0, 0, -0.5], [2, 2, 0.5], (250, 3))       # 5 % outliers
X = np.r_[plane, noise]
is_out = np.r_[np.zeros(4750, bool), np.ones(250, bool)]

d, _ = cKDTree(X).query(X, k=9)                  # itself + 8 neighbours
dbar = d[:, 1:].mean(1)                          # mean distance to the 8 neighbours
mu, s = dbar.mean(), dbar.std()
for alpha in (1.0, 2.0, 3.0):
    removed = dbar > mu + alpha * s
    prec = (removed & is_out).sum() / max(removed.sum(), 1)
    rec = (removed & is_out).sum() / is_out.sum()

  11. ★★ Orienting normals by a spanning tree (Python). Implement Proposition 2.6: PCA normals with random signs, a minimum spanning tree of the 10-nearest-neighbour graph with weights 1 - |n_i^\top n_j|, and propagation from the highest point. Test on a sphere and on a thin plate whose two faces are 2 cm apart.

    Solution:

    import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree
from scipy.sparse import coo_matrix
from scipy.sparse.csgraph import minimum_spanning_tree, breadth_first_order

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def orient(X, k=10):
    """PCA normals with random signs, then Hoppe-style propagation along an MST."""
    _, idx = cKDTree(X).query(X, k=k)
    N = np.zeros_like(X)
    for i, nb in enumerate(idx):
        Q = X[nb] - X[nb].mean(0)
        N[i] = np.linalg.eigh(Q.T @ Q)[1][:, 0]
    N *= rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], (len(X), 1))                  # unknown signs
    rows = np.repeat(np.arange(len(X)), k - 1); cols = idx[:, 1:].ravel()
    w = 1 - np.abs(np.einsum("ij,ij->i", N[rows], N[cols])) + 1e-9
    T = minimum_spanning_tree(coo_matrix((w, (rows, cols)), shape=(len(X),) * 2))
    seed = np.argmax(X[:, 2])                                  # highest point: normal up
    if N[seed, 2] < 0:
        N[seed] *= -1
    order, pred = breadth_first_order(T, seed, directed=False)
    for v in order[1:]:
        if N[v] @ N[pred[v]] < 0:
            N[v] *= -1
    return N

S = rng.normal(size=(3000, 3)); S /= np.linalg.norm(S, axis=1, keepdims=True)
print("sphere: outward normals", np.mean(np.einsum("ij,ij->i", orient(S), S) > 0))

# A thin plate: two parallel faces 2 cm apart, sampled every ~3 cm
u = rng.uniform(0, 1, (1500, 2))
P = np.r_[np.c_[u, np.zeros(1500)], np.c_[rng.uniform(0, 1, (1500, 2)), np.full(1500, 0.02)]]
out = np.r_[-np.ones(1500), np.ones(1500)]                     # true outward n_z
Np = orient(P)
print("thin plate: correct normals", np.mean(np.sign(Np[:, 2]) == out))

    On the sphere, 100 % of the normals point outwards. On the plate only 49.6 % are correct: the neighbourhood graph joins the two faces, so propagation gives both the same sign and one face is entirely wrong. The fix is a neighbourhood smaller than the thickness, or the sensor position.

  12. ★★ The best rotation in the plane. Show that for 2D point sets the minimum of E in Theorem 3.2 is \sum_i \|p_i'\|^2 + \sum_i \|q_i'\|^2 - 2 \sqrt{(H_{xx} + H_{yy})^2 + (H_{xy} - H_{yx})^2} , and evaluate it on Example 3.3.

    Solution: by the proof of Theorem 3.2 we maximise A \cos\theta + B \sin\theta with A = H_{xx} + H_{yy} and B = H_{xy} - H_{yx}. Its maximum is \sqrt{A^2 + B^2} by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, at \theta^\star = \operatorname{atan2}(B, A).

  13. ★★ Point-to-plane ICP on a plane. Suppose all target normals are equal to n and the source points span a plane orthogonal to n. Show that the matrix \sum_i a_i a_i^\top of Proposition 3.7 has rank exactly 3, and describe its kernel.

    Solution: a_i^\top \xi = p_i^\top (n \times \omega) + n^\top t.

    It vanishes for every i when \omega is parallel to n and t is orthogonal to n: a 3-dimensional kernel (rotation about n, translations in the plane).

    Conversely, if a_i^\top \xi = 0 for all i, the affine function p \mapsto p^\top (n \times \omega) + n^\top t vanishes on the plane.

    Since n \times \omega is orthogonal to n, this forces it to be 0, then n^\top t = 0. The kernel is exactly the space above, so the rank is 6 - 3 = 3.

  14. ★★ Weights of robust ICP. Write the Huber and Cauchy functions as \rho(r) = \phi(r^2), check that \phi is concave, and compute the IRLS weights of Proposition 3.10. How much does a residual of 3c count relative to a perfect match?

    Solution: for Huber, \phi(s) = s/2 for s \le c^2 and c\sqrt{s} - c^2/2 beyond; for Cauchy, \phi(s) = \frac{c^2}{2} \log(1 + s / c^2). Both are concave and non-decreasing.   16. ★★ The budget of a Hough transform. For a 20 m façade and angle cells of 0.5°, over how many centimetres of \rho do its votes spread (Proposition 5.7)? How many votes do 10^7 points cast over a hemisphere of normals?

    Solution: 20 \times 0.0087 / 2 \approx 8.7 cm, plus the noise. The hemisphere has 720 \times 180 = 129{,}600 angle cells, hence about 1.3 \times 10^{12} votes. This is why practical detectors use RANSAC, randomised Hough transforms or votes restricted by normals: a point with a known normal votes for a single angle cell.

  17. ★★ Screened Poisson. Add to the functional of Theorem 6.11 the term \alpha \int_\Omega w\, (\chi - \gamma)^2, where w \ge 0 concentrates near the samples. Derive the new Euler–Lagrange equation and explain its effect.

    Solution: the first variation gains 2\alpha \int_\Omega w (\chi - \gamma) \varphi, so the equation becomes \Delta \chi - \alpha\, w\, (\chi - \gamma) = \nabla \cdot V . Near the samples, \chi is pulled towards the iso-value \gamma, so the extracted surface passes closer to the data. This is the screened Poisson equation of Kazhdan and Hoppe (2013), which preserves thin details that plain Poisson smooths out.

  18. ★★★ The basin of convergence of ICP. Using Code 3.8, measure the success rate of point-to-point and point-to-plane ICP (final rotation error below 1°) as a function of the initial rotation angle, from 10° to 90°, and of the overlap, obtained by cropping the source. Propose and test a strategy that enlarges the basin.

    Hints and expected result: run 20 random axes per angle. Both variants succeed at small angles, and the success rate drops beyond a few tens of degrees on this bumpy patch; reducing the overlap makes the drop earlier unless pairs are trimmed (Definition 3.9).

    Multi-start over a grid of initial rotations, coarse-to-fine subsampling (Definition 2.3), or a global initialisation by FPFH and RANSAC (Definition 3.11) all enlarge the basin. Report a curve of success rate against angle for each strategy.

  19. ★★★ From EWA to Gaussian splatting. A 3D Gaussian of covariance \Sigma is seen by a camera of rotation W, with J the Jacobian of the perspective map at its centre. Show that its screen footprint has covariance J W \Sigma W^\top J^\top, the upper 2 \times 2 block being kept. Then write a small 2D experiment that fits a set of 2D Gaussians to a target image by gradient descent on Definition 8.1.

    Hints and expected result: the first part is Proposition 7.8 applied twice: the rotation W maps \Sigma to W \Sigma W^\top, and the local affine approximation of the projection maps it again by J. For the experiment, in PyTorch, parametrise each covariance as L L^\top with L lower triangular, so that it stays positive semi-definite.

    Sort the Gaussians by a learned depth, composite them, and minimise the squared error to a synthetic image of a few disks. With about 50 Gaussians the error should fall steadily and the disks appear; Kerbl et al. (2023) add densification and pruning of Gaussians, which you can imitate.

Projects and assessment

The official assessment (master-mva.com) is a project based on a research paper, in four steps: choose a paper from the instructors’ proposals, implement and test its algorithm(s), analyse the algorithm and its results and propose variants when relevant, and hand in a document explaining the method and the results together with the code. A student who fails may take a new paper from a new list.

The Université Paris-Saclay course detail describes the assessment as partial continuous assessment; no weights are displayed. The solo subjects below follow the four official steps on named papers. The group subjects train the same skills at a larger scale. Every subject is evaluated with the rubric at the end of this section, on its stated success criterion; the written exam is a self-assessment proposal, not part of the official assessment.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: Poisson surface reconstruction. Reproduce Kazhdan, Bolitho and Hoppe (2006) on synthetic shapes.
    • Question: how does accuracy depend on noise, sampling density and grid resolution, compared with the signed distance of Hoppe et al. (1992)?
    • Work: extend Code 6.12 to 3D on a regular grid of 64^3 to 128^3 cells; extract the surface with marching cubes (Definition 6.8, implemented or taken from a library); test on a sphere, a torus and a union of boxes with 0–2 % noise.
    • Success criterion: Chamfer distance (Definition 8.2) to the true surface below 1 % of the bounding-box diagonal at 1 % noise, and a curve of error against noise for both methods.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters 2 and 6.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: efficient RANSAC. Reimplement Schnabel, Wahl and Klein (2007).
    • Work: octree-localised sampling (Proposition 5.5), planes, spheres and cylinders from oriented points (Propositions 5.2–5.3), inlier connectivity, and plain sequential RANSAC as a baseline.
    • Data: synthetic scenes of 10^5 to 10^6 points (a room with pipes, a tank, columns) with clutter, where the true primitives are known.
    • Success criterion: every primitive detected with at least 90 % of its points, and a speed-up of at least 10 over the baseline at 10^6 points.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 2 and 5.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: Generalized-ICP. Reimplement Segal, Haehnel and Thrun (2009) and compare it with point-to-point and point-to-plane ICP.
    • Work: local covariances from ch. 2, the GICP cost minimised by Gauss–Newton on rigid motions, and two scans simulated from different poses with Code 1.7.
    • Success criterion: success-rate curves against the initial rotation (as in Exercise 18) and final errors for the three variants, with the paper’s qualitative ordering confirmed or explained.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 1, 2 and 3.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] LiDAR odometry on simulated drives. Build a scan-to-map odometry in the spirit of IMLS-SLAM (Deschaud, 2018) and CT-ICP (Dellenbach et al., 2022).
    • Work: a simulator of a vehicle driving a synthetic street, extending Code 1.7 with motion distortion; scan-to-map point-to-plane ICP on a voxel map; per-point motion compensation.
    • Split: one member writes the simulator and the ground truth, one the registration and the map, one the motion compensation and the evaluation.
    • Success criterion: drift below 1 % of the distance travelled on a 200 m loop with 2 cm noise; an ablation without motion compensation.
    • Deliverables: code, a 6–8 page report, a 15-minute talk. Chapters 1, 2 and 3.
  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Semantic segmentation of an urban scan. Compare multi-scale features with a random forest (Weinmann et al., 2015; Hackel, Wegner & Schindler, 2016) with a point network (PointNet++ or KPConv, Thomas et al., 2019).
    • Data: a public urban LiDAR data set such as Paris-Lille-3D (Roynard, Deschaud & Goulette, 2018), with training and test areas kept apart; the synthetic scenes of Code 4.6 for debugging.
    • Split: features and forest; network (free GPU tier); graph-cut regularisation (Theorem 4.4) and evaluation.
    • Success criterion: per-class IoU and mIoU on the held-out area for the three pipelines, with an analysis of the classes where each one fails.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 2 and 4.
  3. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] A point-based renderer with level of detail. Implement the hierarchy of QSplat (Rusinkiewicz & Levoy, 2000) with Gaussian splats (Proposition 7.8) and normalised blending.
    • Split: hierarchy and traversal; splatting, blending and shading; evaluation and an interactive viewer.
    • Success criterion: images without holes or show-through (as measured in Code 7.7), and curves of image error against a full-resolution reference and of the number of splats against the threshold \tau.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk with a live demonstration. Chapters 2 and 7.

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 2 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic devices, four exercises.

  1. Question (4 points). Compare triangulation and time-of-flight sensors: give the range formula of each and the depth error of triangulation, and say which you would use to scan a 50 m façade, and why. Expected: z = fb/d and \delta z \approx z^2 \delta d / (fb); pulsed r = c\tau/2 and phase shift with its ambiguity interval; for 50 m the quadratic growth rules out short-baseline triangulation, so a pulsed or multi-frequency phase-shift scanner is the answer.

  2. Question (6 points). (a) Align the points (0, 0), (2, 0), (0, 1) to the points (3, 1), (3, −1), (4, 1) in closed form. (b) A global registration finds 40 % of correct matches: how many 3-match RANSAC draws give a 99 % success probability? Expected: centroids (2/3, 1/3) and (10/3, 1/3); H_{xx} + H_{yy} = 0 and H_{xy} - H_{yx} = -10/3, so \theta^\star = -90° and t^\star = (3, 1); (b) N = 70 by Theorem 3.12.

  3. Question (5 points). Prove that the costs of ICP are non-increasing, and explain why ICP may nevertheless return a wrong alignment. Expected: the two inequalities of Theorem 3.5, one for matching and one for alignment; the limit is only a local minimum, whose basin depends on the initial rotation, the overlap and the symmetries of the scene; remedies are a global initialisation and robust weights.

  4. Question (5 points). State the variational problem of Poisson reconstruction, derive its Euler–Lagrange equation, and explain why normals must be consistently oriented and how the iso-value is chosen. Expected: minimise the integral of \|\nabla\chi - V\|^2, first variation and integration by parts giving \Delta\chi = \nabla\cdot V; a flipped normal reverses its contribution to V and creates spurious surface sheets; the iso-value is the mean of \chi at the samples, which absorbs the scale of V.
