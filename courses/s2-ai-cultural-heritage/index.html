Audience: a student with a good M1 in mathematics, computer science or physics who knows the basics of image processing and neural networks, and wants to apply them to paintings and drawings, where every image has a physical meaning.

Empirical first: photograph a painting in visible light and in the near infrared, and the two images disagree. In the infrared the paint becomes partly transparent and the artist’s underdrawing appears (ch. 1). Overlay the two images and nothing matches to the pixel, because each camera has its own lens distortion. After a sub-pixel registration (ch. 2), the pixels whose infrared value the colour does not explain point to underdrawing, retouching or a change of mind (ch. 3).

Art history and conservation science now collect gigabytes, sometimes terabytes, of images of a single painting, in several physical modalities. This course teaches the imaging physics behind those images and the computer vision that turns them into evidence: registration, density estimation, segmentation of cracks and canvas weave, pretrained embeddings and image-to-image networks. The course is given by Robert G. Erdmann (University of Amsterdam), as listed on master-mva.com.

Related MVA pages: 3D Computer Vision for camera geometry, Sub-Pixel Image Processing for precise shifts, Remote Sensing Data for multispectral imaging at another scale, and Deep Learning for Image Restoration and Synthesis for image-to-image networks.

0. Review: images, filters, Fourier analysis, least squares and probability

The course combines the physics of imaging, classical image processing and deep learning. This review collects the M1-level tools that later chapters use without proof: linear light, convolution, the Fourier transform, the singular value decomposition, conditional expectation and the vocabulary of convolutional networks.

0.1 Images and linear light

Definition 0.1 — Digital image, pixel pitch A digital image with c channels is an array I : \{0, \dots, H-1\} \times \{0, \dots, W-1\} \to \mathbb{R}^{c}. Its pixel pitch \Delta is the physical size of one pixel on the object, in millimetres or micrometres.

Physical models, such as the attenuation of light by a layer, act on quantities proportional to energy. Camera files are usually gamma-encoded: a stored sRGB value C \in [0, 1] is decoded to linear light by C_{\mathrm{lin}} = \begin{cases} C / 12.92, & C \le 0.04045, \\ \big((C + 0.055)/1.055\big)^{2.4}, & C > 0.04045. \end{cases} Every model of ch. 1 and ch. 3 assumes linear values.

0.2 Convolution, correlation and Gaussian smoothing

Definition 0.2 — Convolution and cross-correlation For images f, g on \mathbb{Z}^2, (f * g)(x) = \sum_{y} f(y)\, g(x - y), \qquad (f \star g)(t) = \sum_{x} f(x)\, g(x + t). The Gaussian kernel of scale \sigma is G_\sigma(x) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1}\, e^{-|x|^2 / (2\sigma^2)} .

Three facts are used constantly.

  • The Gaussian is separable: a 2-D smoothing is two 1-D smoothings, one per axis.
  • Smoothings compose: G_\sigma * G_\tau = G_{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}}.
  • Derivatives commute with convolution, so a smoothed derivative is a convolution with a derivative of the Gaussian: \partial_i (G_\sigma * I) = (\partial_i G_\sigma) * I .

0.3 The discrete Fourier transform

Definition 0.3 — DFT For a signal f of length N, the discrete Fourier transform is \hat f(k) = \sum_{x=0}^{N-1} f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i k x / N}, \qquad k = 0, \dots, N - 1 . The fast Fourier transform computes it in O(N \log N) operations; in 2-D it acts on each axis in turn.

Theorem 0.4 — Convolution and shift theorems With indices taken modulo N (circular convolution and shifts): \widehat{f * g} = \hat f\, \hat g, \qquad \widehat{f(\cdot - t)}(k) = e^{-2\pi i k t / N}\, \hat f(k).

Proof.

  • Shift: substitute y = x - t in the sum defining the transform of f(\cdot - t); the factor e^{-2\pi i k t/N} comes out of the sum.
  • Convolution: write \widehat{f*g}(k) = \sum_y f(y) \sum_x g(x - y)\, e^{-2\pi i k x/N} . By the shift identity, the inner sum is e^{-2\pi i k y / N}\, \hat g(k), and the outer sum gives \hat f(k). ∎

The shift theorem is the basis of phase correlation in ch. 2.5, and the convolution theorem makes large correlations cheap.

0.4 Least squares and the SVD

Proposition 0.5 — Homogeneous least squares Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} have singular values \sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_n and right singular vectors v_1, \dots, v_n. Then \min_{\|x\| = 1} \|A x\| = \sigma_n, \quad \text{attained at } x = v_n .

Proof. Write A = U \Sigma V^\top and y = V^\top x, so that \|y\| = 1. Then \|Ax\|^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i^2 y_i^2 \ge \sigma_n^2 \sum_i y_i^2 = \sigma_n^2 , with equality for y = e_n, that is x = v_n. ∎

The ordinary problem \min_x \|Ax - b\|^2 with A of full column rank has the solution of the normal equations, x^\star = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top b . Homography estimation (ch. 2.3) uses the first form, colour calibration (ch. 1.1) the second.

0.5 Densities, information and conditional expectation

Definition 0.6 — Entropy, cross-entropy, Kullback–Leibler divergence For densities (or probability mass functions) p and q: H(p) = -\mathbb{E}_p[\log p], \qquad H(p, q) = -\mathbb{E}_p[\log q], \qquad \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = H(p, q) - H(p) \ge 0 . The quantity -\log p(x) is the surprisal of the outcome x; the entropy is its mean.

Theorem 0.7 — Conditional expectation is the best predictor Let Y be square-integrable and m(X) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]. For every measurable g with g(X) square-integrable, \mathbb{E}\big[(Y - g(X))^2\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[(Y - m(X))^2\big] + \mathbb{E}\big[(m(X) - g(X))^2\big]. So m minimises the mean squared error, and any model trained with a squared loss estimates it.

Proof.

  • Expand Y - g(X) = (Y - m(X)) + (m(X) - g(X)) and square.
  • By the tower property, the cross term is 2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(m(X) - g(X))\, \mathbb{E}[Y - m(X) \mid X]\big] .
  • The inner conditional expectation is zero by definition of m. ∎

0.6 The toolbox of convolutional networks

  • A convolutional layer applies learned k \times k filters to all input channels, adds a bias and a non-linearity such as ReLU, \max(0, u). Pooling or a stride s > 1 reduces the resolution.
  • The receptive field of a unit is the input region it depends on. For layers with kernel sizes k_l and strides s_l, it is r_L = 1 + \sum_{l=1}^{L} (k_l - 1) \prod_{j < l} s_j .
  • A softmax with temperature \tau turns scores z_j into probabilities e^{z_j/\tau} / \sum_k e^{z_k/\tau}. The cosine similarity of two vectors is their inner product after normalisation to unit length.
  • Training minimises an average loss by stochastic gradient methods such as Adam; automatic differentiation computes the gradients.

The code below checks Theorem 0.4 and Proposition 0.5 numerically.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 64
f, g = rng.standard_normal(n), rng.standard_normal(n)

# Convolution theorem (circular, 1-D): DFT(f * g) = DFT(f) DFT(g)
circ = np.array([sum(f[m] * g[(k - m) % n] for m in range(n)) for k in range(n)])
err = np.abs(np.fft.fft(circ) - np.fft.fft(f) * np.fft.fft(g)).max()
print(f"convolution theorem, max error: {err:.1e}")

# Shift theorem: a circular shift by t multiplies the DFT by exp(-2 i pi k t / n)
t, k = 5, np.arange(n)
lhs = np.fft.fft(np.roll(f, t))
rhs = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k * t / n) * np.fft.fft(f)
print(f"shift theorem, max error: {np.abs(lhs - rhs).max():.1e}")

# Homogeneous least squares: argmin ||A x|| over unit x = last right singular vector
A = rng.standard_normal((20, 9))
x_true = rng.standard_normal(9)
x_true /= np.linalg.norm(x_true)
A -= np.outer(A @ x_true, x_true)          # x_true is now an exact null vector of A
_, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
print(f"smallest singular value: {s[-1]:.1e}, |<v_9, x_true>| = {abs(Vt[-1] @ x_true):.6f}")

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Physical models act on linear light; decode gamma-encoded files first.
  • Gaussian smoothing is separable and composes; smoothed derivatives are convolutions with derivatives of the Gaussian.
  • A shift multiplies the Fourier transform by a phase; convolution becomes a product.
  • The unit vector minimising \|Ax\| is the last right singular vector of A.
  • The conditional expectation is the best predictor in mean square; a network trained with a squared loss estimates it.

1. Technical and chemical imaging of paintings

A painting is a layered object, and each imaging modality measures one physical quantity at one depth. This chapter says what each image measures.

Definition 1.1 — Layered painting, multimodal stack A painting is a stack of layers: a support (wood panel or canvas), a ground, an optional underdrawing, paint layers and varnish. A multimodal stack of the painting is a family of images I_m : \Omega_m \to \mathbb{R}^{c_m}, \qquad m = 1, \dots, M, each on its own pixel grid \Omega_m.

Modality Physical quantity Depth reached Typical use
Visible photography (VIS) reflectance, 400–700 nm surface colour, condition, cracks
Infrared reflectography (IRR) reflectance, 0.9–2.5 µm below many paint layers underdrawing
Reflectance imaging spectroscopy (RIS) a spectrum per pixel as VIS and IRR pigments
Radiography X-ray transmission whole object lead white, weave, wood
Macro X-ray fluorescence (MA-XRF) element fluorescence paint layers element maps
Structured-light scanning surface height surface relief, deformation

1.1 Visible photography

A colour chart with n patches of known linear values y_k \in \mathbb{R}^3 is photographed with the painting, giving camera values x_k \in \mathbb{R}^3.

Proposition 1.2 — Colour calibration by least squares Let X, Y \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times n} hold the x_k and y_k as columns, with X X^\top invertible. The 3 \times 3 matrix minimising \sum_k \|y_k - M x_k\|^2 is M^\star = Y X^\top (X X^\top)^{-1}.

Proof. Each row of M solves an ordinary least-squares problem with design matrix X^\top; the normal equations of ch. 0.4, stacked over the three rows, give the formula. ∎

Example 1.3 — Data volume A painting of 1 \times 1 m photographed at a pixel pitch of 10 µm has 10^5 \times 10^5 = 10^{10} pixels. With three channels of 16 bits: 10^{10} \times 3 \times 2 \text{ bytes} = 60 \text{ GB}. Such images are mosaics of many shots, which must be registered (ch. 2).

1.2 Infrared reflectography

Paint scatters less, and many pigments absorb less, at longer wavelengths, so infrared light crosses layers that are opaque in the visible. Carbon-based drawing materials absorb at all wavelengths.

Proposition 1.4 — Contrast of an underdrawing Let a paint layer have reflectance R_p(\lambda) and one-pass transmittance T(\lambda), above either the ground (reflectance R_g) or an underdrawing line (R_u < R_g). Neglecting multiple reflections between the layers, the contrast between the two places is \Delta R(\lambda) = T(\lambda)^2 \,(R_g - R_u).

Proof. Light reflected below the paint crosses it twice, so R \approx R_p + T^2 R_{\text{below}}; subtract the two cases. ∎

The contrast grows with T(\lambda), hence with the wavelength.

1.3 Reflectance imaging spectroscopy

A RIS camera records a cube r(x, \lambda) of often hundreds of bands, which identify pigments far better than three colour channels.

Definition 1.5 — Linear mixing model With p reference spectra (endmembers) as the columns of E \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times p}, the spectrum of a pixel is modelled as r = E a + \varepsilon, \qquad a \ge 0, \quad \mathbf{1}^\top a = 1 . The abundances a are estimated by non-negative least squares. The spectral angle \arccos\big(\langle r, e\rangle / (\|r\|\,\|e\|)\big) compares shapes, independently of illumination.

It holds for side-by-side areas of pigment, not for pigments mixed in one layer.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import nnls

# Moseley's law for K-alpha lines: E = (3/4) * 13.6 eV * (Z - 1)^2
for name, Z, tabulated in [("Ca", 20, 3.69), ("Fe", 26, 6.40), ("Cu", 29, 8.05)]:
    print(f"{name}: Moseley {0.75 * 13.6e-3 * (Z - 1) ** 2:5.2f} keV, tabulated {tabulated:5.2f} keV")

# Linear unmixing of a synthetic reflectance spectrum (400-1000 nm, 121 bands)
lam = np.linspace(400, 1000, 121)
sig = lambda x, c, w: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(x - c) / w))
E = np.stack([0.85 + 0.05 * sig(lam, 450, 30),                      # synthetic "white"
              0.05 + 0.80 * sig(lam, 600, 12),                      # synthetic "red"
              0.10 + 0.30 * np.exp(-((lam - 460) / 40) ** 2)        # synthetic "blue"
              + 0.50 * sig(lam, 800, 40)], axis=1)                  # E has shape (121, 3)
a_true = np.array([0.2, 0.5, 0.3])
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
r = E @ a_true + 0.01 * rng.standard_normal(lam.size)

# Non-negative least squares with a heavily weighted sum-to-one row
w = 100.0
a_hat, _ = nnls(np.vstack([E, w * np.ones(3)]), np.append(r, w))
print("abundances: true", a_true, " estimated", a_hat.round(3))

# Spectral angle between the measured spectrum and each endmember (radians)
sam = np.arccos(E.T @ r / (np.linalg.norm(E, axis=0) * np.linalg.norm(r)))
print("spectral angles:", sam.round(3))

1.4 Radiography

Proposition 1.6 — Beer–Lambert law Along a ray crossing layers of linear attenuation coefficients \mu_k and thicknesses t_k, the transmitted intensity satisfies -\log \frac{I}{I_0} = \sum_k \mu_k\, t_k .

Lead white attenuates X-rays far more than binders, canvas or wood, so a radiograph of an old master is mostly a map of lead-white thickness. Where the ground fills the gaps between the threads, the canvas weave appears (ch. 4.2).

1.5 Macro X-ray fluorescence

An X-ray beam scanned over the painting makes each element emit lines at energies specific to it. One spectrum per position gives one map per element.

Proposition 1.7 — Moseley’s law For an element of atomic number Z, the K\alpha line has energy approximately E_{K\alpha} \approx \tfrac{3}{4} \times 13.6 \text{ eV} \times (Z - 1)^2 .

Proof. Sketch. In the Bohr model, the transition from shell 2 to shell 1 releases 13.6\,\text{eV}\,Z_{\text{eff}}^2 (1 - 1/4). The remaining K electron screens the nucleus, so Z_{\text{eff}} \approx Z - 1. ∎

Example 1.8 — Element maps and overlapping lines The law gives 6.37 keV for iron and 8.00 keV for copper (tabulated: 6.40 and 8.05 keV). Mercury suggests vermilion; copper, azurite, malachite or verdigris; iron, earth pigments.

The As K\alpha (10.54 keV) and Pb L\alpha (10.55 keV) lines coincide; As K\beta (11.73 keV) and Pb L\beta (12.61 keV) separate them.

Deep layers are seen through the layers above, which absorb low energies most, so element maps also carry depth information. Dik et al. (2008) used them to reveal a head hidden under a painting by Van Gogh.

1.6 Structured-light scanning

A projector casts sinusoidal fringes on the surface and a camera views them from another direction. Relief displaces the fringes.

Proposition 1.9 — Three-step phase shifting Let three images be I_j = A + B\cos(\varphi + \delta_j) with \delta_j = 0, 2\pi/3, 4\pi/3. Then \varphi = \operatorname{atan2}\big(\sqrt{3}\,(I_3 - I_2),\; 2 I_1 - I_2 - I_3\big). With fringes of period p on the reference plane, projected at angle \theta to the viewing direction, a height h shifts the phase by \Delta\varphi = 2\pi h \tan\theta / p .

Proof.

  • Expanding the cosines gives I_3 - I_2 = \sqrt{3}\,B \sin\varphi and 2I_1 - I_2 - I_3 = 3B\cos\varphi; A and B cancel in the ratio.
  • A height h moves the projected fringe sideways by h\tan\theta, a fraction h\tan\theta/p of a period. ∎

Example 1.10 — Height resolution With p = 2 mm, \theta = 30^\circ and a phase noise of 0.01 rad, the height noise is \frac{2 \times 0.01}{2\pi \tan 30^\circ} \approx 0.0055 \text{ mm} = 5.5\ \mu\text{m}, the order of magnitude found by the code below.

import numpy as np

# Three-step phase shifting on a synthetic impasto bump (units: mm)
x, y = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-10, 10, 201), np.linspace(-10, 10, 201))
h = 0.5 * np.exp(-(x**2 + y**2) / 8)                 # height map, 0.5 mm peak
p, theta = 2.0, np.deg2rad(30)                        # fringe period, projection angle
phi0 = 2 * np.pi * x / p                              # phase on the flat reference plane
phi = phi0 + 2 * np.pi * h * np.tan(theta) / p        # phase displaced by the relief
A, B = 0.5, 0.4
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
I1, I2, I3 = (A + B * np.cos(phi + d) + 0.005 * rng.standard_normal(phi.shape)
              for d in (0, 2 * np.pi / 3, 4 * np.pi / 3))

wrapped = np.arctan2(np.sqrt(3) * (I3 - I2), 2 * I1 - I2 - I3)
dphi = np.angle(np.exp(1j * (wrapped - phi0)))        # phase difference, wrapped to (-pi, pi]
h_hat = dphi * p / (2 * np.pi * np.tan(theta))
print(f"max phase change {dphi.max():.3f} rad (below pi: no unwrapping needed)")
print(f"height RMS error: {1000 * np.sqrt(np.mean((h_hat - h) ** 2)):.1f} micrometres")

2. Cross-modal image registration

Registration maps each pixel of one image to the corresponding point of another. Across modalities intensities differ, so features must be designed or learned to agree. The pipeline has three stages: keypoint matches, a robust global transform, local sub-pixel offsets.

2.1 Keypoints: scale space and the structure tensor

Definition 2.1 — Scale space, structure tensor The scale space of an image I is L(\cdot\,; t) = G_{\sqrt t} * I; it solves the heat equation \partial_t L = \tfrac12 \Delta L. At derivative scale \sigma and integration scale \rho, the structure tensor is J_{\sigma,\rho} = G_\rho * \big(\nabla L_\sigma \, \nabla L_\sigma^\top\big), \qquad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge 0 \text{ its eigenvalues}.

Two small eigenvalues mean a flat region, one large eigenvalue an edge, two large ones a corner. The Harris detector keeps local maxima of \det J - \kappa\,(\operatorname{tr} J)^2, with \kappa \approx 0.05.

Key.Net (Barroso-Laguna et al., 2019) combines such handcrafted derivative filters with learned filters in a multi-scale pyramid, trained on pairs related by random synthetic transforms.

2.2 Descriptors and matching

AffNet (Mishkin et al., 2018) estimates the affine shape of each keypoint region, trained to make descriptors discriminative rather than merely repeatable. HardNet (Mishchuk et al., 2017) maps a 32 \times 32 patch to a unit vector in dimension 128.

Definition 2.2 — HardNet loss For a batch of n matching pairs (a_i, p_i) of descriptors, let d_{ij} = \|a_i - p_j\|. The loss uses the hardest negative of each pair: \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \max\Big(0,\; 1 + d_{ii} - \min_{j \ne i} \min(d_{ij}, d_{ji})\Big).

A nearest-neighbour match is kept if it is mutual and if the ratio of the first to the second distance is below about 0.8 (Lowe, 2004). Descriptors trained on photographs transfer well between VIS and IRR, which share many edges, less well to element maps.

2.3 Homographies and lens distortion

Proposition 2.3 — A plane induces a homography Let two pinhole cameras be K[I \mid 0] and K'[R \mid t], and let the scene lie on the plane n^\top X = d. The images x and x' of a point, in homogeneous coordinates, satisfy x' \sim H x with H = K' \big(R + t\, n^\top / d\big) K^{-1}.

Proof.

  • The point is X = s K^{-1} x for some s > 0, and n^\top X / d = 1 on the plane.
  • In the second camera, RX + t = RX + t\,(n^\top X)/d = (R + t n^\top/d)\, X.
  • Project with K' and substitute X; the scalar s disappears up to scale. ∎

A painting is nearly planar, so its images are related by homographies. Each match gives two linear equations in the entries of H; with four or more, the direct linear transform (DLT) solves A h = 0 by Proposition 0.5, after normalising the coordinates (Hartley, 1997).

Definition 2.4 — Radial lens distortion With c the distortion centre, f the focal length in pixels and r = \|x_u - c\| / f, an ideal point x_u is imaged at x_d = c + (x_u - c)\,(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4).

Example 2.5 — Distortion is not a detail For a 6000 \times 4000 sensor with f = 8000 px, a corner lies at r = 3606/8000 \approx 0.45. With k_1 = -0.02, it moves by 0.02 \times 0.45^3 \times 8000 \approx 15 \text{ px}, far above sub-pixel accuracy. The coefficients are estimated with the homography by non-linear least squares, or with a target (Zhang, 2000).

2.4 Robust estimation: RANSAC and MAGSAC++

Many matches between modalities are wrong. RANSAC (Fischler and Bolles, 1981) fits the model to random minimal samples and keeps the one with the most inliers below a threshold.

Proposition 2.6 — Number of RANSAC draws If a fraction w of the matches are inliers and a minimal sample has size s, then N draws contain an all-inlier sample with probability at least p as soon as N \ge \frac{\log(1 - p)}{\log(1 - w^s)} .

Proof. Each draw is all-inlier with probability w^s, independently; all N draws fail with probability (1 - w^s)^N \le 1 - p. ∎

For w = 0.4, s = 4 and p = 0.99: 178 draws. The fixed threshold is the weak point; MAGSAC++ (Barath et al., 2020) marginalises over the noise scale \sigma instead, and refits by iteratively reweighted least squares.

Proposition 2.7 — A σ-marginalised weight (simplified MAGSAC++ weight) Suppose that the residual norm r of an inlier is Rayleigh distributed given \sigma, p(r \mid \sigma) = (r/\sigma^2)\, e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}, and that \sigma is uniform on (0, \sigma_{\max}]. Then p(r) = \frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}} \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\; \operatorname{erfc}\!\Big(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{\max}}\Big).

Proof.

  • Write p(r) as the average of p(r \mid \sigma) over \sigma \in (0, \sigma_{\max}], and substitute u = r^2/(2\sigma^2).
  • The integrand becomes 2^{-1/2}\, u^{-1/2} e^{-u}\, du, on u \ge u_0 = r^2/(2\sigma_{\max}^2).
  • Finally, \int_{u_0}^\infty u^{-1/2} e^{-u}\, du = \sqrt{\pi}\, \operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt{u_0}) . This gives the formula. ∎

The weight decreases smoothly, without a threshold; MAGSAC++ applies this construction to each model’s residual distribution, with incomplete gamma functions.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import erfc

def normalise(x):
    """Similarity T sending the points to zero mean and mean distance sqrt(2)."""
    c = x.mean(0)
    s = np.sqrt(2) / np.linalg.norm(x - c, axis=1).mean()
    return np.array([[s, 0, -s * c[0]], [0, s, -s * c[1]], [0, 0, 1]])

def dlt(x, y, w=None):
    """Weighted normalised DLT: homography H with y ~ H x."""
    w = np.ones(len(x)) if w is None else w
    Tx, Ty = normalise(x), normalise(y)
    xh = np.c_[x, np.ones(len(x))] @ Tx.T
    yh = np.c_[y, np.ones(len(y))] @ Ty.T
    z = np.zeros_like(xh)
    A = np.r_[np.c_[z, -xh, yh[:, 1:2] * xh], np.c_[xh, z, -yh[:, 0:1] * xh]]
    A *= np.sqrt(np.r_[w, w])[:, None]
    H = np.linalg.svd(A)[2][-1].reshape(3, 3)          # Proposition 0.5
    H = np.linalg.inv(Ty) @ H @ Tx
    return H / H[2, 2]

def apply(H, x):
    yh = np.c_[x, np.ones(len(x))] @ H.T
    return yh[:, :2] / yh[:, 2:]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
H_true = np.array([[1.02, 0.03, 15.0], [-0.02, 0.98, -8.0], [2e-5, -1e-5, 1.0]])
n, w_in = 300, 0.4
x = rng.uniform(0, 1000, (n, 2))
y = apply(H_true, x) + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal((n, 2))          # inliers: 0.5 px noise
out = rng.random(n) > w_in
y[out] = rng.uniform(0, 1000, (out.sum(), 2))                     # 60 % gross outliers

# RANSAC with the number of draws of Proposition 2.6
N = int(np.ceil(np.log(1 - 0.99) / np.log(1 - w_in**4)))
best, thr = None, 3.0
for _ in range(N):
    idx = rng.choice(n, 4, replace=False)
    H = dlt(x[idx], y[idx])
    inl = np.linalg.norm(apply(H, x) - y, axis=1) < thr
    if best is None or inl.sum() > best.sum():
        best = inl
H_ransac = dlt(x[best], y[best])

# Iteratively reweighted DLT with the sigma-marginalised weight of Proposition 2.7
H_irls = H_ransac
for _ in range(10):
    r = np.linalg.norm(apply(H_irls, x) - y, axis=1)
    H_irls = dlt(x, y, erfc(r / (np.sqrt(2) * thr)))

grid = np.stack(np.meshgrid(np.linspace(0, 1000, 11), np.linspace(0, 1000, 11)), -1).reshape(-1, 2)
for name, H in [("all points", dlt(x, y)), ("RANSAC + refit", H_ransac), ("sigma-weighted IRLS", H_irls)]:
    err = np.linalg.norm(apply(H, grid) - apply(H_true, grid), axis=1)
    print(f"{name:20s}: mean error {err.mean():8.3f} px, max {err.max():8.3f} px")
print(f"RANSAC draws: {N}, inliers kept: {best.sum()} of {(~out).sum()} true inliers")

2.5 Patchwise offsets to sub-pixel precision

After the global transform, local offsets of a few pixels remain (scanner drift, deformed supports, XRF rasters). An offset is estimated per tile, then interpolated into a smooth field.

Proposition 2.8 — Aperture problem Let B(x) = A(x - \delta) + \text{noise} on a tile, with \delta small. Linearising A(x - \delta) \approx A(x) - \nabla A(x)^\top \delta, the least-squares offset solves J \delta = -\textstyle\sum_x \nabla A(x)\, \big(B(x) - A(x)\big), \qquad J = \sum_x \nabla A(x) \nabla A(x)^\top . The offset is determined if and only if the structure tensor J is invertible. With white noise of variance s^2, its covariance is about s^2 J^{-1}.

On a single straight edge \lambda_2 = 0: only the offset across the edge is measured. Tiles are weighted by their smallest eigenvalue.

Proposition 2.9 — Normalised cross-correlation For tiles a and b with means \bar a, \bar b, \mathrm{NCC}(a, b) = \frac{\langle a - \bar a,\, b - \bar b \rangle}{\|a - \bar a\|\,\|b - \bar b\|} \in [-1, 1], and, for every \alpha \ne 0, \mathrm{NCC}(\alpha a + \beta, b) = \operatorname{sign}(\alpha)\, \mathrm{NCC}(a, b) .

NCC tolerates gain, offset and sign changes, not a non-monotone relation between modalities. The remedy is a non-linear filter per modality, such as a gradient magnitude, mapping both images to a common representation.

Proposition 2.10 — Phase correlation If b(x) = a(x - t) circularly on an N \times N tile, the normalised cross-power spectrum is a pure phase, whose inverse DFT is a Kronecker delta at t: \frac{\hat b\, \overline{\hat a}}{|\hat b\, \overline{\hat a}|}(k) = e^{-2\pi i \langle k, t\rangle / N}.

Proof. By Theorem 0.4, \hat b = e^{-2\pi i \langle k, t \rangle/N}\, \hat a, and \hat a\, \overline{\hat a} = |\hat a|^2 cancels in the ratio. ∎

A parabola through the values c_{-1}, c_0, c_{+1} around the peak refines it on each axis: \delta = \frac{c_{-1} - c_{+1}}{2\,(c_{-1} - 2 c_0 + c_{+1})} . Stacking all tiles in one tensor lets a single batched FFT process them in parallel.

Definition 2.11 — Learned similarity filters Let f_\theta and g_\theta be small CNNs applied to the two modalities, and c_t the correlation of their outputs on two tiles, shifted by t. Given tiles with a known offset t^\star, the filters are trained with \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\log \frac{e^{c_{t^\star}/\tau}}{\sum_t e^{c_t/\tau}} , so that the correlation surface becomes a sharp peak at the true offset.

import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, T = 512, 64                                         # image size, tile size
A = ndi.gaussian_filter(rng.standard_normal((n, n)), 2.0)
A = (A - A.min()) / (A.max() - A.min())                # "modality 1" in [0, 1]

# Smooth displacement field of up to about 3 px, then a non-monotone change of contrast
gy, gx = np.mgrid[0:n, 0:n]
dy = 2.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * gx / n) + 0.3
dx = 2.0 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * gy / n) - 0.4
B = np.abs(ndi.map_coordinates(A, [gy - dy, gx - dx], order=3) - 0.5)   # "modality 2"

def tiles(img):
    """Stack of non-overlapping T x T tiles, shape (number of tiles, T, T)."""
    return img.reshape(n // T, T, n // T, T).swapaxes(1, 2).reshape(-1, T, T)

def phase_correlation(a, b):
    """Batched phase correlation (Proposition 2.10) with parabolic sub-pixel refinement."""
    win = np.outer(np.hanning(T), np.hanning(T))
    Fa = np.fft.fft2((a - a.mean((1, 2), keepdims=True)) * win)
    Fb = np.fft.fft2((b - b.mean((1, 2), keepdims=True)) * win)
    R = Fb * np.conj(Fa)
    c = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(R / (np.abs(R) + 1e-12)))   # one correlation surface per tile
    i, j = np.unravel_index(c.reshape(len(c), -1).argmax(1), (T, T))
    k = np.arange(len(c))
    refine = lambda cm, c0, cp: 0.5 * (cm - cp) / (cm - 2 * c0 + cp)
    di = refine(c[k, (i - 1) % T, j], c[k, i, j], c[k, (i + 1) % T, j])
    dj = refine(c[k, i, (j - 1) % T], c[k, i, j], c[k, i, (j + 1) % T])
    wrap = lambda q: (q + T // 2) % T - T // 2
    return np.stack([wrap(i) + di, wrap(j) + dj], 1)

centres = np.arange(n // T) * T + T // 2
true = np.stack([dy[np.ix_(centres, centres)].ravel(), dx[np.ix_(centres, centres)].ravel()], 1)
grad = lambda img: ndi.gaussian_gradient_magnitude(img, 1.0)   # a non-linear common representation
for name, a, b in [("raw intensities", A, B), ("gradient magnitude", grad(A), grad(B))]:
    err = np.linalg.norm(phase_correlation(tiles(a), tiles(b)) - true, axis=1)
    print(f"{name:19s}: median error {np.median(err):6.3f} px, tiles within 0.5 px: {np.mean(err < 0.5):.0%}")

Here the second modality is |A - 0.5|: raw correlation fails, while the gradient magnitude, unchanged by this map, registers every tile.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Keypoints come from the structure tensor in scale space; Key.Net, AffNet and HardNet learn detection, shape and description.
  • A plane is imaged through a homography (DLT from four matches); lens distortion must be modelled.
  • RANSAC needs \log(1-p)/\log(1-w^s) draws; MAGSAC++ replaces its threshold by a σ-marginalised weight.
  • Local offsets exist where the structure tensor is invertible; phase correlation of tile stacks estimates them.
  • Across modalities, correlate non-linear features, not raw intensities.

3. Conditional expectation in multimodal imaging

Once registered, every pixel is a short vector of measurements, such as (R, G, B, IRR). The question “what is unusual here?” becomes a question about densities and conditional expectations.

3.1 Density estimation and surprisal

Definition 3.1 — Kernel density estimator For pixel vectors x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d, a kernel K and a bandwidth h > 0, \hat p_h(x) = \frac{1}{n h^d} \sum_{i=1}^{n} K\Big(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\Big).

Theorem 3.2 — Bias and variance of the estimator Let K be a symmetric density with \int u u^\top K = \mu_2 I and R(K) = \int K^2, and p twice differentiable. As h \to 0 and n h^d \to \infty, \mathbb{E}\,\hat p_h(x) - p(x) \approx \frac{h^2 \mu_2}{2}\, \Delta p(x), \qquad \operatorname{Var} \hat p_h(x) \approx \frac{p(x)\, R(K)}{n h^d}. The mean squared error is smallest for h \propto n^{-1/(d+4)}, where it decreases as n^{-4/(d+4)}.

Proof. Sketch.

  • Substituting x_i = x - hu in the mean gives \mathbb{E}\,\hat p_h(x) = \int K(u)\, p(x - hu)\, du . A second-order Taylor expansion of p gives the bias; the first-order term vanishes by symmetry.
  • The variance is n^{-1} times that of one term, dominated by \mathbb{E}[K_h(x - X)^2] \approx p(x) R(K)/h^d.
  • Minimise c_1 h^4 + c_2/(n h^d) in h. ∎

With d = 4 and 10^8 pixels, the rate n^{-1/2} is excellent: densities of a few channels are easy to estimate, which is why the syllabus speaks of low-dimensional data.

Definition 3.3 — Surprisal and conditional surprisal The surprisal of a pixel vector is s(x) = -\log p(x). For a split x = (u, v), such as u = colour and v = infrared, the conditional surprisal is s(v \mid u) = -\log p(v \mid u) = s(u, v) - s(u).

For densities these values can be negative; only comparisons matter. A high s(u, v) flags a rare pixel, perhaps only a rare colour. A high s(v \mid u) flags an infrared value that the colour does not explain, such as an underdrawing or a change of composition. The syllabus illustrates surprisal maps on paintings by Hieronymus Bosch.

Proposition 3.4 — Gaussian case If (U, V) is jointly Gaussian with V scalar, let m(u) = \mathbb{E}[V \mid U = u] and s^2 = \operatorname{Var}(V \mid U). Then s(v \mid u) = \frac{(v - m(u))^2}{2 s^2} + \frac12 \log(2\pi s^2). Conditional surprisal is then a standardised squared residual of the best predictor of Theorem 0.7.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import KernelDensity
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Synthetic pixels (R, G, B, IRR): three common paints and one rare red paint
means = np.array([[0.85, 0.70, 0.60, 0.80],     # light flesh tone, bright in infrared
                  [0.30, 0.35, 0.50, 0.55],     # blue-grey
                  [0.20, 0.25, 0.15, 0.30],     # dark brown
                  [0.75, 0.15, 0.10, 0.70]])    # rare red
counts = [1500, 1000, 800, 40]
X = np.vstack([m + 0.03 * rng.standard_normal((c, 4)) for m, c in zip(means, counts)])
kind = np.repeat(np.arange(4), counts)
under = (kind == 0) & (rng.random(len(X)) < 0.05)   # carbon underdrawing below the flesh tone
X[under, 3] -= 0.25                                 # darker in IRR only, same colour

kde4 = KernelDensity(bandwidth=0.04).fit(X)         # p(R, G, B, IRR)
kde3 = KernelDensity(bandwidth=0.04).fit(X[:, :3])  # p(R, G, B)
joint = -kde4.score_samples(X)                      # surprisal, in nats
cond = joint + kde3.score_samples(X[:, :3])         # -log p(IRR | RGB)

k = under.sum()                                     # flag as many pixels as there are true ones
for name, s in [("joint", joint), ("conditional", cond)]:
    top = np.argsort(s)[-k:]
    print(f"{name:11s} surprisal: AUC {roc_auc_score(under, s):.3f}, among the {k} most surprising "
          f"pixels {under[top].sum()} are underdrawing and {(kind[top] == 3).sum()} are the rare red")

# Nadaraya-Watson estimate of E[IRR | RGB] and a standardised residual
def nw(x_query, x, y, h=0.04):
    d2 = ((x_query[:, None, :] - x[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
    w = np.exp(-d2 / (2 * h * h))
    return (w * y).sum(1) / w.sum(1)

res = X[:, 3] - nw(X[:, :3], X[:, :3], X[:, 3])
z = res / res[~under].std()
print(f"residual z-score: underdrawing {z[under].mean():.1f}, other pixels {z[~under].mean():.2f}")

Joint surprisal mixes the rare red with the underdrawing; conditional surprisal isolates the underdrawing.

3.2 Predicting colour from chemistry

Definition 3.5 — Colour predicted from element maps Let X \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times H \times W} be registered MA-XRF maps and Y \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W} the calibrated photograph. A CNN f_\theta trained with the squared loss estimates, at each pixel x, \hat Y(x) = f_\theta(X)(x) \approx \mathbb{E}\big[Y(x) \mid X \text{ on a neighbourhood of } x\big], and the residual Y(x) - \hat Y(x) measures what chemistry does not explain.

A neighbourhood is needed rather than a single pixel. XRF maps are photon-limited and noisy, light diffuses inside the paint, and lines from deep layers are attenuated. Retouching made with a different colour over the same chemistry, or with materials absent from the input maps, leaves a large residual. The syllabus applies this to the detection of restorations in paintings by Rembrandt.

Train and test on separate blocks of the image: neighbouring pixels are correlated, and a random split of pixels overstates accuracy.

import numpy as np
import torch
from scipy import ndimage as ndi
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

torch.set_num_threads(1)
torch.manual_seed(0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
S = 48
# Absorption of light (R, G, B) per unit concentration: Hg, Cu, Fe; Pb scatters and brightens
alpha = np.array([[0.1, 2.0, 2.0],      # Hg: vermilion-like red
                  [2.0, 0.6, 0.4],      # Cu: blue-green
                  [0.3, 0.8, 1.6]])     # Fe: earth, yellow-brown

def painting():
    """Element concentrations (Pb, Hg, Cu, Fe) and the visible colour they produce."""
    f = ndi.gaussian_filter(rng.standard_normal((4, S, S)), (0, 4, 4))
    c = np.log1p(np.exp(2 * f / f.std() - 0.5))              # smooth, non-negative maps
    rgb = np.exp(-np.einsum("ehw,ec->chw", c[1:], alpha)) * (0.4 + 0.6 * np.tanh(c[0]))[None]
    rgb = ndi.gaussian_filter(rgb, (0, 1.0, 1.0))           # light diffusion in the paint
    xrf = rng.poisson(20 * c) / 20.0                          # photon-counting noise of MA-XRF
    return xrf.astype(np.float32), rgb.astype(np.float32)

train = [painting() for _ in range(6)]
xrf_t, rgb_t = painting()                                     # a separate test painting
rgb_t[:, 15:26, 27:39] += np.array([0.06, -0.03, -0.04])[:, None, None]   # retouching, chemistry unchanged
patch = np.zeros((S, S), bool); patch[15:26, 27:39] = True

def net(k):
    p = k // 2
    return torch.nn.Sequential(
        torch.nn.Conv2d(4, 24, k, padding=p), torch.nn.ReLU(),
        torch.nn.Conv2d(24, 24, k, padding=p), torch.nn.ReLU(),
        torch.nn.Conv2d(24, 3, 1), torch.nn.Sigmoid())

X = torch.tensor(np.stack([a for a, _ in train])); Y = torch.tensor(np.stack([b for _, b in train]))
for name, k in [("pixel-wise (1x1)", 1), ("CNN (5x5)", 5)]:
    model = net(k)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
    for step in range(200):
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = torch.mean((model(X) - Y) ** 2)            # estimates E[VIS | XRF neighbourhood]
        loss.backward(); opt.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        pred = model(torch.tensor(xrf_t)[None])[0].numpy()
    res = np.linalg.norm(rgb_t - pred, axis=0)
    print(f"{name:16s}: RMSE outside the retouching {np.sqrt(np.mean(res[~patch] ** 2)):.4f}, "
          f"residual AUC for the retouching {roc_auc_score(patch.ravel(), res.ravel()):.3f}")

4. Microstructure: craquelure and canvas

Some evidence lies in the microstructure of a painting: its cracks, and the weave of its canvas seen in radiographs.

4.1 Craquelure

Definition 4.1 — Crack types Drying cracks form while the paint dries and shrinks; they are often wide and irregular. Age cracks form later, through paint and ground, under the mechanical stresses of the support. On panels they tend to follow the wood grain. Bucklow (1997) described crack patterns by direction, island size and shape, smoothness and junction types, and related them to support and school.

Cracks are thin dark curves. At scale \sigma, the Hessian of L_\sigma has a large positive eigenvalue across a dark line and a small one along it.

Proposition 4.2 — Scale selection for lines Let a dark line have the Gaussian profile -e^{-x^2/(2w^2)}. The \gamma-normalised second derivative of L_\sigma at its centre is \sigma^{2\gamma}\, w\, (w^2 + \sigma^2)^{-3/2}. It is maximal at \sigma^2 = \frac{2\gamma}{3 - 2\gamma}\, w^2, \quad \text{so } \sigma = w \text{ for } \gamma = 3/4 .

Proof.

  • Smoothing the profile gives L_\sigma(x) = -\frac{w}{\sqrt{w^2 + \sigma^2}}\, e^{-x^2 / (2(w^2 + \sigma^2))} , whose second derivative at x = 0 is w (w^2+\sigma^2)^{-3/2}.
  • Set the derivative of 2\gamma \log\sigma - \tfrac32 \log(w^2 + \sigma^2) to zero: 2\gamma (w^2 + \sigma^2) = 3\sigma^2. ∎

So with \gamma = 3/4 (Lindeberg, 1998), the selected scale equals the crack width. A multi-scale line measure, as in the vesselness filter of Frangi et al. (1998), keeps pixels where the large eigenvalue dominates the small one.

Definition 4.3 — Orientation statistics of cracks Crack directions \theta_j \in [0, \pi) are axial: \theta and \theta + \pi are the same. Their coherence is the mean resultant length of the doubled angles, \bar R = \Big|\frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} e^{2 i \theta_j}\Big| \in [0, 1] .

Coherence near 1 means one dominant direction, as on many panels. With crack density, island areas and junction degrees, it feeds a classifier. The syllabus takes its examples from Van Eyck; Cornelis et al. (2013) detect cracks on the Ghent Altarpiece.

import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, w = 256, 1.5                                     # image size, crack profile width (px)

def cracks(stretch):
    """Voronoi crack network; stretch > 1 elongates the islands along the vertical axis."""
    seeds = rng.uniform(0, n, (60, 2))
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:n, 0:n]
    d = ((yy[..., None] - seeds[:, 0]) / stretch) ** 2 + (xx[..., None] - seeds[:, 1]) ** 2
    lab = d.argmin(-1)
    edge = (np.diff(lab, axis=0, append=lab[-1:]) != 0) | (np.diff(lab, axis=1, append=lab[:, -1:]) != 0)
    img = 0.8 + 0.03 * rng.standard_normal((n, n))                  # paint surface
    img -= 0.5 * np.minimum(1, ndi.gaussian_filter(edge * 1.0, w) * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi) * w)
    return img, edge

def ridge(img, sigmas, gamma=0.75):
    """Scale-normalised Hessian line measure for dark lines; best scale and local orientation."""
    best, theta = np.zeros_like(img), np.zeros_like(img)
    for s in sigmas:
        Hyy = ndi.gaussian_filter(img, s, order=(2, 0)); Hxx = ndi.gaussian_filter(img, s, order=(0, 2))
        Hxy = ndi.gaussian_filter(img, s, order=(1, 1))
        root = np.sqrt(((Hxx - Hyy) / 2) ** 2 + Hxy ** 2)
        lmax, lmin = (Hxx + Hyy) / 2 + root, (Hxx + Hyy) / 2 - root
        r = s ** (2 * gamma) * np.maximum(lmax, 0) * np.exp(-(lmin / (lmax + 1e-9)) ** 2 / 0.5)
        th = 0.5 * np.arctan2(2 * Hxy, Hxx - Hyy) + np.pi / 2    # direction along the line
        upd = r > best
        best[upd], theta[upd] = r[upd], th[upd]
    return best, theta

for name, stretch in [("isotropic network (canvas-like)", 1.0), ("elongated islands (panel-like)", 4.0)]:
    img, edge = cracks(stretch)
    R, th = ridge(img, sigmas=[1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0])
    mask = R > 0.5 * np.percentile(R, 99)
    truth = ndi.binary_dilation(edge, iterations=2)
    precision, recall = (mask & truth).sum() / mask.sum(), (mask & edge).sum() / edge.sum()
    Rbar = np.abs(np.mean(np.exp(2j * th[mask])))               # Definition 4.3
    print(f"{name:32s}: precision {precision:.2f}, recall {recall:.2f}, "
          f"crack density {mask.mean():.3f}, orientation coherence {Rbar:.2f}")

# Scale selection on a 1-D Gaussian line of width w: argmax of sigma^(2 gamma) L_xx(0)
sig = np.linspace(0.3, 6, 2000)
for gamma in (0.75, 1.0):
    resp = sig ** (2 * gamma) * w * (w**2 + sig**2) ** -1.5
    print(f"gamma = {gamma}: selected sigma / w = {sig[resp.argmax()] / w:.3f}")

4.2 Canvas analysis from radiographs

In a radiograph, the lead-white ground that fills the gaps between threads draws the weave (Proposition 1.6). The threads running along a roll of canvas are the warp; the weft threads cross them.

Definition 4.4 — Thread density On a tile with pixel pitch \Delta (in cm), let the windowed Fourier spectrum peak at f cycles per pixel in the direction across the warp. The local warp density is \rho = f / \Delta \quad \text{threads per cm}.

Scanning tiles along the painting gives a warp-density profile; zero-padding and a parabolic fit locate the peak to a fraction of a bin.

Proposition 4.5 — Warp matching (weaving model) Assume that each warp thread runs the whole length of the roll with a fixed spacing to its neighbours. Then two pieces cut from the same roll, at any positions along its length, have equal warp-spacing profiles over their common warp threads, up to a shift and possibly a flip. Weft threads, inserted one at a time, carry no such shared profile.

Matching therefore maximises the normalised correlation of two warp-density profiles over shifts and flips. The syllabus applies this to works by Poussin, Van Gogh, Rembrandt and Frans Hals; the method is described by Johnson, Johnson and Erdmann (2013).

Example 4.6 — How significant is a match? Under independence, Fisher’s transform z = \operatorname{atanh}(r)\sqrt{n - 3} is about standard normal. A correlation r = 0.6 over n = 50 independent samples gives z = 0.693 \times \sqrt{47} \approx 4.75 . But smooth profiles are not independent samples, and searching over shifts inflates the maximum: a null from unrelated canvases is safer.

import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
px = 0.01                                          # pixel size in cm (0.1 mm)

def roll(width_cm=60, density=14.0):
    """Warp thread positions (cm) across the width of a roll: irregular, correlated spacings."""
    k = int(width_cm * density)
    eps = 0.08 * ndi.gaussian_filter1d(rng.standard_normal(k), 8) * np.sqrt(8) + 0.02 * rng.standard_normal(k)
    return np.cumsum((1 + eps) / density)

def radiograph(warp, x0_cm, w_px=1536, h_px=256, weft_density=12.0):
    """X-ray image of a piece cut at x0 across the roll: the ground fills the interstices."""
    x = x0_cm + px * np.arange(w_px)
    u = np.interp(x, warp, np.arange(len(warp)))                      # warp thread phase
    v = np.cumsum((1 + 0.05 * rng.standard_normal(h_px)) * weft_density * px)  # weft phase
    img = 0.2 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * u)[None, :] + 0.2 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * v)[:, None]
    return img + 0.15 * rng.standard_normal((h_px, w_px))

def warp_profile(img, T=64, step=32, pad=512):
    """Warp thread density (threads/cm) along x, from the peak of windowed tile spectra."""
    win = np.outer(np.hanning(T), np.hanning(T))
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(pad) / px                                      # threads per cm
    band = (f > 8) & (f < 20)
    prof = []
    for x in range(0, img.shape[1] - T + 1, step):
        spec = [np.abs(np.fft.rfft2(img[y:y + T, x:x + T] * win, s=(pad, pad)))[0]
                for y in range(0, img.shape[0] - T + 1, T)]
        s = np.mean(spec, 0)
        i = np.flatnonzero(band)[s[band].argmax()]
        d = 0.5 * (s[i - 1] - s[i + 1]) / (s[i - 1] - 2 * s[i] + s[i + 1])   # parabolic refinement
        prof.append((i + d) * (f[1] - f[0]))
    return np.array(prof)

def best_match(a, b, min_overlap=24):
    """Largest normalised correlation of two density profiles over shifts (in tile steps)."""
    out = []
    for lag in range(-len(b) + min_overlap, len(a) - min_overlap + 1):
        p, q = a[max(lag, 0):], b[max(-lag, 0):]
        m = min(len(p), len(q))
        out.append((np.corrcoef(p[:m], q[:m])[0, 1], lag))
    return max(out)

R1 = roll()
A = warp_profile(radiograph(R1, x0_cm=10.0))
B = warp_profile(radiograph(R1, x0_cm=14.0))       # same roll, overlapping 11 cm of warp threads
print(f"mean warp density of A: {A.mean():.2f} threads/cm (the roll was woven at 14)")
same, lag = best_match(A, B)
null = np.array([best_match(A, warp_profile(radiograph(roll(), x0_cm=10.0)))[0] for _ in range(20)])
print(f"same roll: correlation {same:.2f} at a shift of {lag * 32 * px:.2f} cm (true: 4 cm)")
print(f"20 unrelated rolls: mean {null.mean():.2f}, max {null.max():.2f}")
print(f"z-score of the match against the unrelated rolls: {(same - null.mean()) / null.std():.1f}")

Unrelated canvases reach 0.8 by chance: a match needs a calibrated null.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • Cracks are dark ridges; Hessian filters with \gamma = 3/4 select their width.
  • Crack networks are described by density, islands, junctions and orientation coherence.
  • Tile spectra of radiographs give local thread densities.
  • Shared warp profiles reunite pieces of one roll, given a calibrated null.

5. Text and image embeddings with pretrained networks

Pretrained networks map images and texts to vectors whose geometry reflects meaning, turning search and comparison into nearest-neighbour problems.

5.1 CLIP: a joint embedding of images and texts

Definition 5.1 — Contrastive image–text embedding An image encoder f and a text encoder g output unit vectors. For a batch of N pairs (x_i, t_i), let s_{ij} = \langle f(x_i), g(t_j) \rangle / \tau. The symmetric InfoNCE loss is \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Big[\log \frac{e^{s_{ii}}}{\sum_j e^{s_{ij}}} + \log \frac{e^{s_{ii}}}{\sum_j e^{s_{ji}}}\Big].

CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) trained such encoders on 400 million image–text pairs from the internet. A label set becomes a zero-shot classifier: embed prompts such as “a painting of a dog”, and pick the closest.

Proposition 5.2 — Duplicate captions set a floor If the caption of x_i has m variants in the batch that the encoders cannot tell apart from the image, the image-to-text term of pair i is at least \log m.

Proof. The m logits s_{ij} of those captions are equal, so the softmax gives the correct one a probability of at most 1/m. ∎

5.2 Search engine and semantic directions

A search engine embeds the collection once, then returns the images of largest cosine similarity to a text or image query. At millions of images, an approximate nearest-neighbour index such as FAISS (Johnson et al., 2021) replaces the exhaustive scan.

Definition 5.3 — Semantic direction For two prompts t^+ and t^- that differ in one concept, the direction is v = g(t^+) - g(t^-), and an image is scored by \langle f(x), v\rangle.

Directions such as “night scene” minus “day scene” rank a collection along a concept without any labelled example.

5.3 High-resolution microstructural comparison

Most CLIP models see 224 \times 224 pixels; a painting has billions. Embed tiles cut at native resolution, and compare two works by the distribution of their tile embeddings, for instance by the mean best-match similarity: brushwork, craquelure or paper texture become comparable. Micrographs are rare in CLIP’s training data, so trust relative, not absolute, similarities.

The code trains a miniature CLIP from scratch on the scikit-learn digits, with bag-of-words captions such as “a sketch of a seven on a light ground”.

import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

torch.set_num_threads(1)
torch.manual_seed(0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
digits = load_digits()
X = digits.data / 16.0
y = digits.target
inv = rng.random(len(X)) < 0.5                    # half the images: light ground, dark ink
X[inv] = 1.0 - X[inv]
names = "zero one two three four five six seven eight nine".split()
templates = ["a drawing of the digit {}", "the number {} written by hand", "a sketch of a {}"]
ground = {False: "on a dark ground", True: "on a light ground"}

def caption(i):
    return templates[rng.integers(3)].format(names[y[i]]) + " " + ground[bool(inv[i])]

vocab = {w: k for k, w in enumerate(sorted({w for t in templates for w in t.split()}
                                           | set(names) | {"on", "a", "dark", "light", "ground"}))}
def tokens(texts):
    """Bag of words, as a normalised count matrix (a stand-in for a text transformer)."""
    M = torch.zeros(len(texts), len(vocab))
    for r, t in enumerate(texts):
        for w in t.split():
            M[r, vocab[w]] += 1
    return M / M.sum(1, keepdim=True)

img_enc = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(64, 128), torch.nn.ReLU(), torch.nn.Linear(128, 32))
txt_enc = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(len(vocab), 128), torch.nn.ReLU(), torch.nn.Linear(128, 32))
log_scale = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(np.log(1 / 0.07), dtype=torch.float32))
params = list(img_enc.parameters()) + list(txt_enc.parameters()) + [log_scale]
opt = torch.optim.Adam(params, lr=2e-3)
train, test = np.arange(1300), np.arange(1300, len(X))
Xt = torch.tensor(X, dtype=torch.float32)

for step in range(400):
    b = rng.choice(train, 256, replace=False)
    zi = F.normalize(img_enc(Xt[b]), dim=1)
    zt = F.normalize(txt_enc(tokens([caption(i) for i in b])), dim=1)
    logits = log_scale.exp().clamp(max=100) * zi @ zt.T          # s_ij of Definition 5.1
    target = torch.arange(len(b))
    loss = (F.cross_entropy(logits, target) + F.cross_entropy(logits.T, target)) / 2
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    zi = F.normalize(img_enc(Xt[test]), dim=1)
    enc = lambda texts: F.normalize(txt_enc(tokens(texts)), dim=1)
    # Zero-shot classification with a prompt ensemble over the two grounds
    zc = F.normalize(sum(enc([f"a drawing of the digit {d} {g}" for d in names]) for g in ground.values()), dim=1)
    acc = ((zi @ zc.T).argmax(1).numpy() == y[test]).mean()
    # Text-to-image search: the 10 nearest test images to one query
    q = enc(["a sketch of a seven on a light ground"])
    top = (zi @ q.T).squeeze(1).argsort(descending=True)[:10].numpy()
    hits = np.mean((y[test][top] == 7) & inv[test][top])
    # Semantic direction (Definition 5.3): light ground minus dark ground
    d = enc(["a drawing of the digit five on a light ground"]) - enc(["a drawing of the digit five on a dark ground"])
    score = (zi @ d.T).squeeze(1).numpy()
print(f"final loss {loss.item():.3f}, temperature {1 / log_scale.exp().item():.3f}")
print(f"zero-shot accuracy on {len(test)} held-out images: {acc:.3f}")
print(f"query 'a sketch of a seven on a light ground': precision at 10 = {hits:.1f}")
print(f"semantic direction 'light ground': AUC {roc_auc_score(inv[test], score):.3f}")

5.4 DINOv2: self-supervised patch embeddings

DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) trains vision transformers without labels on 142 million curated images. A student network matches a teacher, its moving average, across crops (DINO loss) and predicts masked patches (iBOT loss). The models cut images into patches of 14 \times 14 pixels and return one embedding per patch.

Definition 5.4 — Mutual nearest neighbours Let z_p and z'_q be the unit patch embeddings of two images. The pair (p, q) is a correspondence if each is the other’s most similar patch: q = \arg\max_{q'} \langle z_p, z'_{q'} \rangle \quad \text{and} \quad p = \arg\max_{p'} \langle z_{p'}, z'_q \rangle .

Because the embeddings encode parts rather than pixels, correspondences link the same figure in a painting, a copy and a print after it, despite changes of medium.

5.5 Visualisation by PCA

Fit a PCA to the patch embeddings of one or several images, and display the first three components as red, green and blue. Parts with similar meaning receive similar colours across images, and the first component often separates the subject from the background.

The code uses a contrast-invariant stand-in for the patch embeddings, on a scikit-learn sample image and a rotated, tone-inverted copy of it; DINOv2 patch tokens plug into the same pipeline.

import numpy as np
from scipy import ndimage as ndi
from sklearn.datasets import load_sample_image
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

P = 14                                                   # patch size of the DINOv2 ViT-*/14 models
img1 = load_sample_image("china.jpg").mean(2)[:420, :630] / 255.0
# A "copy": rotated by 8 degrees, scaled by 0.92, with an inverted, non-linear tone curve
a, s = np.deg2rad(8), 0.92
M = np.array([[np.cos(a), -np.sin(a)], [np.sin(a), np.cos(a)]]) / s
off = np.array(img1.shape) / 2 - M @ (np.array(img1.shape) / 2)
img2 = 1.0 - ndi.affine_transform(img1, M, offset=off, order=1, mode="nearest") ** 0.6

def patch_features(img, bins=4):
    """Stand-in for ViT patch tokens: orientation histograms (modulo pi, so contrast-invariant)
    pooled in each 14 x 14 patch at two scales, concatenated over the 3 x 3 neighbouring patches."""
    h, w = img.shape[0] // P, img.shape[1] // P
    chans = []
    for sg in (1.0, 2.0):
        gy, gx = ndi.gaussian_filter(img, sg, order=(1, 0)), ndi.gaussian_filter(img, sg, order=(0, 1))
        mag, th = np.hypot(gx, gy), np.arctan2(gy, gx)
        for k in range(bins):
            ch = mag * np.cos(th - np.pi * k / bins) ** 2              # axial orientation bin
            chans.append(ch[:h * P, :w * P].reshape(h, P, w, P).mean((1, 3)))
    f = np.stack(chans, -1)                                            # (h, w, 2 * bins)
    f = np.concatenate([np.roll(f, (dy, dx), (0, 1)) for dy in (-1, 0, 1) for dx in (-1, 0, 1)], -1)
    f = np.sqrt(f.reshape(h * w, -1))
    f -= f.mean(0)
    return f / np.linalg.norm(f, axis=1, keepdims=True), (h, w)

f1, (h, w) = patch_features(img1)
f2, _ = patch_features(img2)
S = f1 @ f2.T                                            # cosine similarities of all patch pairs
nn12, nn21 = S.argmax(1), S.argmax(0)
mutual = np.flatnonzero(nn21[nn12] == np.arange(len(f1)))    # Definition 5.4
centre = lambda k: np.stack([k // w, k % w], 1) * P + P / 2
truth = (centre(np.arange(len(f1))) - off) @ np.linalg.inv(M).T    # where each patch of img1 lands
seen = np.all((truth >= 0) & (truth < np.array(img2.shape)), axis=1)
ok = np.linalg.norm(centre(nn12) - truth, axis=1) < P              # correct within one patch
print(f"plain nearest neighbours: {ok[seen].mean():.0%} correct ({seen.sum()} visible patches)")
print(f"mutual nearest neighbours: {len(mutual)} pairs, {ok[mutual].mean():.0%} correct")

# PCA of the patch features: three components mapped to RGB give a false-colour map
pca = PCA(n_components=3).fit(f1)
rgb = pca.transform(f1).reshape(h, w, 3)
rgb = (rgb - rgb.min((0, 1))) / (rgb.max((0, 1)) - rgb.min((0, 1)))
print("explained variance ratios:", pca.explained_variance_ratio_.round(3), "| false-colour map:", rgb.shape)

6. Image-to-image translation with CNNs

Many conservation questions take an image and ask for another: the sheet without its ink, the colours of a lost strip. This chapter gives two tools and two case studies.

6.1 The U-Net

The U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) has a contracting path of 3 \times 3 convolutions and 2 \times 2 max pooling, and an expanding path of up-convolutions. Skip connections concatenate each encoder level to the decoder level of the same resolution, so fine detail survives the bottleneck. The original network uses unpadded convolutions and maps a 572 \times 572 input to a 388 \times 388 output; large images are processed tile by tile.

Proposition 6.1 — Seamless tiling Let every output pixel of a network depend only on inputs within distance \rho = (r - 1)/2, where r is the receptive field of ch. 0.6. Run the network on overlapping tiles and keep, from each tile, only the pixels at distance at least \rho from its border. The assembled result equals the output on the whole image.

Proof. A kept pixel’s receptive field lies inside its tile, where tile and image coincide; padding effects reach at most \rho pixels from the border. ∎

6.2 VGG16, Gram matrices and style transfer

VGG16 (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2015) has 13 convolutional layers with 3 \times 3 kernels; its features, trained on ImageNet, serve as a perceptual measure. Gatys et al. (2016) separated content from style with them.

Definition 6.2 — Content and style losses Let F^l(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{N_l \times M_l} hold the feature maps of layer l at its M_l positions, and let G^l = F^l F^{l\top} be its Gram matrix.

For a content image c and a style image s, the pixels of x minimise \alpha \mathcal{L}_c + \beta \mathcal{L}_s, with \mathcal{L}_c = \tfrac12 \big\|F^{l_c}(x) - F^{l_c}(c)\big\|^2, \qquad \mathcal{L}_s = \sum_l \frac{w_l}{4 N_l^2 M_l^2} \big\|G^l(x) - G^l(s)\big\|_F^2 . Gatys et al. take the content at layer conv4_2 and the style at conv1_1 to conv5_1.

Proposition 6.3 — The style loss is a maximum mean discrepancy Let f_m and p_m be the columns of F, P \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}, and k(a, b) = (a^\top b)^2. Then \big\|F F^\top - P P^\top\big\|_F^2 = \sum_{m, m'} \big[k(f_m, f_{m'}) + k(p_m, p_{m'}) - 2\, k(f_m, p_{m'})\big], which is M^2 times the squared MMD between the two sets of columns. The Gram matrix ignores the order of positions.

Proof. F F^\top = \sum_m f_m f_m^\top, and \langle a a^\top, b b^\top \rangle_F = (a^\top b)^2; expand the squared norm. Permuting columns does not change \sum_m f_m f_m^\top. ∎

Style is thus matched as a distribution of local features, wherever they occur (Li et al., 2017). The code checks the identity, then synthesises a texture with random convolutional features, which already capture much of a texture (Ustyuzhaninov et al., 2017).

import numpy as np
import torch
from sklearn.datasets import load_sample_image

torch.set_num_threads(1)
torch.manual_seed(0)

# (1) Squared Gram distance = M^2 MMD^2 with the kernel k(x, y) = (x . y)^2 (Proposition 6.3)
N, M = 16, 200                                    # channels, positions
Fx, Fy = torch.randn(N, M, dtype=torch.float64), 1.3 * torch.randn(N, M, dtype=torch.float64)
G = lambda F: F @ F.T
k = lambda A, B: (A.T @ B) ** 2                   # kernel between the columns (positions)
lhs = ((G(Fx) - G(Fy)) ** 2).sum()
rhs = k(Fx, Fx).sum() + k(Fy, Fy).sum() - 2 * k(Fx, Fy).sum()
print(f"Gram distance {lhs.item():.6e} = MMD form {rhs.item():.6e}")
print("Gram matrix invariant to shuffling the positions:", torch.allclose(G(Fx), G(Fx[:, torch.randperm(M)])))

# (2) Texture synthesis by Gram matching, with random convolutional features
ex = torch.tensor(load_sample_image("flower.jpg")[100:228:2, 200:328:2] / 255.0,
                  dtype=torch.float32).permute(2, 0, 1)[None]        # 64 x 64 colour exemplar
layers = [torch.nn.Conv2d(3, 32, 5, padding=2), torch.nn.Conv2d(32, 32, 5, padding=2),
          torch.nn.Conv2d(32, 32, 5, padding=2)]
for l in layers:
    l.requires_grad_(False)

def grams(x):
    out = []
    for i, l in enumerate(layers):
        x = torch.relu(l(x if i == 0 else torch.nn.functional.avg_pool2d(x, 2)))
        f = x.flatten(2)[0]
        out.append(f @ f.T / f.shape[1])
    return out

target = grams(ex)
img = torch.rand_like(ex).requires_grad_(True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([img], lr=0.03)
style = lambda x: sum(((g - t) ** 2).sum() for g, t in zip(grams(x), target))
print(f"style loss at start: {style(img).item():.4f}")
for step in range(250):
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = style(img)
    loss.backward(); opt.step()
    img.data.clamp_(0, 1)
centred = lambda t: (t - t.mean((2, 3), keepdim=True)).detach().flatten().numpy()
corr = np.corrcoef(centred(img), centred(ex))[0, 1]
print(f"style loss after 250 steps: {style(img).item():.4f}; "
      f"pixel correlation with the exemplar (channel means removed): {corr:.2f}")
print(f"channel means: exemplar {ex.mean((0, 2, 3)).numpy().round(2)}, synthesis {img.detach().mean((0, 2, 3)).numpy().round(2)}")

6.3 Case study: the Magic Eraser

In transmitted light, a sheet shows its watermark, chain lines and laid lines, which date and locate the paper; ink drawn on it hides them. By Proposition 1.6, in the log domain \log I = \log T_{\text{paper}} - d_{\text{ink}}, \qquad d_{\text{ink}} \ge 0 . A U-Net trained on synthetic pairs, clean sheets with simulated strokes, predicts the non-negative d_{\text{ink}}; adding it back erases the ink. In one channel, a bright watermark under ink looks like thinner ink; the shape of strokes, or a coloured ink, resolves the ambiguity. The revealed watermark is compared with repertories such as Briquet’s (1907).

import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from scipy import ndimage as ndi

torch.set_num_threads(1)
torch.manual_seed(0)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
S = 40
tint = np.log([0.95, 0.90, 0.80])[:, None, None]          # colour of the paper in transmitted light
a_ink = np.array([0.6, 1.0, 1.6])[:, None, None]          # optical density of a brown ink per channel

def sheet():
    """Log transmitted light (R, G, B) of laid paper with a watermark, covered by pen strokes."""
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:S, 0:S]
    paper = 0.6 + 0.03 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * yy / 2.2)                       # laid lines
    paper += 0.08 * (np.abs((xx + rng.integers(50)) % 50 - 25) < 1)         # a chain line
    cy, cx, r = rng.uniform(12, 28), rng.uniform(12, 28), rng.uniform(5, 10)
    ring = np.abs(np.hypot(yy - cy, xx - cx) - r) < 0.8                     # watermark: thinner paper
    paper += 0.12 * ring + 0.02 * ndi.gaussian_filter(rng.standard_normal((S, S)), 3)
    ink = np.zeros((S, S))
    for _ in range(rng.integers(2, 5)):                                     # quadratic Bezier strokes
        t = np.linspace(0, 1, 200)[:, None]
        p0, p1, p2 = rng.uniform(0, S, (3, 2))
        cv = np.clip(((1 - t) ** 2 * p0 + 2 * t * (1 - t) * p1 + t ** 2 * p2).astype(int), 0, S - 1)
        ink[cv[:, 0], cv[:, 1]] = 1
    ink = ndi.gaussian_filter(ndi.binary_dilation(ink, iterations=rng.integers(1, 3)) * 1.0, 0.7)
    clean = np.log(paper)[None] + tint
    seen = clean - a_ink * ink + 0.01 * rng.standard_normal((3, S, S))     # Beer-Lambert: additive in log
    return seen, clean, ring, ink > 0.3

def unet(cin, c=8):
    """A two-level U-Net whose output adds back a non-negative ink density."""
    block = lambda i, o: nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(i, o, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(o, o, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU())
    class UNet(nn.Module):
        def __init__(self):
            super().__init__()
            self.e1, self.e2, self.mid = block(cin, c), block(c, 2 * c), block(2 * c, 4 * c)
            self.u2, self.d2 = nn.ConvTranspose2d(4 * c, 2 * c, 2, stride=2), block(4 * c, 2 * c)
            self.u1, self.d1 = nn.ConvTranspose2d(2 * c, c, 2, stride=2), block(2 * c, c)
            self.out = nn.Conv2d(c, cin, 1)
            nn.init.zeros_(self.out.weight); nn.init.constant_(self.out.bias, -1.0)
        def forward(self, x):
            e1 = self.e1(x)
            e2 = self.e2(nn.functional.max_pool2d(e1, 2))
            m = self.mid(nn.functional.max_pool2d(e2, 2))
            d2 = self.d2(torch.cat([self.u2(m), e2], 1))                   # skip connection
            d1 = self.d1(torch.cat([self.u1(d2), e1], 1))                  # skip connection
            return x + nn.functional.softplus(self.out(d1))                # ink only darkens: add back >= 0
    return UNet()

train = [sheet() for _ in range(2400)]
test = [sheet() for _ in range(50)]

def visibility(img, b):
    """Watermark contrast-to-clutter ratio on the inked pixels (log units)."""
    on, off = img[b[2] & b[3]], img[~b[2] & b[3]]
    return (on.mean() - off.mean()) / off.std()

model = unet(3)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for step in range(150):
    batch = train[16 * step: 16 * step + 16]
    x = torch.tensor(np.stack([b[0] for b in batch]), dtype=torch.float32)
    y = torch.tensor(np.stack([b[1] for b in batch]), dtype=torch.float32)
    loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(model(x), y)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
with torch.no_grad():
    out = model(torch.tensor(np.stack([b[0] for b in test]), dtype=torch.float32)).numpy()

keep = [i for i, b in enumerate(test) if (b[2] & b[3]).sum() > 3]      # watermark crosses the ink
for name, imgs in [("inked input", [b[0] for b in test]), ("after erasing", out), ("clean paper", [b[1] for b in test])]:
    g = [imgs[i].mean(0) for i in range(len(test))]                     # grey level, log units
    err = np.mean([np.sqrt(np.mean((g[i] - test[i][1].mean(0))[test[i][3]] ** 2)) for i in keep])
    vis = np.mean([visibility(g[i], test[i]) for i in keep])
    print(f"{name:13s}: RMS error under the ink {err:.3f}, watermark visibility {vis:5.2f}")

After 150 steps on a CPU, the error under the ink drops tenfold, but the watermark is still far less visible than on the clean sheet: closing that gap is Exercise 18.

6.4 Case study: the Night Watch

In 1715 Rembrandt’s Night Watch was cut down on all sides, most of all on the left, to fit a wall of Amsterdam’s town hall. A smaller seventeenth-century copy attributed to Gerrit Lundens, in the National Gallery, London, shows the full composition. The Rijksmuseum showed reconstructed strips with the painting in 2021. A reconstruction of this kind has four steps.

  1. Register the copy to the painting on the region both show (ch. 2); the copy’s proportions are not exact, so the warp must be local.
  2. On that overlap \Omega_o, learn a translation from the copy’s handling and colours to Rembrandt’s (ch. 6.1–6.2).
  3. Apply it on the missing strips \Omega_m.
  4. Validate on strips of \Omega_o held out from training, with the errors reported.

The result is a hypothesis about the lost parts, not a restoration: the network extrapolates from \Omega_o to \Omega_m.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • U-Net skip connections keep fine detail; overlap tiles by the receptive-field radius.
  • The Gram style loss is an MMD between distributions of local features.
  • Ink removal works in the log domain, with a non-negative ink density.
  • A reconstruction learned on an overlap is a hypothesis, validated on held-out strips.

7. Complements: optics of paint, information and uncertainty

This self-study chapter deepens four points of the course: why pigments mixed in one layer do not mix linearly, how to compare modalities without learning, why contrastive learning needs large batches, and how to attach honest error bars to a reconstruction.

7.1 Kubelka–Munk: the optics of a paint layer

Proposition 1.4 followed light through the paint once. In a turbid layer, light is absorbed and scattered many times. The two-flux model of Kubelka and Munk (1931) follows a downward flux i(z) and an upward flux j(z) at depth z, with absorption coefficient K and scattering coefficient S: \frac{di}{dz} = -(K + S)\, i + S\, j, \qquad \frac{dj}{dz} = (K + S)\, j - S\, i .

Proposition 7.1 — Reflectance of an opaque layer For a layer thick enough to hide its support, the reflectance R_\infty = j/i at the surface satisfies R_\infty = 1 + \frac{K}{S} - \sqrt{\Big(\frac{K}{S}\Big)^2 + 2\,\frac{K}{S}}, \qquad \frac{K}{S} = \frac{(1 - R_\infty)^2}{2 R_\infty} .

Proof.

  • Let \rho = j/i. Differentiating and using the two equations gives \frac{d\rho}{dz} = -S\rho^2 + 2(K + S)\rho - S .
  • In an infinitely thick layer the ratio does not depend on depth, so S\rho^2 - 2(K + S)\rho + S = 0; the root in [0, 1] is R_\infty.
  • The quadratic says R_\infty + 1/R_\infty = 2(1 + K/S), which rearranges into the second formula. ∎

Definition 7.2 — Mixing rule For pigments with absorption and scattering spectra K_i(\lambda), S_i(\lambda) per unit concentration, mixed at concentrations c_i in one layer, \Big(\frac{K}{S}\Big)_{\text{mix}} = \frac{\sum_i c_i K_i}{\sum_i c_i S_i} .

The reflectance of a mixture is therefore not a mixture of reflectances. But the rule is linear in c once q = (K/S)_{\text{mix}} is computed from the measured reflectance: \sum_i c_i\, \big(K_i(\lambda) - q(\lambda)\, S_i(\lambda)\big) = 0 \quad \text{for every } \lambda . With \sum_i c_i = 1, non-negative least squares recovers the concentrations. The code compares the two unmixings for a strong blue in a white.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import nnls

lam = np.linspace(400, 700, 61)
# Absorption K and scattering S (per unit concentration) of two synthetic pigments
K = np.stack([0.02 + 0 * lam,                                     # white: weak absorption
              0.05 + 2.0 / (1 + np.exp(-(lam - 560) / 15))], 1)   # blue: absorbs red light
S = np.stack([1.0 + 0 * lam, 0.3 + 0 * lam], 1)                   # white scatters strongly

def R_inf(k_over_s):
    """Reflectance of an opaque layer (Proposition 7.1)."""
    return 1 + k_over_s - np.sqrt(k_over_s**2 + 2 * k_over_s)

c_true = np.array([0.8, 0.2])                                     # concentrations
r_mix = R_inf((K @ c_true) / (S @ c_true))                        # mixing rule (Definition 7.2)
R_pure = R_inf(K / S)                                             # spectra of the pure pigments

# (a) Linear unmixing of reflectances, as in Definition 1.5
w = 100.0
a_lin, _ = nnls(np.vstack([R_pure, w * np.ones(2)]), np.append(r_mix, w))
# (b) Unmixing in K/S space: sum_i c_i (K_i - q S_i) = 0 with q = (K/S) of the mixture
q = (1 - r_mix) ** 2 / (2 * r_mix)
A = K - q[:, None] * S
c_km, _ = nnls(np.vstack([A, w * np.ones(2)]), np.r_[np.zeros(len(lam)), w])
print("true concentrations   :", c_true)
print("linear unmixing       :", a_lin.round(3))
print("Kubelka-Munk unmixing :", c_km.round(3))
print(f"reflectance at 650 nm: white {R_pure[50, 0]:.3f}, blue {R_pure[50, 1]:.3f}, mixture {r_mix[50]:.3f}")

A fifth of blue pulls the reflectance at 650 nm from 0.82 to 0.38, so linear unmixing reads about half blue. In the infrared, both K and S of most paints decrease, so a layer of given thickness hides less of what lies below: this is the physics behind ch. 1.2.

7.2 Mutual information as a similarity

Definition 7.3 — Mutual information For random variables U and V with joint law p_{UV}, I(U; V) = \mathrm{KL}\big(p_{UV} \,\big\|\, p_U \otimes p_V\big) = H(U) + H(V) - H(U, V) \ge 0 .

Proposition 7.4 — Invariance to recoding For discrete variables and any bijection \phi of the values of U, I(\phi(U); V) = I(U; V).

Proof. A bijection only relabels the rows of the joint probability table; the entropies H(U) and H(U, V) are sums over entries and do not depend on labels. ∎

So mutual information, estimated from the joint histogram of the intensities of two overlapping images, tolerates any one-to-one intensity relation, monotone or not. Registration by maximisation of mutual information over the transform parameters was introduced by Viola and Wells (1997) and Maes et al. (1997). It needs no training data, but its histograms are noisy on small tiles, where the learned filters of Definition 2.11 are more precise.

7.3 Contrastive learning and mutual information

Proposition 7.5 — The InfoNCE bound Let (X, Y) be a positive pair, and Y_2, \dots, Y_N independent negatives drawn from the marginal of Y. For every score function, the expected image-to-text InfoNCE loss \mathcal{L}_N of Definition 5.1 satisfies I(X; Y) \ge \log N - \mathcal{L}_N .

Proof. Sketch (van den Oord et al., 2018; Poole et al., 2019). The loss is the cross-entropy of guessing which of N candidates is the positive. The guess is no better than the Bayes posterior, whose expected log-probability is bounded through the density ratio p(y \mid x)/p(y); averaging that ratio over the negatives gives the bound. ∎

The bound cannot exceed \log N, so estimating large dependencies requires many negatives: CLIP used batches of 32,768 pairs. Duplicates in a batch (Proposition 5.2) raise the floor of the loss without saying anything about the encoders.

7.4 Honest error bars for reconstructions

A reconstruction such as that of ch. 6.4 is validated on held-out strips. Split conformal prediction turns those strips into a guaranteed interval.

Proposition 7.6 — Split conformal coverage Let e_1, \dots, e_n be the absolute errors of a fixed predictor on calibration points, and e_{n+1} its error on a new point, the n + 1 errors being exchangeable.

Let \hat q be the \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil-th smallest of e_1, \dots, e_n, or +\infty if that index exceeds n. Then \mathbb{P}\big(e_{n+1} \le \hat q\big) \ge 1 - \alpha .

Proof. By exchangeability, the rank of e_{n+1} among the n + 1 errors is uniform on \{1, \dots, n+1\} (ties broken at random). The event e_{n+1} \le \hat q contains the event that this rank is at most \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil, which has probability at least 1 - \alpha. ∎

The guarantee is marginal, and it rests on exchangeability. Neighbouring pixels are dependent, and a missing strip differs from the overlap; calibrate on whole held-out blocks, and report the interval as conditional on that assumption (Vovk et al., 2005; Lei et al., 2018).

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • In a turbid layer, K/S = (1 - R_\infty)^2 / (2R_\infty), and mixtures are linear in K/S, not in reflectance.
  • Mutual information is invariant to any one-to-one recoding of intensities.
  • InfoNCE bounds mutual information by \log N minus the loss: large batches are needed.
  • Split conformal prediction gives coverage 1 - \alpha under exchangeability; spatial data need block-wise calibration.

8. Research directions and open problems

This self-study chapter surveys active questions, with entry points in the literature. Each item names a problem that the methods of the course do not yet solve.

8.1 Registration and matching

  • Detector-free matchers, such as LoFTR (Sun et al., 2021) and RoMa (Edstedt et al., 2024), and fast learned matchers, such as LightGlue (Lindenberger et al., 2023), have changed matching between photographs.
  • Open problem: matching between modalities that share few edges, such as element maps and photographs, at gigapixel scale and with sub-pixel accuracy, with reliable estimates of the local error.

8.2 From spectra and element maps to materials

  • Reflectance spectra, element maps and radiographs see the same layers through different physics. Hyperspectral unmixing is reviewed by Bioucas-Dias et al. (2012); the physics of mixtures is that of ch. 7.1.
  • Open problems: joint inversion of RIS and MA-XRF into pigment maps with uncertainty, and models of layering, where the order of layers matters as much as their content.

8.3 Microstructure

  • Convolutional networks now detect cracks in paintings, as in the work of Sizyakin et al. (2020).
  • Open problems: a typology of craquelure learned from data and related to supports and schools, as Bucklow (1997) did by hand; and standards of statistical evidence for canvas matches, since profiles are autocorrelated and searches over shifts are multiple comparisons (Example 4.6).

8.4 Foundation models and digitised collections

  • Work by Shen, Efros and Aubry (2019) discovers repeated motifs in art collections with spatially consistent features. Elgammal et al. (2018) studied how networks trained on style labels organise art history. Mensink and van Gemert (2014) built a recognition benchmark from the Rijksmuseum collection.
  • Open problems: the domain gap between web photographs and paintings, drawings or micrographs; fine-tuning on small expert-labelled sets; and search interfaces whose semantic directions are checked by art historians, not only by retrieval scores.

8.5 Generative reconstruction

  • Inpainting has moved from partial convolutions (Liu et al., 2018) to diffusion models, such as RePaint (Lugmayr et al., 2022).
  • Open problems: calibrated uncertainty for reconstructed regions (ch. 7.4), and conventions to disclose what is measured, what is inferred and what is invented in an image shown to the public.

8.6 Attribution and connoisseurship

  • Tummers (2011) examines how connoisseurs authenticate paintings by Rembrandt and his contemporaries. Computational studies have proposed statistical signatures of artists: Lyu, Rockmore and Farid (2004) used wavelet statistics, and Johnson et al. (2008) compared several image-processing approaches to attributing paintings to Van Gogh from his brushwork.
  • Open problems: small data sets, workshops where several hands painted one work, deliberate forgeries, and the communication of evidence as calibrated probabilities that a connoisseur can weigh against other evidence.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • Learned matching works between photographs; matching across physical modalities remains open.
  • Joint inversion of spectral, elemental and radiographic data into layered material maps is a frontier.
  • Foundation models need adaptation, and expert checks, to serve art history.
  • Reconstructions and attributions need calibrated uncertainty and transparent communication.

  8. ★ Tiling a gigapixel image. (a) Compute the receptive field of two 3 \times 3 convolutions, a 2 \times 2 pooling, then two 3 \times 3 convolutions (ch. 0.6). (b) A U-Net with a receptive field of 93 pixels runs on tiles of 512 pixels. By Proposition 6.1, what core is kept per tile, and how many tiles cover a 40\,000 \times 30\,000 image? (c) What is the computational overhead?

    Solution: (a) r = 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 \times 2 + 2 \times 2 = 14.

    (b) The margin is \rho = 46, so the core is 512 - 92 = 420 pixels, and the number of tiles is \lceil 40\,000/420 \rceil \times \lceil 30\,000/420 \rceil = 96 \times 72 = 6912 .

    (c) (512/420)^2 \approx 1.49: about 49 % more computation than one pass over the image.

  9. ★ Mutual nearest neighbours. The cosine similarities between three patches of a painting (rows) and three patches of a print after it (columns) are S = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 & 0.4 \\ 0.8 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\ 0.1 & 0.6 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}. (a) Find the correspondences of Definition 5.4. (b) Why is the second patch left unmatched, and why is that desirable?

    Solution: (a) Row maxima: 1→1, 2→1, 3→2. Column maxima: 1→1, 2→3, 3→3. The mutual pairs are (1, 1) and (3, 2).

    (b) Patch 2 prefers column 1, which prefers patch 1: two patches compete for one partner. Discarding such one-sided matches removes most errors, as the code of ch. 5.5 shows (61 % against 82 % correct).

  10. ★★ The homography of a plane. (a) Write the two linear equations in H given by one match, and explain why four matches in general position determine H. (b) Deduce from Proposition 2.3 that a camera rotating about its centre also gives a homography, whatever the scene. (c) Show that H is affine when K = K', R = I, the plane is n = (0, 0, 1)^\top and the translation is parallel to it.

    Solution: (a) With x' = (u, v, 1) and rows h_1, h_2, h_3 of H: h_1^\top x - u\, h_3^\top x = 0 and h_2^\top x - v\, h_3^\top x = 0. H has 8 degrees of freedom (scale is free), and four points, no three collinear, give 8 independent equations.

    (b) With t = 0, H = K' R K^{-1} does not depend on the plane: every point maps the same way.

    (c) Here H = I + K t n^\top K^{-1} / d . With n = e_3 and t_3 = 0, the third entry of K t is zero, because the last row of K is e_3^\top. So H keeps the last row (0, 0, 1) of the identity: it is affine.

  11. ★★ The σ-marginalised weight. (a) Check Proposition 2.7 numerically for r = 1 and \sigma_{\max} = 2 by quadrature. (b) Compute the weight w(r) = \operatorname{erfc}\big(r / (\sqrt2\,\sigma_{\max})\big) at r equal to one, two and three times \sigma_{\max}, and compare with a hard threshold at \sigma_{\max}. (c) Show that p(r) integrates to 1.

    Solution: (a) Both sides equal 0.3867.

    (b) 0.317, 0.046 and 0.003: matches just beyond the scale still count a little, far ones not at all, and no single threshold has to be chosen.

    (c) With u = r/(\sqrt2\,\sigma_{\max}) and \int_0^\infty \operatorname{erfc}(u)\,du = 1/\sqrt\pi, the integral is \frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}} \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}} \times \frac{\sqrt2\,\sigma_{\max}}{\sqrt\pi} = 1 .

  12. ★★ Dimension and surprisal. (a) Minimise c_1 h^4 + c_2/(n h^d) in h and give the resulting rate (Theorem 3.2). (b) By which factor must n grow to halve the error for d = 4, and for the d = 300 bands of a RIS cube? What follows for surprisal maps of spectra? (c) Prove Proposition 3.4.

    Solution: (a) h^\star = \big(d\, c_2 / (4 c_1 n)\big)^{1/(d+4)}, and the error decreases as n^{-4/(d+4)}.

    (b) By 2^{(d+4)/4}: a factor 4 for d = 4, and 2^{76} for d = 300. Spectra must first be reduced to a few components (PCA or unmixing, Definition 1.5).

    (c) Given U = u, V is normal with mean m(u), linear in u, and variance s^2; take minus the logarithm of its density.

  13. ★★ How significant is a canvas match? (Python) In the code of ch. 4.2: (a) apply Fisher’s transform of Example 4.6 to the same-roll correlation and to the largest unrelated one, with n = 24 overlapping tiles. (b) Estimate the lag-one autocorrelation \rho_1 of a profile and the effective sample size. (c) Replace the 20 unrelated rolls by 200, and report an empirical p-value.

    Hints and expected result: (a) z \approx 8.4 for r = 0.95, but also z \approx 5.2 for the chance correlation of 0.81: the naive test would call both significant.

    (b) For an AR(1)-like profile, n_{\text{eff}} \approx n (1 - \rho_1)/(1 + \rho_1), much smaller than n for smooth profiles. (c) The empirical p-value is the fraction of unrelated rolls that beat the observed correlation; with no exceedance in 200, report p < 1/201.

  14. ★★ The floor of a contrastive loss. (Python) In the code of ch. 5.3, the image cannot know which of the three templates its caption used. (a) With 20 classes (digit and ground) in batches of 256, estimate the floor of Proposition 5.2 and compare with the printed final loss. (b) Prove the analogue of Proposition 5.2 for the text-to-image term. (c) Modify the loss so that all captions of the same class count as positives, and observe the new final loss.

    Hints and expected result: (a) Each caption has on average 1 + 255/20 \approx 13.75 indistinguishable variants in the batch, so the floor is about \log 13.75 \approx 2.6; the code prints 2.601.

    (b) Images of one class with the same caption are interchangeable for the text encoder, so the same argument applies by columns. (c) With a multi-positive target the loss can approach 0; the retrieval metrics barely change.

  15. ★★ What the Gram matrix sees. (a) Prove \langle a a^\top, b b^\top \rangle_F = (a^\top b)^2 and deduce Proposition 6.3. (b) For a single feature channel, show that the style loss only compares the mean of f_m^2. (c) Show that the Gram matrix of centred features is M times their covariance, and relate this to matching channel means and variances, as adaptive instance normalisation does (Huang and Belongie, 2017).

    Solution: (a) For vectors a and b, \langle a a^\top, b b^\top \rangle_F = \operatorname{tr}(a a^\top b b^\top) = (a^\top b)^2 ; expand the squared norm of \sum_m f_m f_m^\top - \sum_m p_m p_m^\top with it.

    (b) For N = 1, G = \sum_m f_m^2, a scalar: two images with the same energy have the same style, whatever their arrangement.

    (c) The Gram matrix of centred features is \sum_m (f_m - \bar f)(f_m - \bar f)^\top = M\, \widehat{\mathrm{Cov}} . AdaIN matches only the diagonal (means and variances per channel), a cheaper and weaker version of Gram matching.

  16. ★★ Kubelka–Munk unmixing. (Python) (a) From Proposition 7.1, compute K/S for R_\infty = 0.82, 0.38 and 0.064, and check against the code of ch. 7.1. (b) Explain why linear unmixing overestimates the blue. (c) Add 1 % noise and a third pigment to the code, and compare the errors of the two unmixings over 100 noisy draws.

    Hints and expected result: (a) 0.0198, 0.506 and 6.84; the blue at 650 nm has K/S = 2.045/0.3 \approx 6.8, and the mixture 0.425/0.86 \approx 0.49.

    (b) The mixture’s reflectance is far below the average of the pure reflectances, since absorption adds up in K/S, not in R; linear unmixing explains the darkness with too much blue. (c) The Kubelka–Munk unmixing stays close to the truth; the linear one keeps a bias that noise does not remove.

  17. ★★★ Calibrated evidence for an attribution. A classifier trained on tiles of authenticated paintings by an artist and by contemporaries gives scores to the tiles of a disputed painting. (a) Explain why the accuracy on random held-out tiles overstates the evidence. (b) Propose a validation and a calibration that turn the scores into a likelihood ratio for the whole painting. (c) Discuss how this ratio could be weighed with other evidence (Tummers, 2011).

    Hints: (a) Tiles of one painting share support, ground, condition and photography: the classifier can learn the painting, not the hand. (b) Leave one painting out; aggregate the tile scores per painting; fit the score distributions under both hypotheses on held-out paintings, and report, with its uncertainty, the likelihood ratio \mathrm{LR} = \frac{p(\text{score} \mid \text{artist})}{p(\text{score} \mid \text{other})} .

    (c) In Bayesian terms, posterior odds are the likelihood ratio times the prior odds formed by provenance, documents and connoisseurship. Discuss workshop participation, the small number of works, and the risk of deliberately deceptive works.

  18. ★★★ Closing the gap of the Magic Eraser. (Python) In the code of ch. 6.3, the error under the ink drops tenfold, yet the watermark visibility stays far below that of the clean sheet. (a) List the causes you can test. (b) Train longer, and weight the loss on inked pixels; report the visibility over several seeds. (c) Propose a measurement that would make the separation of ink and watermark easier on real sheets.

    Hints and expected result: (a) The short training, errors at the edges of strokes, and the ambiguity between thin ink and thin paper.

    (b) In one run of ours, 600 steps instead of 150 raised the visibility from 1.4 to about 1.9, with an error of 0.054 under the ink; weighting the inked pixels five times gave about 2.1 and 0.046. The clean sheet is at 4.6. Report means and spreads over at least five seeds.

    (c) A second image in which the ink is more transparent, for instance in the infrared, where iron-gall inks are known to fade, or a raking-light image that shows the texture of the paper; then feed all channels to the network.

Projects and assessment

The official assessment is by projects, submitted as code and documented and demonstrated in Jupyter notebooks (master-mva.com). Université Paris-Saclay describes it as “Evaluation Continue non Intégrale”; no weights are displayed. The subjects below follow that format: a notebook that runs, explains and demonstrates, with a short report and a talk. Each reproduces or extends published work, in the MVA tradition. No written exam is announced; the exam proposal is for self-assessment.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: MAGSAC++ (Barath et al., 2020) against tuned RANSAC. Does marginalising over the noise scale beat a tuned threshold when the noise of correct matches varies across the image?
    • Implement the normalised DLT, RANSAC, the simplified weight of Proposition 2.7 and the MAGSAC++ weights of the paper, with incomplete gamma functions.
    • Data: synthetic matches with radial distortion and noise growing towards the corners; optionally, image pairs of the public HPatches benchmark (Balntas et al., 2017).
    • Success criterion: grid reprojection error within 10 % of an oracle fit on the true inliers, over 20 seeds and all thresholds tried. Deliverables: notebook, 4–6 page report, 10-minute talk. Chapters 0 and 2.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: style as a distribution (Gatys et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017). Which feature statistics reproduce the brushwork of a painting?
    • Synthesise textures from crops of public-domain paintings (open-access images of the Rijksmuseum or the Metropolitan Museum of Art) with pretrained VGG16 features from torchvision.
    • Compare Gram matching on several layer sets, matching of channel means and variances only, and random features (Ustyuzhaninov et al., 2017); verify Proposition 6.3 numerically.
    • Success criterion: a figure per method, and an MMD on held-out VGG features ranking the methods. Deliverables: notebook, report, talk. Chapters 5.3 and 6.2.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Paper study: weave analysis from radiographs (Johnson, Johnson and Erdmann, 2013), on simulated canvases. How long must the common part of two canvases be for a reliable match?
    • Extend the simulator of ch. 4.2 with thread-thickness variations, a slight skew of the weave and stronger noise.
    • Implement thread counting by tile spectra, warp matching over shifts and flips, and a null from 500 unrelated rolls.
    • Success criterion: ROC curves of same-roll detection against the overlap length, and the overlap needed for a 90 % detection rate at a 1 % false-match rate. Deliverables: notebook, report, talk. Chapters 1.4 and 4.2.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] A multimodal registration pipeline, end to end. Simulate a stack from an open-access image of a painting: an IRR-like channel with a synthetic underdrawing, element-like maps with Poisson noise, each modality with its own homography, lens distortion and smooth local warp.
    • Member 1: keypoints, descriptors (handcrafted, or HardNet through the Kornia library of Riba et al., 2020), matching, robust homography with distortion (ch. 2.1–2.4).
    • Member 2: patchwise offsets with tile stacks, structure-tensor weights and learned filters (Definition 2.11).
    • Member 3: conditional surprisal on the registered stack (ch. 3.1), as a function of the residual misregistration.
    • Success criterion: a median residual below 0.3 px, and a curve of underdrawing detection (AUC) against registration error. Deliverables: notebooks, a 6–8 page report, a 20-minute talk. Chapters 1–3.
  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Paper study: CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) on an open-access collection. Download a few thousand public-domain images with their metadata from the Metropolitan Museum of Art or the Rijksmuseum, and run the pretrained models on a free GPU tier.
    • Member 1: a search engine with an approximate nearest-neighbour index; precision at 10 on queries built from the metadata.
    • Member 2: semantic directions (Definition 5.3) tested against dates and techniques of the metadata, with an analysis of failures.
    • Member 3: DINOv2 correspondences (Definition 5.4) between works and prints or copies after them, and PCA maps.
    • Success criterion: precision at 10 above a keyword baseline, and a hand-checked set of correspondences with its error rate. Deliverables: notebooks, report, talk. Chapter 5.
  3. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Paper study: U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) for ink removal, after the Magic Eraser case. Can a watermark hidden under a drawing be identified after erasing the ink?
    • Member 1: a simulator of sheets in transmitted light (laid and chain lines, 100 synthetic watermark designs, inks with spectral signatures).
    • Member 2: the U-Net with a non-negative ink density, trained with the ablations of Exercise 18.
    • Member 3: watermark retrieval from the synthetic repertory, and conformal error bars (Proposition 7.6) on held-out sheets.
    • Success criterion: top-1 retrieval of the right watermark under the ink above 80 %. Deliverables: notebooks, report, talk. Chapters 1, 6 and 7.

Written exam (proposal)

Two hours, one handwritten A4 sheet and a calculator allowed; four questions of 5 points.

  1. Question (5 points). For visible photography, infrared reflectography, radiography and MA-XRF, state the physical quantity measured, the depth reached and one question about a painting that each modality helps answer. Then explain, with a model, why an underdrawing appears in the infrared. Expected: the table of ch. 1 (reflectance at the surface; reflectance below paint; X-ray transmission dominated by lead white; element fluorescence attenuated with depth), and Proposition 1.4 with a transmittance that grows with the wavelength over a carbon-based drawing.

  2. Question (5 points). (a) A third of the matches are correct: how many RANSAC draws give 99 % confidence for a homography? (b) With f = 6000 px and k_1 = -0.01, how far is a point at r = 0.5 displaced? (c) A 128-pixel tile at 0.05 mm per pixel, zero-padded to 1024 points, has its spectral peak at index 72: give the thread density.

    Expected: (a) \log 0.01 / \log(1 - 3^{-4}) \approx 370.7, so 371 draws. (b) 0.01 \times 0.5^3 \times 6000 = 7.5 px. (c) 72/1024 = 0.0703 cycles per pixel, divided by 0.005 cm: about 14.1 threads per cm.

  3. Question (5 points). Prove Proposition 2.10 from the shift theorem. Then derive the normal equations of Proposition 2.8, and explain why a tile containing a single straight edge cannot be registered in both directions. Expected: the cross-power spectrum reduces to a pure phase whose inverse DFT is a Kronecker delta; linearisation of A(x - \delta), the system J\delta = -\sum \nabla A\,(B - A), and a rank-one structure tensor when all gradients are parallel.

  4. Question (5 points). Prove Proposition 6.3, and deduce that two images whose features are the same up to a permutation of positions have the same style loss. What does this imply for the transfer of style to a painting with a given composition? Expected: \langle a a^\top, b b^\top\rangle_F = (a^\top b)^2, expansion into the three kernel sums, invariance of \sum_m f_m f_m^\top to permutations; style is a distribution of local features, so composition must come from the content loss.
