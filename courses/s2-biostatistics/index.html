Empirical first: give a drug with no effect at all to the patients of a cohort who happen to start it during follow-up, then compare “users” with “non-users” from the start of follow-up. Biostatistics is the statistics of clinical research. The course follows the two goals that the official description puts at its centre, prediction and causation, and the methods it singles out as not covered elsewhere in the master: the design of studies and trials, the analysis of censored (time-to-event) data, and causal inference.

0. Review: statistical inference for clinical data

Clinical research reports almost every result as an effect measure with a confidence interval or a test. This chapter recalls the M1-level tools behind those numbers, on the objects used throughout the course: proportions, two-by-two tables and the logistic model.

0.1 Wald intervals and the delta method

Definition 0.1 — Asymptotically normal estimator, Wald interval An estimator \hat\theta_n of a real parameter \theta is asymptotically normal with asymptotic variance \sigma^2 if \sqrt{n}\,(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{\ d\ } \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2).

Let \widehat{\mathrm{se}} satisfy \sqrt{n}\,\widehat{\mathrm{se}} \to \sigma in probability, and let z_q be the q-quantile of the standard normal law, so that z_{0.975} = 1.96. The Wald interval \hat\theta_n \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\, \widehat{\mathrm{se}} then has asymptotic coverage 1 - \alpha.

For a proportion \hat p = X/n with X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p), the central limit theorem gives the standard error \sqrt{\hat p (1 - \hat p)/n}. Ratios are better handled on the log scale, which needs the delta method.

Proposition 0.2 — Delta method Assume \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) converges in distribution to \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), and let g be differentiable at \theta. Then \sqrt{n}\,\big(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)\big) \xrightarrow{\ d\ } \mathcal{N}\big(0,\ g'(\theta)^2 \sigma^2\big).

Proof. Asymptotic normality implies \hat\theta_n \to \theta in probability. By differentiability, g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta) = \big(g'(\theta) + r_n\big)(\hat\theta_n - \theta), \qquad r_n \to 0 \text{ in probability}.

Multiply by \sqrt n. The term g'(\theta)\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) converges to \mathcal{N}(0, g'(\theta)^2\sigma^2).

The remainder r_n \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) is the product of a term bounded in probability and a term tending to zero, so it tends to zero. Slutsky’s lemma concludes. ∎

The multivariate version replaces g'(\theta)^2\sigma^2 by \nabla g(\theta)^\top \Sigma\, \nabla g(\theta).

0.2 Two-by-two tables and effect measures

Definition 0.3 — Risk difference, risk ratio, odds ratio Let p_1 and p_0 be the probabilities of an event, for example death within one year, in a treated and a control group. The three usual effect measures are \mathrm{RD} = p_1 - p_0, \qquad \mathrm{RR} = \frac{p_1}{p_0}, \qquad \mathrm{OR} = \frac{p_1/(1-p_1)}{p_0/(1-p_0)} .

The number needed to treat is \mathrm{NNT} = 1/|\mathrm{RD}|: the number of patients to treat to avoid one event, on average.

When the event is rare in both groups, 1 - p_a \approx 1 and the odds ratio is close to the risk ratio. With frequent events they differ, and the odds ratio is always further from 1 than the risk ratio.

Proposition 0.4 — Standard errors on the log scale Let a events occur among n_1 treated patients and c among n_0 controls, independently, and set b = n_1 - a, d = n_0 - c. Then, asymptotically, \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\log \widehat{\mathrm{RR}}) = \frac1a - \frac1{n_1} + \frac1c - \frac1{n_0}, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\log \widehat{\mathrm{OR}}) = \frac1a + \frac1b + \frac1c + \frac1d .

Proof. For one group, apply Proposition 0.2 with g(p) = \log p. Since g'(p) = 1/p, \mathrm{Var}(\log \hat p) \approx \frac{1}{p^2}\cdot\frac{p(1-p)}{n} = \frac{1}{np} - \frac1n , which is estimated by 1/a - 1/n_1.

For the odds, take g(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}, so that g'(p) = \frac{1}{p(1-p)}. Then \mathrm{Var}(g(\hat p)) \approx \frac{1}{np(1-p)} = \frac{1}{np} + \frac{1}{n(1-p)}, estimated by 1/a + 1/b. The two groups are independent, so the variances of the log ratios are sums. ∎

The interval for the odds ratio is due to Woolf (1955). The intervals are computed on the log scale and then exponentiated, so they are not symmetric around the estimate.

Example 0.5 — A two-arm trial A trial records 30 deaths among 200 treated patients and 50 among 200 controls, so \hat p_1 = 0.15 and \hat p_0 = 0.25. Code 0.9 gives:

  • risk difference -0.10, 95 % CI [-0.178, -0.022], hence \mathrm{NNT} = 10;
  • risk ratio 0.60, 95 % CI [0.399, 0.902];
  • odds ratio 0.529, 95 % CI [0.320, 0.876].

All three intervals exclude the value of no effect, which is 0 for a difference and 1 for a ratio.

0.3 Tests, errors and power

Definition 0.6 — Test, error rates, p-value A test of a null hypothesis H_0 rejects H_0 when a statistic falls in a rejection region.

  • Its type I error rate \alpha is the probability of rejecting H_0 when H_0 is true.
  • Its power 1 - \beta is the probability of rejecting H_0 under a given alternative; \beta is the type II error rate.
  • The p-value is the smallest level at which the observed data lead to rejection.

The two-sided Wald test of \theta = \theta_0 at level \alpha rejects exactly when \theta_0 lies outside the Wald interval of level 1 - \alpha. This duality is used in ch. 2 for non-inferiority trials.

Reading a p-value A p-value is not the probability that H_0 is true. A large p-value in a small trial is not evidence that the treatment has no effect: “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence” (Altman and Bland, 1995). Report the effect and its confidence interval, not only the test.

0.4 Likelihood and logistic regression

Definition 0.7 — Logistic regression For a binary outcome Y and a covariate vector x \in \mathbb{R}^p whose first entry is 1, \log \frac{P(Y = 1 \mid x)}{1 - P(Y = 1 \mid x)} = x^\top \beta .

For a binary covariate x_j, e^{\beta_j} is the odds ratio comparing x_j = 1 with x_j = 0 at fixed values of the other covariates.

Proposition 0.8 — Score, information and Newton–Raphson Write p_i = 1/(1 + e^{-x_i^\top\beta}). The log-likelihood \ell(\beta) of independent observations (x_i, y_i) has score and observed information U(\beta) = \sum_i x_i\,(y_i - p_i), \qquad I(\beta) = -\nabla^2 \ell(\beta) = \sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)\, x_i x_i^\top .

Hence \ell is concave, so any root of U is the maximum likelihood estimator. The Newton iteration \beta \leftarrow \beta + I(\beta)^{-1} U(\beta) finds it in a few steps in practice; step halving guarantees convergence when the estimator exists. Under the usual regularity conditions, \hat\beta is approximately \mathcal{N}(\beta, I(\hat\beta)^{-1}).

Proof. The contribution of observation i is \ell_i = y_i x_i^\top\beta - \log(1 + e^{x_i^\top\beta}). Its gradient is x_i(y_i - p_i).

The gradient of p_i is p_i(1 - p_i)x_i, so the Hessian of \ell is -\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)x_ix_i^\top, a negative semi-definite matrix: \ell is concave.

The asymptotic normality is the general theory of maximum likelihood (van der Vaart, 1998, chapter 5). ∎

The first component of U(\hat\beta) = 0 reads \sum_i (y_i - \hat p_i) = 0: the fitted risks average to the observed proportion. Chapter 6 uses this identity to explain why calibration can never be judged on the development data.

Code 0.9 — Effect measures of Example 0.5, and the same odds ratio by logistic regression

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# 2x2 table: events / patients in each arm (Example 0.5)
a, n1 = 30, 200     # treated: 30 events out of 200
c, n0 = 50, 200     # control: 50 events out of 200
p1, p0 = a / n1, c / n0
z = stats.norm.ppf(0.975)

rd = p1 - p0
se_rd = np.sqrt(p1 * (1 - p1) / n1 + p0 * (1 - p0) / n0)
rr = p1 / p0
se_logrr = np.sqrt(1 / a - 1 / n1 + 1 / c - 1 / n0)
odds = (a * (n0 - c)) / ((n1 - a) * c)
se_logor = np.sqrt(1 / a + 1 / (n1 - a) + 1 / c + 1 / (n0 - c))
print(f"RD {rd:.3f}  [{rd - z*se_rd:.3f}, {rd + z*se_rd:.3f}]")
print(f"RR {rr:.3f}  [{rr*np.exp(-z*se_logrr):.3f}, {rr*np.exp(z*se_logrr):.3f}]")
print(f"OR {odds:.3f}  [{odds*np.exp(-z*se_logor):.3f}, {odds*np.exp(z*se_logor):.3f}]")

# The same odds ratio from a logistic regression fitted by Newton-Raphson
x = np.r_[np.ones(n1), np.zeros(n0)]
y = np.r_[np.ones(a), np.zeros(n1 - a), np.ones(c), np.zeros(n0 - c)]
X = np.c_[np.ones_like(x), x]
beta = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(25):
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ beta))
    score = X.T @ (y - p)
    info = X.T @ (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None])
    beta += np.linalg.solve(info, score)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(np.linalg.inv(info)))
print(f"logistic: OR {np.exp(beta[1]):.3f}, SE(log OR) {se[1]:.4f} vs Woolf {se_logor:.4f}")
# RD -0.100  [-0.178, -0.022]
# RR 0.600  [0.399, 0.902]
# OR 0.529  [0.320, 0.876]
# logistic: OR 0.529, SE(log OR) 0.2567 vs Woolf 0.2567

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • Wald intervals rest on asymptotic normality; ratios get their intervals on the log scale through the delta method.
  • Risk difference, risk ratio and odds ratio answer different questions; the number needed to treat is the inverse of the risk difference.
  • Power is the probability of detecting a given effect; a non-significant test is not a proof of no effect.
  • The logistic log-likelihood is concave; its score equations make the fitted risks average to the observed proportion.

1. Clinical research questions and study designs

A study is designed to answer a question, and the same data can answer some questions and not others. This chapter separates the kinds of questions that clinical research asks, describes the observational and experimental designs that answer them, and proves the property that makes case-control studies work.

1.1 Describe, predict, explain

Definition 1.1 — Descriptive, predictive and causal questions

  • A descriptive question asks for a quantity in a population: the prevalence of diabetes in a country, the median survival after a diagnosis.
  • A predictive question asks for the risk of an outcome for an individual, given the information available at a given time. It is diagnostic when the outcome is present now and prognostic when it occurs later. Any variable that improves the prediction may be used, whatever its causal role.
  • A causal question asks what would happen to the outcome if an intervention were applied, for instance a treatment. The variables used for adjustment are chosen by causal reasoning (ch. 7), not by their predictive value.

The official course description puts the second and third goals at its centre: ch. 6 is about prediction, ch. 7 about causation, and the designs of ch. 1–2 serve both.

Example 1.2 — One cohort, two questions

  • Grey hair predicts death within ten years in a cohort of adults. It belongs in a prognostic model, yet dyeing one’s hair would not change one’s risk.
  • A study of the effect of statins on myocardial infarction must not adjust for the LDL cholesterol measured after the start of treatment. That variable is a consequence of the treatment and carries part of its effect.
  • The same cholesterol value may be an excellent predictor in a model that forecasts infarction, since there the question is not causal.

1.2 Observational designs

Definition 1.3 — Cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies

  • In a cohort study, subjects are classified by exposure and followed forward in time, either prospectively or from historical records; incidences, risk ratios and rates can be estimated.
  • In a case-control study, subjects with the outcome (cases) and without it (controls) are sampled separately, and their past exposure is measured. The design is efficient for rare outcomes, but the risk of the outcome cannot be estimated directly.
  • In a cross-sectional study, exposure and outcome are measured at the same time, which gives prevalences.

Theorem 1.4 — Case-control sampling preserves the odds ratio Let E be a binary exposure and D a binary disease in a source population. Suppose cases are sampled with probability s_1 and non-cases with probability s_0, independently of exposure. Then the exposure odds ratio in the sample equals the disease odds ratio in the population: \frac{P(E \mid D, S)\,/\,P(\bar E \mid D, S)}{P(E \mid \bar D, S)\,/\,P(\bar E \mid \bar D, S)} = \frac{P(D \mid E)\,/\,P(\bar D \mid E)}{P(D \mid \bar E)\,/\,P(\bar D \mid \bar E)} .

Proof. Sampling depends on D only, so P(E \mid D, S) = P(E \mid D), and the same holds for \bar D. The sample exposure odds ratio is therefore the population exposure odds ratio.

By Bayes’ formula, \frac{P(E \mid D)}{P(\bar E \mid D)} = \frac{P(D \mid E)\,P(E)}{P(D \mid \bar E)\,P(\bar E)}, \qquad \frac{P(E \mid \bar D)}{P(\bar E \mid \bar D)} = \frac{P(\bar D \mid E)\,P(E)}{P(\bar D \mid \bar E)\,P(\bar E)} .

In the ratio of these two expressions the prevalence terms cancel, which leaves the disease odds ratio. ∎

The theorem goes back to Cornfield (1951). When the disease is rare, the odds ratio approximates the risk ratio (ch. 0), so a case-control study estimates a risk ratio without ever following a cohort.

Example 1.5 — A simulated case-control study In a population of one million, 10 % are exposed. The risk of disease is 1 % if exposed and 1/3 % if not, so the risk ratio is 3.

Code 1.6 samples all cases and four controls per case. It finds an odds ratio of 2.99 in the full cohort and 2.92 in the case-control sample.

The proportion of cases among exposed subjects of the sample, 0.38, means nothing: it reflects the 1:4 sampling ratio chosen by the investigator, not a risk.

Code 1.6 — Case-control sampling

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

# Source population: exposure E, disease D with risk 1 % if exposed, 1/3 % if not
N = 1_000_000
E = rng.random(N) < 0.10
D = rng.random(N) < np.where(E, 0.010, 0.010 / 3)

def odds_ratio(e, d):
    a, b = np.sum(e & d), np.sum(e & ~d)
    c, dd = np.sum(~e & d), np.sum(~e & ~d)
    return (a * dd) / (b * c)

rr = D[E].mean() / D[~E].mean()
print(f"cohort: RR {rr:.2f}, OR {odds_ratio(E, D):.2f}")

# Case-control study: all cases, 4 controls per case drawn among non-cases
cases = np.flatnonzero(D)
controls = rng.choice(np.flatnonzero(~D), size=4 * cases.size, replace=False)
idx = np.r_[cases, controls]
print(f"case-control ({cases.size} cases): OR {odds_ratio(E[idx], D[idx]):.2f}")
print(f"naive 'risk' among exposed in the case-control sample: {D[idx][E[idx]].mean():.2f}")
# cohort: RR 2.97, OR 2.99
# case-control (4093 cases): OR 2.92
# naive 'risk' among exposed in the case-control sample: 0.38

The theorem assumes that controls represent the source population of the cases and that exposure is measured alike in both groups; ch. 3 shows what happens otherwise.

1.3 Experimental designs and the phases of drug development

In an experimental study the investigator assigns the intervention. A new drug goes through four phases before and after it reaches the market.

Definition 1.7 — Phases of clinical development

  • Phase I: first administration to humans, often healthy volunteers; safety, pharmacokinetics and the choice of dose, on a few dozen subjects.
  • Phase II: preliminary evidence of efficacy and refinement of the dose in patients, in single-arm or small randomized trials.
  • Phase III: confirmatory randomized controlled trial against placebo or the standard of care, with a pre-specified primary endpoint and analysis, usually on hundreds or thousands of patients.
  • Phase IV: surveillance after marketing approval, to detect rare or late adverse effects.

Each design comes with a reporting guideline that lists what a paper must state: CONSORT for randomized trials (Schulz et al., 2010), STROBE for observational studies (von Elm et al., 2007), and TRIPOD for prediction models (ch. 6).

1.4 Framing the question

A clinical question is framed by the PICO scheme: Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome. Regulatory guidance now asks for a more precise target, the estimand, which also says how events such as stopping treatment or taking a rescue medication are handled; section 8.1 presents it.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • Descriptive, predictive and causal questions need different analyses; a good predictor need not be a cause.
  • Cohort studies estimate risks; case-control studies estimate odds ratios, which approximate risk ratios for rare outcomes (Theorem 1.4).
  • Drugs are developed in four phases; phase III trials are randomized and confirmatory.
  • A study starts from a precise question: population, intervention, comparator and outcome.

2. Randomized controlled trials: design and sample size

The randomized controlled trial is the reference design to evaluate a treatment. This chapter explains why randomization works, how it is carried out, and how the number of patients is computed. It then covers non-inferiority, repeated looks at the data, and the choice of the analysis population.

2.1 Why randomize

Definition 2.1 — Potential outcomes For each patient and each treatment a \in \{0, 1\}, let Y(a) be the outcome that would be observed if the patient received a. The observed outcome of a patient who receives A is Y = A\,Y(1) + (1 - A)\,Y(0) \qquad \text{(consistency)}.

The individual effect Y(1) - Y(0) is never observed, since each patient receives one treatment only. The average treatment effect is \mathrm{ATE} = E[Y(1) - Y(0)].

This formalism goes back to Neyman (1923) and Rubin (1974); ch. 7 develops it for observational data.

Theorem 2.2 — Randomization identifies the average effect Let L denote all baseline characteristics of a patient, measured or not. Suppose that the assignment A is independent of (Y(0), Y(1), L) and that 0 < P(A = 1) < 1. Then E[Y \mid A = 1] - E[Y \mid A = 0] = E[Y(1) - Y(0)] .

Proof. By consistency, E[Y \mid A = 1] = E[Y(1) \mid A = 1]. By independence, this equals E[Y(1)].

The same argument gives E[Y \mid A = 0] = E[Y(0)], and the difference is the average treatment effect. ∎

The difference of the sample means is therefore an unbiased estimate of the average effect. The independence holds for unmeasured characteristics too, which no adjustment of an observational study can guarantee (ch. 3 and 7).

2.2 Randomization procedures, concealment and blinding

Definition 2.3 — Randomization procedures

  • Simple randomization assigns each patient by an independent fair coin.
  • Permuted blocks cut the allocation list into blocks of size 2k, each a random permutation of k treated and k control assignments, so that the arms are balanced after every block.
  • Stratified randomization uses a separate block list within each stratum of a strong prognostic factor, such as the centre or the stage of the disease.
  • Minimization (Taves, 1974; Pocock and Simon, 1975) assigns each new patient to the arm that minimizes an imbalance score over several prognostic factors, usually with a random element.

Proposition 2.4 — Imbalance under simple randomization With n patients, the size N_1 of the treated arm is \mathrm{Bin}(n, 1/2). With a continuity correction, P\big(|N_1 - n/2| \ge k\big) \approx 2\Big(1 - \Phi\Big(\frac{2k - 1}{\sqrt n}\Big)\Big).

Proof. By the central limit theorem, N_1 is approximately normal with mean n/2 and variance n/4. The integer event |N_1 - n/2| \ge k is replaced by |N_1 - n/2| \ge k - 1/2, and the standardized threshold is (k - 1/2)/(\sqrt n/2). ∎

With n = 100 and k = 5, the probability of a split at least as unbalanced as 45/55 is 0.368, both exactly and by the formula. Block randomization removes this risk, which matters for small trials and for subgroups.

Definition 2.5 — Allocation concealment and blinding

  • Allocation concealment means that whoever enrols a patient cannot know the next assignment. Without it, investigators may consciously or not steer patients to an arm, which is a selection bias.
  • Blinding (masking) hides the assignment after randomization, from patients, caregivers, outcome assessors or analysts. With a placebo, a trial can be double blind.

When blinding is impossible, as for surgery, the outcome can still be assessed by a blinded committee (the PROBE design: prospective randomized open blinded end-point).

2.3 Endpoints

Definition 2.6 — Endpoints

  • The primary endpoint is the single pre-specified outcome on which the trial is sized and judged.
  • Secondary endpoints support the interpretation, but a positive secondary result cannot rescue a negative primary one.
  • A surrogate endpoint is a biomarker meant to stand in for a clinical outcome, such as blood pressure for stroke.
  • A composite endpoint counts the first of several events, such as death, infarction or stroke.

Surrogates can mislead In the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial (CAST Investigators, 1989), encainide and flecainide suppressed ventricular arrhythmias after myocardial infarction, a surrogate believed to predict sudden death, yet they increased mortality compared with placebo.

2.4 Sample size for a superiority trial

Theorem 2.7 — Sample size of a two-arm trial Two arms of n patients have outcomes with means \mu_1, \mu_0 and variances \sigma_1^2, \sigma_0^2.

The two-sided z-test of equal means at level \alpha has power 1 - \beta at the difference \Delta = \mu_1 - \mu_0 when

Key result — sample size per arm n = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2\,(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2)}{\Delta^2}

For a binary endpoint, \sigma_a^2 = p_a(1 - p_a).

Proof. The statistic is Z = (\bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0)/\mathrm{se} with \mathrm{se} = \sqrt{(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2)/n}. Under the alternative, Z is approximately \mathcal{N}(\Delta/\mathrm{se}, 1); take \Delta > 0.

Neglecting the rejection in the wrong direction, the power is P(Z > z_{1-\alpha/2}) = \Phi\big(\Delta/\mathrm{se} - z_{1-\alpha/2}\big).

Setting it to 1 - \beta gives \Delta/\mathrm{se} = z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}. Squaring and solving for n gives the formula. ∎

Halving the expected effect multiplies the trial by four, and the formula is only as good as the assumed effect and variance.

Example 2.8 — A trial on mortality The control mortality is p_0 = 0.30 and the trial must detect p_1 = 0.20 with \alpha = 0.05 and power 0.90. Then z_{0.975} + z_{0.90} = 1.960 + 1.282 = 3.242 and n = \frac{3.242^2 \times (0.21 + 0.16)}{0.10^2} = 388.8, \quad \text{so } 389 \text{ patients per arm}.

With 10 % expected losses to follow-up, 389/0.9 gives 433 per arm. Code 2.9 checks the power by simulation.

2.5 Non-inferiority and equivalence

A new treatment that is cheaper, safer or easier to take need not be better: it is enough that it is not much worse.

Definition 2.10 — Non-inferiority and equivalence Let larger outcomes be better, and let \delta > 0 be a non-inferiority margin fixed in advance. A non-inferiority trial tests H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_0 \le -\delta \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_1: \mu_1 - \mu_0 > -\delta .

An equivalence trial shows |\mu_1 - \mu_0| < \delta by two one-sided tests.

Proposition 2.11 — Non-inferiority by a confidence interval The one-sided Wald test of non-inferiority at level \alpha/2 rejects H_0 exactly when the lower bound of the two-sided 1 - \alpha interval for \mu_1 - \mu_0 lies above -\delta.

If the true difference is \Delta \ge 0, the sample size is that of Theorem 2.7 with \Delta replaced by \Delta + \delta.

Proof. The test rejects when \frac{\bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0 + \delta}{\mathrm{se}} > z_{1-\alpha/2}, \quad \text{that is, when} \quad \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0 - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\mathrm{se} > -\delta .

Under the alternative the statistic is centred at (\Delta + \delta)/\mathrm{se}, and the power calculation of Theorem 2.7 applies unchanged. ∎

Pitfalls of non-inferiority The margin must be smaller than the effect of the active comparator against placebo, which is known only from historical trials (the constancy assumption). Poor compliance makes the two arms look alike and so favours non-inferiority; the intention-to-treat analysis of section 2.7 is therefore not conservative here, and both it and the per-protocol analysis are reported (Piaggio et al., 2012).

2.6 Multiplicity and interim analyses

Every additional test is another chance of a false positive.

Proposition 2.12 — Bonferroni correction If each of K tests is performed at level \alpha/K, the probability of at least one false rejection (the family-wise error rate) is at most \alpha, whatever the dependence between the tests.

Proof. Let R_k be the event that true null hypothesis k is rejected. By the union bound, P\Big(\bigcup_k R_k\Big) \le \sum_k P(R_k) \le K \cdot \frac{\alpha}{K} = \alpha .

∎

Holm’s step-down procedure (Holm, 1979) controls the same error rate and is uniformly more powerful.

Repeated looks at accumulating data are a form of multiplicity: trials are monitored for safety and efficacy, and stopping at the first p < 0.05 inflates the type I error. Section 8.3 computes this inflation and the group-sequential boundaries that control it.

2.7 Analysis populations and non-compliance

Definition 2.13 — Intention to treat, per protocol

  • The intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis includes every randomized patient, in the arm to which the patient was randomized, whatever the treatment actually received.
  • The per-protocol analysis keeps only the patients who followed the protocol.
  • The as-treated analysis groups patients by the treatment actually received.

Only the ITT comparison inherits Theorem 2.2. The other two compare groups selected after randomization, and adherence is itself prognostic. In the Coronary Drug Project (1980), patients who took at least 80 % of their placebo had a much lower five-year mortality than those who did not.

Under non-compliance the ITT effect is diluted towards zero (section 8.2): it estimates the effect of assigning a treatment, while a valid per-protocol comparison needs the causal methods of ch. 7.

Two common variants of the parallel-group design, cluster and crossover trials, are treated in section 8.4.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Randomization makes treatment independent of all baseline factors, measured or not, so the difference of means estimates the average effect (Theorem 2.2).
  • Blocks and stratification keep arms balanced; concealment and blinding protect the comparison after randomization.
  • The sample size per arm (Theorem 2.7) grows like 1/\Delta^2: halving the effect multiplies it by four.
  • Non-inferiority is shown by a confidence interval above -\delta; repeated looks need group-sequential boundaries.
  • Intention to treat preserves randomization; per-protocol and as-treated analyses compare groups selected after randomization.

3. Therapeutic evaluation and biases in clinical research

An estimate can be precise and wrong. This chapter states what a treatment effect should be measured against, defines bias, reviews its main sources, and studies in detail the two named in the official syllabus: confounding bias and time-dependent bias.

3.1 Principles of therapeutic evaluation

A treatment is evaluated for efficacy, its effect under the controlled conditions of an explanatory trial, and for effectiveness, its effect in routine care, which a pragmatic trial measures with broad eligibility and usual practice (Schwartz and Lellouch, 1967).

A fair evaluation rests on the principles of ch. 2:

  • a concurrent control group, since historical controls differ in case mix and care;
  • randomized, concealed allocation and, when possible, blinded assessment;
  • one pre-specified, clinically relevant primary endpoint;
  • the analysis of all randomized patients (intention to treat, section 2.7).

Every decision then weighs benefit against harm, on the absolute scale.

Example 3.1 — A constant relative effect, a varying absolute benefit Suppose a treatment reduces the risk of stroke by a quarter in every patient, so \mathrm{RR} = 0.75. A patient with baseline risk p_0 gains \mathrm{RD} = p_0(1 - \mathrm{RR}), \qquad \mathrm{NNT} = \frac{1}{p_0(1 - \mathrm{RR})} .

  • With p_0 = 0.20, the NNT is 20.
  • With p_0 = 0.02, the NNT is 200.

Suppose the treatment also causes a major bleed in 1 % of patients, whatever their risk. The number needed to harm is then \mathrm{NNH} = 1/0.01 = 100. Per 100 patients treated:

  • at p_0 = 0.20, 5 strokes are prevented for 1 bleed;
  • at p_0 = 0.02, 0.5 stroke is prevented for 1 bleed.

The same relative effect can justify treating high-risk patients and not low-risk ones.

Effects reported in subgroups are fragile; section 8.5 shows how to test them.

3.2 Bias and its main sources

Definition 3.2 — Bias The bias of an estimator \hat\theta of an estimand \theta is E[\hat\theta] - \theta, or the difference between the limit of \hat\theta and \theta when the sample size grows (asymptotic bias). Random error shrinks as the sample grows; bias does not.

Epidemiology classifies biases in three families:

  • selection bias, from the way subjects enter or remain in the analysis;
  • information bias, from errors in measuring exposure, outcome or covariates;
  • confounding, from common causes of exposure and outcome (section 3.3).

Selection bias arises when hospital controls are used in a case-control study, when patients lost to follow-up have a different prognosis, or when only “healthy users” persist with a preventive drug.

Proposition 3.3 — Non-differential misclassification of the outcome Suppose a binary outcome is recorded with the same sensitivity \mathrm{Se} and specificity \mathrm{Sp} in both arms. The recorded risk is p^* = \mathrm{Se}\, p + (1 - \mathrm{Sp})(1 - p) in each arm, and \mathrm{RD}^* = (\mathrm{Se} + \mathrm{Sp} - 1)\, \mathrm{RD}.

Proof. The map p \mapsto \mathrm{Se}\,p + (1-\mathrm{Sp})(1-p) is affine with slope \mathrm{Se} + \mathrm{Sp} - 1, and a difference of images of an affine map is the slope times the difference. ∎

The observed difference is shrunk towards zero: with \mathrm{Se} = 0.8 and \mathrm{Sp} = 0.9, it keeps 70 % of its size. Differential errors can bias in either direction, as when cases recall past exposures better than controls (recall bias).

A third source of error mimics a treatment effect when patients are selected on an extreme measurement.

Proposition 3.4 — Regression to the mean Let a baseline measurement X_1 and a follow-up measurement X_2 be jointly normal, with the same mean \mu, the same variance and correlation \rho. Then E[X_2 \mid X_1 = x] = \mu + \rho\,(x - \mu).

Proof. For a bivariate normal pair, E[X_2 \mid X_1 = x] = \mu + \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X_1, X_2)}{\mathrm{Var}(X_1)}\,(x - \mu), and the ratio equals \rho when the variances are equal. ∎

Patients enrolled because their systolic pressure is 170 mmHg, in a population with mean 140 and test–retest correlation 0.7, will have an expected pressure of 161 at the next visit, without any treatment. Only a concurrent, randomized control group separates this drop from the effect of a drug.

3.3 Confounding

Definition 3.5 — Confounding and confounder There is confounding when the crude association between an exposure A and an outcome Y differs from the causal effect of A on Y because of common causes. A confounder L is, informally, a variable that

  1. is associated with A;
  2. is a cause of Y, or a proxy for a cause, among the unexposed;
  3. is not affected by A.

In therapeutic studies the most common form is confounding by indication: physicians treat the patients who need it most, who also have the worst prognosis.

Example 3.6 — Simpson’s paradox: kidney stones Charig et al. (1986) compared open surgery (A) with percutaneous nephrolithotomy (B). The success rates were:

Stone size Open surgery (A) Percutaneous (B)
Small 81/87 (93 %) 234/270 (87 %)
Large 192/263 (73 %) 55/80 (69 %)
All 273/350 (78 %) 289/350 (83 %)

Surgery A does better for small stones and for large stones, yet worse overall. Large stones are both harder to treat and more often treated by A: stone size confounds the crude comparison.

The first remedy is to compare like with like within strata of the confounder, then to pool the stratum-specific comparisons.

Definition 3.7 — Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio In stratum k, let a_k, b_k be the events and non-events among the exposed, c_k, d_k among the unexposed, and n_k the stratum size. The Mantel–Haenszel (1959) estimator of a common odds ratio is \widehat{\mathrm{OR}}_{\mathrm{MH}} = \frac{\sum_k a_k d_k / n_k}{\sum_k b_k c_k / n_k} .

Proposition 3.8 — Consistency of the Mantel–Haenszel estimator If the odds ratio \psi is the same in every stratum, \widehat{\mathrm{OR}}_{\mathrm{MH}} converges to \psi when the stratum sizes grow with a fixed number of strata.

Proof. In stratum k, with n_{1k} exposed and n_{0k} unexposed subjects and risks p_{1k}, p_{0k}, the law of large numbers gives \frac{a_k d_k}{n_k} \approx \frac{n_{1k}n_{0k}}{n_k}\, p_{1k}(1 - p_{0k}), \qquad \frac{b_k c_k}{n_k} \approx \frac{n_{1k}n_{0k}}{n_k}\, (1 - p_{1k})\,p_{0k} .

A common odds ratio means p_{1k}(1 - p_{0k}) = \psi\,(1 - p_{1k})\,p_{0k} in every stratum. Each term of the numerator is thus \psi times the matching term of the denominator, and the ratio of the sums tends to \psi. ∎

The estimator remains consistent when the number of strata grows with fixed small strata, such as matched pairs, where the unconditional maximum likelihood estimator is inconsistent (Breslow, 1981). On the kidney-stone data, Code 3.12 gives stratum odds ratios of 2.08 and 1.23, a Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio of 1.45 in favour of A, and a crude odds ratio of 0.75.

Regression, standardization and propensity scores (ch. 7) also adjust for confounders, but only for measured ones: residual confounding remains the main weakness of observational studies.

3.4 Time-dependent bias: immortal time

Many exposures start during follow-up, such as a drug prescribed after discharge. Classifying subjects by their exposure status at the end of follow-up biases the comparison in favour of the exposure.

Definition 3.9 — Immortal time Immortal time is a period of follow-up during which, by construction of the analysis, the outcome cannot occur in the group to which the time is attributed. The typical case is the time between cohort entry and the start of treatment, counted as treated time for subjects classified as “ever treated” because they eventually start the treatment (Suissa, 2008).

Proposition 3.10 — Size of the immortal-time bias Let the time to death be T \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda) and the time to start of treatment be W \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\mu), independent, so that the treatment has no effect. Classify subjects as ever treated if W < T and never treated otherwise, and compute each group’s death rate as deaths divided by person-time from entry. Then the rate ratio converges to \frac{\text{rate}_{\text{ever}}}{\text{rate}_{\text{never}}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\lambda + \mu} < \frac12 .

Proof. Without censoring, every subject eventually dies.

  • Never treated. Deaths occur with probability P(T < W) = \lambda/(\lambda + \mu), and the person-time is E[T\,\mathbb{1}\{T < W\}] = \int_0^\infty t\,\lambda e^{-\lambda t} e^{-\mu t}\,dt = \frac{\lambda}{(\lambda + \mu)^2}, so the rate is \lambda + \mu.
  • Ever treated. Deaths occur with probability \mu/(\lambda + \mu), and the person-time is E[T] - \frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+\mu)^2} = \frac{1}{\lambda} - \frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+\mu)^2} = \frac{\mu\,(2\lambda + \mu)}{\lambda\,(\lambda + \mu)^2}, so the rate is \lambda(\lambda + \mu)/(2\lambda + \mu).

The empirical rates converge to these ratios of expectations by the law of large numbers, and dividing the two rates gives the result. ∎

When the waiting time to treatment has the same mean as the survival time (\mu = \lambda), a useless drug appears to divide mortality by three. The never-treated rate is inflated too, because those subjects are selected for dying early.

Proposition 3.11 — The correct person-time split In the model of Proposition 3.10, attribute each subject’s time before W to the untreated state and the time after W to the treated state. Then both death rates converge to \lambda, and the rate ratio to 1.

Proof. Untreated state. Each subject spends \min(T, W) \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda + \mu) there, with mean 1/(\lambda+\mu), and dies there with probability \lambda/(\lambda + \mu): the rate is \lambda.

Treated state. A subject enters it with probability \mu/(\lambda + \mu). By the memoryless property, the remaining time T - W given T > W is \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda), with mean 1/\lambda, and it ends in death. The rate is \frac{\mu/(\lambda+\mu)}{\mu/\big(\lambda(\lambda+\mu)\big)} = \lambda .

∎

The person-time split becomes a time-dependent covariate in the Cox model of ch. 5 (Mantel and Byar, 1974); landmark analyses and target trial emulation (ch. 7) are the other remedies. Lévesque et al. (2010) show the bias at work in a cohort of statin users.

Other time-related biases

  • Time-window bias: exposure measured over windows of different lengths for cases and controls.
  • Lead-time bias: earlier diagnosis by screening lengthens survival from diagnosis even when death is not delayed.
  • Length-time bias: screening preferentially detects slowly progressing tumours.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • A fair evaluation needs a concurrent randomized control, a pre-specified endpoint and intention to treat; a constant relative effect gives absolute benefits proportional to the baseline risk, to be weighed against absolute harms.
  • Bias does not shrink with the sample size; its sources are selection, information and confounding.
  • Non-differential outcome misclassification shrinks the risk difference by \mathrm{Se} + \mathrm{Sp} - 1; regression to the mean needs a concurrent control group.
  • Confounding can reverse an association (Simpson’s paradox); stratification and the Mantel–Haenszel estimator remove confounding by measured factors.
  • Classifying subjects by exposure ever received creates immortal time: a useless treatment can look protective by a factor of 2 or more; person-time must be split at the start of treatment.

4. Censored data I: survival functions, Kaplan–Meier and the log-rank test

In many clinical studies the outcome is a time: time to death, to relapse, to discharge from hospital. For many patients follow-up ends before the event, and their time is only known to exceed the follow-up: it is censored. This chapter defines the objects that describe a time to event and estimates them without any parametric model.

4.1 Survival function and hazard

Definition 4.1 — Survival function, hazard, cumulative hazard For a non-negative random time T with density f: S(t) = P(T > t), \qquad h(t) = \lim_{dt \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t + dt \mid T \ge t)}{dt} = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)}, \qquad H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\,du .

The hazard h(t) is the instantaneous event rate among the subjects still event-free at t.

Proposition 4.2 — From hazard to survival In continuous time, S(t) = \exp\big(-H(t)\big).

In discrete time, with h_u = P(T = u \mid T \ge u), the survival is S(t) = \prod_{u \le t} (1 - h_u).

Proof. Since f = -S', the hazard is h = -S'/S = -(\log S)'. Integrating from 0 with S(0) = 1 gives \log S(t) = -H(t).

In discrete time, surviving beyond t means surviving each time u \le t given survival up to u; the chain rule of conditional probabilities gives the product. ∎

Example 4.3 — Exponential and Weibull laws

  • A constant hazard h(t) = \lambda gives S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}, with mean 1/\lambda and median \log 2/\lambda.
  • The Weibull hazard h(t) = \lambda\gamma t^{\gamma - 1} gives S(t) = \exp(-\lambda t^\gamma). It increases with time when \gamma > 1 (ageing) and decreases when \gamma < 1 (the risk just after surgery).

4.2 Censoring

Definition 4.4 — Right censoring Let C be the time at which follow-up stops. One observes X = \min(T, C), \qquad \delta = \mathbb{1}\{T \le C\}.

Censoring is independent (or non-informative) when T and C are independent, possibly given covariates.

Censoring comes from the end of the study (administrative censoring), from losses to follow-up and from withdrawals. Independence is plausible for the first and doubtful when patients leave because they feel worse.

A related phenomenon is left truncation: subjects enter observation at a time V > 0, for instance at the date of referral, and are seen only if T > V. Risk sets then include a subject only after its entry time, as in the (\text{start}, \text{stop}] format of ch. 5.

Example 4.5 — Why naive summaries fail Ten patients are followed for at most 15 months (Example 4.9 below). Five die and five are censored.

  • The proportion of deaths, 5/10, underestimates the risk, since censored patients could still die; excluding them overestimates it.
  • The mean of the observed times underestimates the mean survival.

Both summaries depend on the follow-up duration, a property of the study, not of the disease.

4.3 Likelihood under right censoring

Proposition 4.6 — Censored-data likelihood Under independent censoring whose distribution does not involve the parameters of T, the likelihood of (x_i, \delta_i)_{i \le n} is proportional to \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i)^{\delta_i}\, S(x_i)^{1 - \delta_i} = \prod_{i=1}^n h(x_i)^{\delta_i}\, \exp\big(-H(x_i)\big).

Proof. An event observed at x has probability P(T \in dx, C > x) = f(x)\,dx\; P(C > x). A censoring at x has probability P(T > x)\, P(C \in dx).

The factors involving C do not depend on the parameters of T and drop out. The second form uses f = hS and S = e^{-H}. ∎

For the exponential law, the log-likelihood is D\log\lambda - \lambda \sum_i x_i with D = \sum_i \delta_i the number of events, so that

\hat\lambda = \frac{D}{\sum_i x_i} = \frac{\text{number of events}}{\text{person-time}} .

The observed information is D/\hat\lambda^2, so \mathrm{se}(\log \hat\lambda) = 1/\sqrt{D}: the precision depends on the number of events, not on the number of patients. For the ten patients of Example 4.9, D = 5 over 78 months of follow-up gives \hat\lambda = 0.064 per month and an estimated median survival of \log 2/\hat\lambda = 10.8 months.

4.4 The Kaplan–Meier estimator

Definition 4.7 — Kaplan–Meier estimator Let t_1 < \dots < t_K be the distinct event times, d_j the number of events at t_j, and n_j the number of subjects at risk just before t_j, that is, with X_i \ge t_j. The Kaplan–Meier (1958) estimator is

Key result — Kaplan–Meier estimator \hat S(t) = \prod_{j:\ t_j \le t} \Big(1 - \frac{d_j}{n_j}\Big)

The estimator applies Proposition 4.2 in discrete time: each conditional probability of surviving an event time is estimated by the proportion of survivors among those at risk. Censored subjects count in the risk sets until they leave, and independent censoring makes those at risk representative. The curve drops only at event times; it is the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator.

Theorem 4.8 — Greenwood’s formula The variance of the Kaplan–Meier estimator is estimated by \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\big(\hat S(t)\big) = \hat S(t)^2 \sum_{j:\ t_j \le t} \frac{d_j}{n_j(n_j - d_j)} .

Proof. Sketch. Write \log \hat S(t) = \sum_j \log(1 - \hat h_j) with \hat h_j = d_j/n_j. Given the risk set, d_j is approximately binomial (n_j, h_j), and the terms are uncorrelated because each is centred given the past (a martingale structure).

By the delta method, \mathrm{Var}\big(\log(1 - \hat h_j)\big) \approx \frac{h_j}{n_j(1 - h_j)}, \quad \text{estimated by} \quad \frac{d_j}{n_j(n_j - d_j)} .

Summing gives the variance of \log\hat S(t), and a second delta method, with g = \exp, multiplies it by \hat S(t)^2. A rigorous proof uses counting processes (Andersen et al., 1993, ch. IV). ∎

Intervals computed on the \log(-\log) scale stay within [0, 1] and have better coverage than the plain Wald interval.

Definition 4.10 — Nelson–Aalen estimator The cumulative hazard is estimated by \hat H(t) = \sum_{j:\ t_j \le t} \frac{d_j}{n_j} .

Since 1 - x \le e^{-x}, the survival estimate e^{-\hat H(t)} is slightly above \hat S(t).

4.5 Comparing two groups: the log-rank test

Theorem 4.11 — Log-rank test At each event time t_j, let n_j subjects be at risk, n_{1j} of them in group 1, and let d_j events occur, d_{1j} of them in group 1.

Under H_0: S_1 = S_0, and conditionally on these margins, d_{1j} is hypergeometric with E_{1j} = d_j\,\frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}, \qquad V_j = d_j\,\frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}\Big(1 - \frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}\Big)\frac{n_j - d_j}{n_j - 1} .

With O_1 = \sum_j d_{1j}, E_1 = \sum_j E_{1j} and V = \sum_j V_j, the statistic \frac{(O_1 - E_1)^2}{V} is approximately \chi^2_1 under H_0.

Proof. Sketch. Under H_0 every subject at risk at t_j has the same hazard. Given that d_j of the n_j subjects fail, every subset of d_j subjects is equally likely to be the one that fails, so d_{1j} is hypergeometric with the stated mean and variance.

The centred terms d_{1j} - E_{1j} have mean zero given the past of the study: they form a martingale difference sequence. The martingale central limit theorem then makes (O_1 - E_1)/\sqrt V asymptotically standard normal (Fleming and Harrington, 1991; Andersen et al., 1993). ∎

The test is due to Mantel (1966) and Peto and Peto (1972). It is the locally most powerful rank test against proportional-hazards alternatives (ch. 5) and loses power when the survival curves cross. Weighted versions put more weight on early differences (Gehan–Breslow) or, with suitable parameters, late ones (the Fleming–Harrington family), and the stratified log-rank test sums O - E and V over strata of a prognostic factor.

When hazards are not proportional, a single hazard ratio summarizes a difference poorly; the restricted mean survival time of section 8.6, the area under the survival curve up to a horizon, is then preferred.

Code 4.12 — Kaplan–Meier, Greenwood and log-rank from scratch

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)

def kaplan_meier(time, event):
    """Event times, KM survival and Greenwood standard error."""
    t = np.unique(time[event == 1])
    n = np.array([(time >= s).sum() for s in t])           # at risk just before s
    d = np.array([((time == s) & (event == 1)).sum() for s in t])
    S = np.cumprod(1 - d / n)
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
        gw = np.cumsum(d / (n * (n - d)))
    return t, S, S * np.sqrt(gw)

def logrank(time, event, group):
    O = E = V = 0.0
    for s in np.unique(time[event == 1]):
        at_risk = time >= s
        n, n1 = at_risk.sum(), (at_risk & (group == 1)).sum()
        d = ((time == s) & (event == 1)).sum()
        d1 = ((time == s) & (event == 1) & (group == 1)).sum()
        O += d1
        E += d * n1 / n
        if n > 1:
            V += d * (n1 / n) * (1 - n1 / n) * (n - d) / (n - 1)
    chi2 = (O - E) ** 2 / V
    return O, E, chi2, stats.chi2.sf(chi2, 1)

time = np.array([3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 15])
event = np.array([1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0])
for s, S, se in zip(*kaplan_meier(time, event)):
    print(f"t={s:2d}  S={S:.3f}  se={se:.3f}")

k = np.searchsorted(t, 12, side="right") - 1
print(f"control S(12) = {S[k]:.3f} +/- {1.96*se[k]:.3f}; truth {np.exp(-0.10*12):.3f}")
O, E, chi2, p = logrank(X, delta, group)
print(f"log-rank: O={O:.0f}, E={E:.1f}, chi2={chi2:.2f}, p={p:.1e}")

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • A time to event is described by its survival function or its hazard, linked by S = e^{-H}.
  • Censored observations contribute S(x) to the likelihood; with a constant hazard the estimate is events divided by person-time.
  • The Kaplan–Meier estimator multiplies the conditional survival proportions at the event times; Greenwood’s formula gives its variance.
  • The log-rank test sums observed minus expected events over the risk sets; it is optimal under proportional hazards.

5. Censored data II: the Cox model and time-dependent covariates

The log-rank test compares groups; clinical studies need to estimate an effect while accounting for several covariates, some of which change over time. The Cox model does this without specifying the baseline hazard. This chapter derives its partial likelihood, shows how to check its main assumption, and uses it to remove the immortal-time bias of ch. 3.

5.1 Proportional hazards

Definition 5.1 — Cox proportional hazards model The hazard of a subject with covariates x \in \mathbb{R}^p is h(t \mid x) = h_0(t)\, \exp(x^\top \beta), where the baseline hazard h_0 is left unspecified. Then S(t \mid x) = S_0(t)^{\exp(x^\top\beta)}.

The ratio of the hazards of two subjects does not depend on time: this is the proportional hazards assumption. For a binary treatment indicator x_j, e^{\beta_j} is the hazard ratio of treated versus untreated patients with the same other covariates.

5.2 The partial likelihood

Let R(t) = \{j : X_j \ge t\} be the risk set at t, and assume first that event times are distinct.

Theorem 5.2 — Partial likelihood (Cox, 1972, 1975) The parameter \beta is estimated by maximizing

Key result — Cox partial likelihood L(\beta) = \prod_{i:\ \delta_i = 1} \frac{\exp(x_i^\top\beta)}{\sum_{j \in R(x_i)} \exp(x_j^\top\beta)}

Write w_j = e^{x_j^\top\beta}, and let \bar x(\beta, t) and V(\beta, t) be the mean and covariance of the covariates over R(t) with weights proportional to w_j. The score and information are U(\beta) = \sum_{i:\ \delta_i = 1} \big(x_i - \bar x(\beta, x_i)\big), \qquad I(\beta) = \sum_{i:\ \delta_i = 1} V(\beta, x_i).

The log partial likelihood is concave, and \hat\beta is approximately \mathcal{N}(\beta, I(\hat\beta)^{-1}).

Proof. Given that exactly one event occurs at time t among the subjects of R(t), the probability that it is subject i is \frac{h_0(t)\,e^{x_i^\top\beta}\,dt}{\sum_{j \in R(t)} h_0(t)\,e^{x_j^\top\beta}\,dt} = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j \in R(t)} w_j} : the baseline hazard cancels. The product of these probabilities over the event times is L(\beta).

The log of one factor is x_i^\top\beta - \log \sum_j w_j. Its gradient is x_i - \bar x(\beta, t), and its Hessian is -V(\beta, t), a covariance matrix and hence positive semi-definite: the log partial likelihood is concave.

The asymptotic normality follows from counting-process martingale theory (Andersen and Gill, 1982). ∎

With tied event times, Breslow’s approximation lets every tied event use the full risk set, which is what Code 5.7 does; Efron’s approximation is more accurate when ties are many.

Proposition 5.3 — The score test is the log-rank test For a single binary covariate (the group) and distinct event times, the score test of \beta = 0 in the Cox model is the log-rank test of Theorem 4.11.

Proof. At \beta = 0 all weights equal 1, so \bar x(0, t_j) = n_{1j}/n_j and U(0) = \sum_j (d_{1j} - n_{1j}/n_j) = O_1 - E_1.

The variance of a binary covariate over the risk set is V(0, t_j) = \frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}\Big(1 - \frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}\Big), which is V_j when d_j = 1. The score statistic U(0)^2/I(0) is therefore the log-rank statistic. ∎

Definition 5.4 — Breslow estimator and predicted survival The baseline cumulative hazard is estimated by \hat H_0(t) = \sum_{j:\ t_j \le t} \frac{d_j}{\sum_{l \in R(t_j)} \exp(x_l^\top \hat\beta)} , and the predicted survival of a new subject is \hat S(t \mid x) = \exp\big(-\hat H_0(t)\, e^{x^\top\hat\beta}\big).

With \hat\beta = 0 the Breslow estimator is the Nelson–Aalen estimator. Predicted survival curves are what a prognostic model reports, and section 8.9 shows how they are validated.

5.3 Checking proportional hazards

  • Graphically, curves of \log(-\log \hat S_g(t)) against \log t should be parallel across the groups g.
  • With residuals, the Schoenfeld residual of an event i is r_i = x_i - \bar x(\hat\beta, x_i). If the true coefficient varies with time, the residuals scaled by D\, I(\hat\beta)^{-1}, with D the number of events, have a mean close to \beta(t) - \hat\beta; Grambsch and Therneau (1994) turn this into a test.
  • Remedies include stratifying the baseline hazard on the offending variable, letting the coefficient change over time through an interaction with a function of t, and reporting restricted mean survival times (section 8.6).

The hazards of hazard ratios Even in a randomized trial, the hazard ratio at a late time compares the survivors of each arm, who are no longer exchangeable once the treatment has selected them. Hernán (2010) recommends reporting adjusted survival curves or their differences rather than period-specific hazard ratios.

5.4 Time-dependent covariates

Definition 5.5 — Cox model with time-dependent covariates The hazard may depend on the current value of a covariate process x(t) known at time t: h\big(t \mid x(t)\big) = h_0(t)\, \exp\big(x(t)^\top\beta\big).

In the counting-process format, each subject is split into intervals (\text{start}, \text{stop}] on which the covariates are constant, and the risk set at t contains the intervals with \text{start} < t \le \text{stop}.

The same format handles left truncation, since a subject enters the risk sets only after its first start time.

Example 5.6 — Immortal time, analysed correctly Return to the useless treatment of Proposition 3.10 with \lambda = \mu = 0.1. Code 5.7 fits two Cox models on 4000 simulated subjects:

  • with treatment coded at baseline as “ever treated”, the hazard ratio is 0.28;
  • with x(t) = \mathbb{1}\{W < t\}, each treated subject contributing an untreated interval (0, W] and a treated interval (W, T], the hazard ratio is 0.93, with 95 % interval [0.86, 1.01].

The second analysis is the person-time split of Proposition 3.11 inside the Cox model; its interval covers 1, the absence of effect.

A landmark analysis (Anderson, Cain and Gelber, 1983) is the simple alternative: choose a time s, classify subjects by their treatment status at s, and analyse survival from s among those still alive.

Internal covariates A time-dependent covariate that is itself affected by earlier treatment, such as a biomarker measured during follow-up, can be used for prediction. It cannot simply be adjusted for when estimating the total effect of treatment, because it lies on the causal path; this needs the g-methods introduced in ch. 7.

Code 5.7 — The Cox model from scratch, with time-dependent covariates

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

def cox(start, stop, event, X, iters=30):
    """Cox partial likelihood (Breslow ties), counting-process data (start, stop]."""
    X = np.atleast_2d(X.T).T
    beta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    times = np.unique(stop[event == 1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        U, I = np.zeros_like(beta), np.zeros((beta.size, beta.size))
        for t in times:
            R = (start < t) & (stop >= t)                    # risk set at t
            D = (stop == t) & (event == 1)                   # failures at t
            w = np.exp(X[R] @ beta)
            xbar = w @ X[R] / w.sum()
            cov = (X[R] * w[:, None]).T @ X[R] / w.sum() - np.outer(xbar, xbar)
            U += X[D].sum(0) - D.sum() * xbar
            I += D.sum() * cov
        step = np.linalg.solve(I, U)
        beta += step
        if np.abs(step).max() < 1e-9:
            break
    return beta, np.sqrt(np.diag(np.linalg.inv(I)))

# (a) Recovering known log hazard ratios: h(t|x) = 0.1 exp(0.7 x1 - 0.5 x2)
n = 1000
X = np.c_[rng.integers(0, 2, n), rng.standard_normal(n)]
T = rng.exponential(1 / (0.1 * np.exp(X @ np.array([0.7, -0.5]))))
C = rng.uniform(0, 15, n)
b, se = cox(np.zeros(n), np.minimum(T, C), (T <= C).astype(int), X)
print("beta", b.round(3), "se", se.round(3))

# (b) Immortal time (ch. 3): useless treatment started at W ~ Exp(0.1), death T ~ Exp(0.1)
n = 4000
T, W = rng.exponential(10, n), rng.exponential(10, n)
ever = (W < T).astype(float)
b_naive, _ = cox(np.zeros(n), T, np.ones(n, int), ever)
# split treated subjects into (0, W] untreated and (W, T] treated
s = np.r_[np.zeros(n), W[W < T]]
e = np.r_[np.minimum(W, T), T[W < T]]
d = np.r_[(T <= W).astype(int), np.ones(int(ever.sum()), int)]
z = np.r_[np.zeros(n), np.ones(int(ever.sum()))]
b_td, se_td = cox(s, e, d, z)
lo, hi = np.exp(b_td[0] + np.array([-1.96, 1.96]) * se_td[0])
print(f"HR naive {np.exp(b_naive[0]):.2f}; time-dependent {np.exp(b_td[0]):.2f} [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}]")
# beta [ 0.714 -0.523] se [0.086 0.045]
# HR naive 0.28; time-dependent 0.93 [0.86, 1.01]

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • The Cox model multiplies an unspecified baseline hazard by e^{x^\top\beta}; e^{\beta_j} is a hazard ratio.
  • The partial likelihood is a product over events of the probability that the failing subject is the one observed; it is concave, and its score test for one binary covariate is the log-rank test.
  • Proportional hazards are checked with log–log plots and Schoenfeld residuals; when they fail, stratify, model \beta(t) or report restricted mean survival times.
  • Exposures that start during follow-up must enter as time-dependent covariates or through a landmark: the ever-treated coding manufactures a protective effect.

6. Validating predictive models in health science

A prediction model is useful only if its predictions are accurate for new patients. This chapter defines what “accurate” means for a risk model (discrimination, calibration, overall error and clinical usefulness), explains why performance measured on the development data is optimistic, and shows how to correct it.

6.1 Prediction models and their life cycle

Definition 6.1 — Clinical prediction model A clinical prediction model is a function \hat p(x) that gives the probability of an outcome from predictors x available at the moment of prediction. The outcome is present now for a diagnostic model and occurs within a horizon \tau for a prognostic one.

A model goes through development, internal validation on the development population, external validation on patients from another time or place, and ideally an impact study. TRIPOD (Collins et al., 2015) and TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al., 2024) list what a report must contain. Performance has four aspects (Steyerberg et al., 2010), taken in turn below.

6.2 Discrimination

Definition 6.2 — ROC curve and AUC For a threshold c, the sensitivity is \mathrm{Se}(c) = P(\hat p \ge c \mid Y = 1) and the specificity is \mathrm{Sp}(c) = P(\hat p < c \mid Y = 0). The ROC curve traces (1 - \mathrm{Sp}(c), \mathrm{Se}(c)) as c varies, and the AUC is the area under it.

Theorem 6.3 — The AUC is a probability of correct ranking Let S_1 be the score of a random patient with the outcome (a case) and S_0 that of an independent random patient without it (a control). Then \mathrm{AUC} = P(S_1 > S_0) + \tfrac12\, P(S_1 = S_0).

The empirical AUC is the Mann–Whitney statistic divided by the number of case–control pairs.

Proof. For continuous scores, parametrize the curve by the false-positive rate u = P(S_0 \ge c). Then \mathrm{AUC} = \int_0^1 \mathrm{Se}\big(c(u)\big)\,du = \int P(S_1 \ge c)\, dF_{S_0}(c) = P(S_1 \ge S_0), where the last step conditions on S_0 = c and uses independence. With ties, the curve has diagonal segments, and the trapezoids contribute half of P(S_1 = S_0) (Hanley and McNeil, 1982). ∎

The AUC depends only on the ranks of the predictions, so it is unchanged by any increasing transformation of \hat p: a model can rank patients exactly as the true risk does, with the same AUC, and still predict odds that are all ten times too high.

Example 6.4 — AUC by hand Three cases have scores 0.8, 0.6, 0.4 and four controls have 0.7, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1. Of the 12 case–control pairs, the case scores higher in 4 + 3 + 3 = 10, so \mathrm{AUC} = 10/12 = 0.833 .

For time-to-event outcomes, Harrell’s C (Harrell et al., 1982) is the proportion of usable pairs, those whose shorter time is an event, in which the subject who fails first has the higher predicted risk. It depends on censoring, which Uno et al. (2011) correct by inverse probability of censoring weights.

6.3 Calibration

Definition 6.5 — Calibration A model is (strongly) calibrated if P\big(Y = 1 \mid \hat p(X) = p\big) = p \quad \text{for all } p .

Two weaker summaries are used in practice. Calibration-in-the-large compares the mean predicted risk with the observed proportion. The calibration slope is the coefficient b of the recalibration model \mathrm{logit}\, P(Y = 1) = a + b\, \mathrm{logit}\, \hat p(X).

A slope below 1 means that the predictions are too extreme, the signature of overfitting.

Proposition 6.6 — Apparent calibration-in-the-large and slope are always perfect For a logistic model with an intercept, fitted by maximum likelihood, the apparent calibration-in-the-large is exact and the apparent calibration slope is 1.

Proof. The score equations give \sum_i x_i(y_i - \hat p_i) = 0. The intercept component reads \sum_i (y_i - \hat p_i) = 0: the mean prediction equals the observed proportion.

Refit (a, b) on the linear predictor \hat\eta_i = x_i^\top\hat\beta. At (a, b) = (0, 1) the fitted risks are \hat p_i, and the two score equations of the recalibration model hold: \sum_i (y_i - \hat p_i) = 0, \qquad \sum_i \hat\eta_i\,(y_i - \hat p_i) = \hat\beta^\top \sum_i x_i\,(y_i - \hat p_i) = 0 .

The log-likelihood is concave (Proposition 0.8), so (0, 1) is its maximizer. ∎

Calibration can therefore only be assessed on new data or by resampling. A calibration plot shows the observed proportion against the predicted risk, by groups or with a smoother; Van Calster et al. (2019) call calibration “the Achilles heel of predictive analytics”.

6.4 Overall performance: the Brier score

Definition 6.7 — Brier score The Brier (1950) score is the mean squared error of the predicted probabilities, \mathrm{BS} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat p_i - y_i)^2 .

Proposition 6.8 — Decomposition of the Brier score Let \pi(x) = P(Y = 1 \mid X = x) be the true risk. Then E\big[(\hat p(X) - Y)^2\big] = E\big[(\hat p(X) - \pi(X))^2\big] + E\big[\pi(X)\,(1 - \pi(X))\big].

Hence the expected Brier score is minimized by the true risk and by no other prediction: it is a strictly proper scoring rule.

Proof. Write \hat p - Y = (\hat p - \pi) + (\pi - Y) and expand the square. The cross term has expectation E\big[(\hat p(X) - \pi(X))\, E[\pi(X) - Y \mid X]\big] = 0, and E[(\pi(X) - Y)^2 \mid X] = \pi(X)(1 - \pi(X)). The first term vanishes only if \hat p = \pi almost surely. ∎

The second term is irreducible: it depends on how predictable the outcome is. Predicting the prevalence \bar y for everyone scores \bar y(1 - \bar y), and the scaled Brier score is 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\bar y\,(1 - \bar y)} .

6.5 Overfitting, optimism and internal validation

Definition 6.9 — Optimism The optimism of a performance measure is the difference between its apparent value, on the development data, and its expected value on new patients from the same population.

Algorithm 6.10 — Bootstrap correction of optimism (Efron, 1983; Harrell et al., 1996)

  1. Fit the model on the full data and compute the apparent performance P_{\text{app}}.
  2. For b = 1, \dots, B: draw a bootstrap sample, repeat the whole modelling procedure on it (variable selection included), and compute the performance of the resulting model on the bootstrap sample, P_b^{\text{boot}}, and on the original data, P_b^{\text{orig}}.
  3. Estimate the optimism by the mean of P_b^{\text{boot}} - P_b^{\text{orig}}, and report P_{\text{app}} minus this optimism.

A single train–test split wastes data and gives unstable estimates; the bootstrap or repeated cross-validation is preferred (Steyerberg et al., 2001). The optimism grows with the number of candidate predictors per event. The rule of ten events per variable (Peduzzi et al., 1996) has given way to sample-size calculations that target, among other criteria, an expected calibration slope of at least 0.9 (Riley et al., 2020). Shrinkage, uniform (Van Houwelingen and Le Cessie, 1990) or penalized, reduces overfitting.

Example 6.11 — Optimism in a small data set Code 6.14 fits a logistic model with 20 candidate predictors, of which 4 matter, on 200 patients with 70 events (3.5 events per variable). It then checks the model on 20 000 new patients from the same population:

  • AUC: apparent 0.857, bootstrap-corrected 0.790, on new patients 0.724;
  • calibration slope on new patients: 0.46.

The bootstrap removes only half of the optimism seen on new patients (0.067 of 0.133): at so few events per variable its estimate is itself noisy. The slope shows predictions far too extreme.

6.6 Clinical usefulness: net benefit

A model is used to decide whom to treat, and discrimination and calibration do not say whether these decisions improve.

Definition 6.12 — Net benefit When patients with \hat p \ge t are treated, the net benefit at threshold t is \mathrm{NB}(t) = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{n} - \frac{\mathrm{FP}}{n}\cdot \frac{t}{1 - t}, where TP and FP count true and false positives. A decision curve plots \mathrm{NB}(t) over a range of thresholds (Vickers and Elkin, 2006), together with “treat none”, whose net benefit is 0, and “treat all”, whose net benefit at prevalence \bar y is \bar y - (1 - \bar y)\,\frac{t}{1 - t} .

Proposition 6.13 — Meaning of the threshold Suppose treating a patient who has the outcome brings a benefit B, and treating one who does not causes a harm H. Treating a patient of risk p is worthwhile if and only if p > t with t = \frac{H}{H + B}, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \frac{H}{B} = \frac{t}{1 - t} .

The net benefit is then the expected utility per patient of the model-guided policy, relative to treating nobody, in units of B.

Proof. The expected utility of treating a patient of risk p is pB - (1 - p)H, positive exactly when p > H/(H + B).

For the policy “treat if \hat p \ge t”, the utility per patient is (B \cdot \mathrm{TP} - H \cdot \mathrm{FP})/n. Dividing by B and replacing H/B by t/(1-t) gives \mathrm{NB}(t). ∎

In Code 6.14 the prevalence is 0.32. At t = 0.1 the model’s net benefit (0.232) is below that of treating everyone (0.240), so the model is useless there. At t = 0.2 it beats both alternatives (0.166 against 0.145 and 0).

Validation on patients from another setting (external validation), the updating of a model for a new setting, and the measures used for survival models are presented in section 8.9.

Code 6.14 — Apparent and corrected performance, calibration and net benefit

import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
rng = np.random.default_rng(9)

def fit_logistic(X, y, iters=25):
    """Maximum likelihood logistic regression with intercept (Newton-Raphson)."""
    Z = np.c_[np.ones(len(y)), X]
    b = np.zeros(Z.shape[1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-Z @ b))
        b += np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ (Z * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]), Z.T @ (y - p))
    return b

def predict(b, X):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(b[0] + X @ b[1:])))

beta = np.r_[0.8, -0.6, 0.5, 0.4, np.zeros(16)]          # 4 real predictors, 16 noise
def draw(n):
    X = rng.standard_normal((n, beta.size))
    return X, (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-1.0 + X @ beta)))).astype(int)

X, y = draw(200)                                          # development sample
Xv, yv = draw(20_000)                                     # large external sample
b = fit_logistic(X, y)
auc_app = roc_auc_score(y, predict(b, X))
print(f"events {y.sum()}, candidate predictors {X.shape[1]}")

# Bootstrap optimism correction (Efron 1983; Harrell et al. 1996)
optimism = []
for _ in range(200):
    i = rng.integers(0, len(y), len(y))
    bb = fit_logistic(X[i], y[i])
    optimism.append(roc_auc_score(y[i], predict(bb, X[i])) - roc_auc_score(y, predict(bb, X)))
p = predict(b, Xv)
slope = fit_logistic(np.log(p / (1 - p))[:, None], yv)[1]   # logistic recalibration
print(f"AUC apparent {auc_app:.3f}, optimism-corrected {auc_app - np.mean(optimism):.3f}, "
      f"external {roc_auc_score(yv, p):.3f}")
print(f"external: calibration slope {slope:.2f}, mean risk {p.mean():.3f} "
      f"vs observed {yv.mean():.3f}, Brier {np.mean((p - yv) ** 2):.3f}")

# Net benefit at threshold t (Vickers and Elkin, 2006)
for t in (0.1, 0.2, 0.4):
    tp, fp = np.mean((p >= t) & (yv == 1)), np.mean((p >= t) & (yv == 0))
    nb_all = yv.mean() - (1 - yv.mean()) * t / (1 - t)
    print(f"t={t}: net benefit model {tp - fp * t / (1 - t):.3f}, treat all {nb_all:.3f}")
# events 70, candidate predictors 20
# AUC apparent 0.857, optimism-corrected 0.790, external 0.724
# external: calibration slope 0.46, mean risk 0.341 vs observed 0.316, Brier 0.203
# t=0.1: net benefit model 0.232, treat all 0.240
# t=0.2: net benefit model 0.166, treat all 0.145
# t=0.4: net benefit model 0.064, treat all -0.140

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Validation assesses discrimination (AUC, C index), calibration (calibration-in-the-large, slope, plots), overall error (Brier score) and clinical usefulness (net benefit).
  • The AUC is the probability that a case is ranked above a control; it ignores calibration.
  • On its own data, a maximum likelihood logistic model always has perfect calibration-in-the-large and slope, so calibration needs new data or resampling.
  • Apparent performance is optimistic; the bootstrap must repeat the whole modelling procedure, and few events per variable give predictions that are too extreme.
  • A model is useful at a threshold only if its net benefit beats both treating everyone and treating no one.

7. Introduction to causal inference

Randomization answers causal questions by design (ch. 2). When a trial is impossible, the effect of a treatment must be estimated from observational data, where treated and untreated patients differ. This chapter states the assumptions that make this possible and the estimators that follow.

7.1 Causal estimands

Recall the potential outcomes Y(1), Y(0) of Definition 2.1 and the consistency relation Y = Y(A), which also assumes that one patient’s treatment does not affect another’s outcome (no interference).

Definition 7.1 — Causal estimands

  • The average treatment effect is \mathrm{ATE} = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)].
  • The average effect in the treated is \mathrm{ATT} = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 1].
  • For a binary outcome, the causal risk ratio is P(Y(1) = 1)/P(Y(0) = 1).

These are marginal (population-average) effects. Conditional effects, given covariates, can differ from them even without confounding when the measure is an odds ratio or a hazard ratio, which are not collapsible (Exercise 16).

7.2 Identification by standardization

Theorem 7.2 — g-formula Let L be a set of baseline covariates, and assume

  1. consistency: Y = Y(A);
  2. conditional exchangeability: Y(a) \perp A \mid L for a = 0, 1;
  3. positivity: P(A = a \mid L) > 0 almost surely.

Then the mean potential outcome is identified from the observed distribution: E[Y(a)] = E\big[\, E[Y \mid A = a, L] \,\big] = \int E[Y \mid A = a, L = l]\, dP_L(l).

Proof. By the tower property, E[Y(a)] = E\big[E[Y(a) \mid L]\big].

By exchangeability, E[Y(a) \mid L] = E[Y(a) \mid A = a, L]; positivity makes this conditioning well defined.

By consistency, E[Y(a) \mid A = a, L] = E[Y \mid A = a, L]. ∎

The formula, due to Robins (1986), standardizes the outcome to the covariate distribution of the whole population. In practice one fits an outcome model \hat m_a(l) and averages \hat m_1(L_i) - \hat m_0(L_i) over the sample (g-computation).

Example 7.3 — Standardizing the kidney-stone data In Example 3.6, 51 % of patients have small stones and 49 % large ones. Assuming stone size is the only confounder, the standardized success rates are 0.931 \times 0.51 + 0.730 \times 0.49 = 0.833 \ \text{(A)}, \qquad 0.867 \times 0.51 + 0.688 \times 0.49 = 0.779 \ \text{(B)}.

The standardized difference is +0.054 in favour of A, against a crude difference of -0.046.

7.3 Inverse probability weighting

Theorem 7.4 — Inverse probability weighting Under the assumptions of Theorem 7.2, with \pi_a(L) = P(A = a \mid L), E[Y(a)] = E\left[\frac{\mathbb{1}\{A = a\}\, Y}{\pi_a(L)}\right].

Proof. By consistency, \mathbb{1}\{A = a\}Y = \mathbb{1}\{A = a\}Y(a). Condition on L: E\left[\frac{\mathbb{1}\{A = a\}\, Y(a)}{\pi_a(L)} \,\Big|\, L\right] = \frac{P(A = a \mid L)\; E[Y(a) \mid L]}{\pi_a(L)} = E[Y(a) \mid L], where exchangeability factorizes the expectation and positivity allows the division. Taking expectations gives the result. ∎

Weighting each patient by the inverse probability of the treatment received creates a pseudo-population in which treatment is independent of L. The Horvitz–Thompson (1952) form divides by n, the more stable Hájek form by the sum of the weights. Near-violations of positivity produce huge weights, which stabilization or truncation tame at the price of some bias.

7.4 The propensity score

Definition 7.5 — Propensity score The propensity score is e(L) = P(A = 1 \mid L), the probability of receiving the treatment given the covariates.

Theorem 7.6 — Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983)

  1. Balancing: A \perp L \mid e(L).
  2. If (Y(0), Y(1)) \perp A \mid L, then (Y(0), Y(1)) \perp A \mid e(L).

Proof.

  1. Since e(L) is a function of L, P(A = 1 \mid L, e(L)) = e(L). By the tower property, P\big(A = 1 \mid e(L)\big) = E\big[P(A = 1 \mid L) \mid e(L)\big] = e(L). The two conditional probabilities coincide, so A is independent of L given e(L).

  2. Write Y^\ast = (Y(0), Y(1)). By the tower property and exchangeability given L, P\big(A = 1 \mid Y^\ast, e(L)\big) = E\big[P(A = 1 \mid Y^\ast, L) \mid Y^\ast, e(L)\big] = E\big[e(L) \mid Y^\ast, e(L)\big] = e(L), which does not depend on Y^\ast. ∎

A scalar score thus replaces a high-dimensional L for matching, stratification or weighting. The fitted model is judged by the balance it achieves, measured by standardized mean differences (below 0.1), not by its AUC (Austin, 2011).

7.5 Doubly robust estimation

Proposition 7.7 — Augmented IPW is doubly robust Let \hat m_a be an outcome model and \hat e a propensity model. The augmented IPW estimator of the ATE is \hat\psi = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \left[\hat m_1(L_i) - \hat m_0(L_i) + \frac{A_i\,(Y_i - \hat m_1(L_i))}{\hat e(L_i)} - \frac{(1 - A_i)(Y_i - \hat m_0(L_i))}{1 - \hat e(L_i)}\right].

It is consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correctly specified (Robins, Rotnitzky and Zhao, 1994).

Proof. Consider the treated term, and let m_1, e be the limits of the fitted models, \mu_1(L) = E[Y \mid A = 1, L] the true regression and \pi(L) the true propensity score.

Conditioning on L, E[A(Y - m_1(L)) \mid L] = \pi(L)\,(\mu_1(L) - m_1(L)). The limit of the treated part minus E[Y(1)] = E[\mu_1(L)] is therefore E\left[\big(\mu_1(L) - m_1(L)\big)\,\frac{\pi(L) - e(L)}{e(L)}\right], which vanishes if m_1 = \mu_1 or e = \pi. The control term is symmetric. ∎

The bias is a product of the two errors. This is why the estimator tolerates flexible, slowly converging machine-learning fits of both models, the starting point of double machine learning (ch. 9).

Example 7.8 — Estimators compared In Code 7.13, two confounders drive treatment and the true effect is 2, with an outcome that depends on e^{L_1}:

  • the naive difference of means is 4.80;
  • g-computation with a model linear in L_1 gives 2.31, and with the correct model 2.00;
  • IPW with the correct propensity model gives 2.00;
  • AIPW gives 2.04 with a wrong outcome model, 2.00 with a wrong propensity model, and 2.36 when both are wrong.

7.6 Causal diagrams

Definition 7.9 — Causal DAG, paths and colliders A causal directed acyclic graph has an arrow X \to Y when X is a direct cause of Y. Along a path:

  • a chain A \to M \to Y goes through a mediator M;
  • a fork A \leftarrow L \to Y goes through a common cause L;
  • a collider A \to C \leftarrow Y meets at a common effect C.

A path is blocked by a set Z if it contains a chain or fork whose middle node is in Z, or a collider such that neither it nor any of its descendants is in Z.

Theorem 7.10 — Back-door criterion (Pearl, 1995) A set L of covariates satisfies the back-door criterion relative to a treatment A and an outcome Y if

  • no element of L is a descendant of A, and
  • L blocks every path between A and Y that starts with an arrow into A.

Then conditional exchangeability holds given L, and Theorem 7.2 identifies the effect.

Proof. See Pearl (2009, section 3.3). The idea: the blocked back-door paths carry no association, and the remaining paths from A to Y are causal. Excluding descendants of A prevents blocking part of the effect or opening colliders. ∎

The criterion explains the checklist of Definition 3.5, and it explains two errors: adjusting for a mediator (the LDL cholesterol of Example 1.2) removes part of the effect, and adjusting for a collider creates an association.

Example 7.11 — Collider bias Two independent diseases each have prevalence 0.1, and a patient is hospitalized if he or she has either one. Among hospitalized patients:

  • if the second disease is absent, the first is certainly present;
  • if the second disease is present, the first has probability 0.1.

Restricting to hospital patients, a form of conditioning on a common effect, makes the two diseases strongly negatively associated (Berkson’s bias). Many selection biases have this structure (Hernán, Hernández-Díaz and Robins, 2004).

7.7 Target trial emulation

Definition 7.12 — Target trial emulation To answer a causal question with observational data, first write the protocol of the randomized trial that would answer it: eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, assignment, time zero, follow-up, outcome, causal contrast and analysis plan. Then emulate each element with the data, and adjust for baseline confounders as if assignment had been random given them (Hernán and Robins, 2016).

Aligning eligibility, treatment assignment and the start of follow-up at time zero removes the immortal time of ch. 3 by construction (Hernán et al., 2016). When strategies differ only after time zero, such as “start within six months”, patients are cloned into every compatible strategy, censored when they deviate, and reweighted. The emulation still assumes no unmeasured confounding.

The E-value of VanderWeele and Ding (2017) measures how strong such confounding would have to be. For an observed risk ratio \mathrm{RR} > 1, E = \mathrm{RR} + \sqrt{\mathrm{RR}\,(\mathrm{RR} - 1)} is the minimum risk ratio that an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to explain the estimate away. For \mathrm{RR} = 2, E = 3.41; a protective ratio is first inverted.

Code 7.13 — g-computation, IPW and AIPW on simulated confounded data

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(10)

def fit_logistic(Z, y, iters=25):
    b = np.zeros(Z.shape[1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-Z @ b))
        b += np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ (Z * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]), Z.T @ (y - p))
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-Z @ b))

# Confounders L1, L2; treatment depends on both; true average treatment effect = 2
n = 50_000
L1, L2 = rng.standard_normal(n), rng.standard_normal(n)
A = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-1 + 1.2 * L1 - 0.6 * L2)))).astype(float)
Y = 2 * A + 2 * np.exp(L1) + L2 + rng.standard_normal(n)
one = np.ones(n)

def outcome_model(features):          # linear regression of Y on (1, A, features)
    F = np.c_[one, A, features]
    coef = np.linalg.lstsq(F, Y, rcond=None)[0]
    m1 = np.c_[one, one, features] @ coef
    m0 = np.c_[one, 0 * one, features] @ coef
    return m1, m0

def aipw(m1, m0, e):
    return np.mean(m1 - m0 + A * (Y - m1) / e - (1 - A) * (Y - m0) / (1 - e))

e_good = fit_logistic(np.c_[one, L1, L2], A)          # correct propensity model
e_bad = fit_logistic(np.c_[one, L2], A)               # omits L1
m1_bad, m0_bad = outcome_model(np.c_[L1, L2])         # linear in L1: misspecified
m1_good, m0_good = outcome_model(np.c_[np.exp(L1), L2])

w1, w0 = A / e_good, (1 - A) / (1 - e_good)
print(f"naive difference         {Y[A == 1].mean() - Y[A == 0].mean():.3f}")
print(f"g-formula, wrong model   {np.mean(m1_bad - m0_bad):.3f}")
print(f"g-formula, right model   {np.mean(m1_good - m0_good):.3f}")
print(f"IPW (Hajek)              {(w1 @ Y) / w1.sum() - (w0 @ Y) / w0.sum():.3f}")
print(f"AIPW, wrong outcome      {aipw(m1_bad, m0_bad, e_good):.3f}")
print(f"AIPW, wrong propensity   {aipw(m1_good, m0_good, e_bad):.3f}")
print(f"AIPW, both wrong         {aipw(m1_bad, m0_bad, e_bad):.3f}")
# naive difference         4.799
# g-formula, wrong model   2.312
# g-formula, right model   1.997
# IPW (Hajek)              2.001
# AIPW, wrong outcome      2.036
# AIPW, wrong propensity   1.996
# AIPW, both wrong         2.364

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • Causal effects compare potential outcomes; they are identified from observational data under consistency, conditional exchangeability and positivity.
  • Standardization (g-formula) averages an outcome model; inverse probability weighting reweights by the propensity score; both remove measured confounding only.
  • The propensity score balances the covariates it is built from; judge it by balance, not by discrimination.
  • Augmented IPW is consistent if either model is correct, and its bias is a product of the two errors.
  • Causal diagrams say which covariates to adjust for: confounders yes, mediators and colliders no; target trial emulation aligns time zero and removes immortal time.

8.1 Estimands and intercurrent events

The PICO question of section 1.4 leaves open what happens when patients stop their treatment, take a rescue medication or die before the endpoint is measured. The ICH E9(R1) addendum (2019) asks for an explicit estimand.

Definition 8.1 — Estimand An estimand is the precise treatment effect that a study targets. It is specified by five attributes:

  1. the population of patients;
  2. the treatment conditions that are compared;
  3. the variable (endpoint) measured on each patient;
  4. the handling of intercurrent events, which occur after the start of treatment and affect the interpretation of the endpoint, such as stopping treatment, taking a rescue medication, or dying;
  5. the population-level summary: a difference of means, a risk ratio, a hazard ratio.

The addendum names five strategies for intercurrent events: treatment policy, hypothetical, composite, while on treatment, and principal stratum.

Example 8.2 — An estimand in diabetes A trial compares a new drug with placebo on HbA1c at week 26. Some patients receive rescue insulin when their glucose gets too high.

  • Under the treatment-policy strategy, the outcome is HbA1c at week 26 whatever the rescue: the effect of prescribing the drug in practice.
  • Under the hypothetical strategy, the target is the HbA1c that would have been observed without rescue: a pharmacological effect, which needs modelling assumptions to be estimated.

The two estimands answer different questions and can differ substantially.

8.2 Non-compliance and the complier average effect

Section 2.7 noted that non-compliance dilutes the intention-to-treat effect. Let Z be the randomized arm and D the treatment received, and suppose that controls cannot obtain the treatment.

Proposition 8.3 — Dilution of the ITT effect Call compliers the patients who take the treatment if assigned to it, and never-takers the others. Assume randomization and that the arm affects the outcome only through the treatment received (exclusion restriction). With \pi_c the proportion of compliers, E[Y \mid Z = 1] - E[Y \mid Z = 0] = \pi_c \; E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid \text{complier}] .

Proof. By randomization, the proportion of compliers and the distribution of potential outcomes within each type are the same in both arms.

Never-takers receive no treatment in either arm, so they contribute E[Y(0) \mid \text{never-taker}] to both means, and their contributions cancel.

Compliers receive D = Z: they contribute \pi_c E[Y(1) \mid \text{complier}] in arm 1 and \pi_c E[Y(0) \mid \text{complier}] in arm 0. The difference is the formula. ∎

Dividing the ITT effect by \pi_c estimates the effect among compliers. This is the instrumental-variable estimator, with randomization as the instrument (Angrist, Imbens and Rubin, 1996).

8.3 Interim analyses and group-sequential designs

Section 2.6 noted that repeated looks at accumulating data are a form of multiplicity: stopping a trial at the first p < 0.05 inflates the type I error.

Proposition 8.4 — Two looks at the data Let Z_1 be the test statistic after half the patients and Z_2 after all of them, both standard normal under H_0. Then \mathrm{Corr}(Z_1, Z_2) = \sqrt{1/2}, and testing at 1.96 at both looks has a type I error of about 0.083 instead of 0.05.

Proof. Write Z_1 = S_{m}/\sqrt{m} and Z_2 = S_{2m}/\sqrt{2m}, where S_k is a sum of k independent standardized contributions. Then \mathrm{Cov}(S_m, S_{2m}) = \mathrm{Var}(S_m) = m, so the correlation is m/\sqrt{m \cdot 2m} = \sqrt{1/2}.

The error is 1 - P(|Z_1| < 1.96, |Z_2| < 1.96) for this bivariate normal pair, which numerical integration or simulation (Code 8.6) puts at 0.083. ∎

Definition 8.5 — Group sequential designs A group sequential design plans K analyses and a boundary c_k for each, chosen so that the overall type I error is \alpha.

  • Pocock (1977) uses a constant boundary.
  • O’Brien and Fleming (1979) use c_k = c\sqrt{K/k}: very strict at the first looks and close to 1.96 at the end.
  • Alpha-spending functions (Lan and DeMets, 1983) fix how much error is spent as information accrues, so that the number and timing of looks need not be fixed in advance.

In practice an independent data monitoring committee performs the interim analyses, so that the investigators stay blind to the accumulating results.

Code 8.6 — Type I error of repeated looks and O’Brien–Fleming boundaries

import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize

# K equally spaced looks at the accumulating z-statistic of a trial under H0.
# Z_k = S_k / sqrt(k) where S_k is a sum of k independent N(0, 1) increments.
def crossing_prob(bounds, reps=400_000, seed=3):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    K = len(bounds)
    S = np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal((reps, K)), axis=1)
    Z = S / np.sqrt(np.arange(1, K + 1))
    return np.mean((np.abs(Z) > np.asarray(bounds)).any(axis=1))

for K in (1, 2, 3, 5):
    print(f"{K} looks at 1.96: type I error {crossing_prob([1.96] * K):.3f}")

# O'Brien-Fleming boundaries c * sqrt(K / k), with c tuned to a 5 % type I error
K = 5
f = lambda c: crossing_prob(c * np.sqrt(K / np.arange(1, K + 1))) - 0.05
c = optimize.brentq(f, 1.8, 2.4, xtol=1e-3)
print("O'Brien-Fleming, 5 looks:", np.round(c * np.sqrt(K / np.arange(1, K + 1)), 2))
# 1 looks at 1.96: type I error 0.050
# 2 looks at 1.96: type I error 0.083
# 3 looks at 1.96: type I error 0.107
# 5 looks at 1.96: type I error 0.141
# O'Brien-Fleming, 5 looks: [4.56 3.23 2.63 2.28 2.04]

With five looks, the final boundary of 2.04 is barely above 1.96: early stopping costs little power, which is why O’Brien–Fleming boundaries are popular.

8.4 Cluster and crossover trials

Some interventions act on groups: a hygiene programme in a hospital ward, a training of general practitioners. Such trials randomize clusters, and patients of the same cluster resemble each other.

Proposition 8.7 — Design effect Let k clusters of m patients be randomized, with outcomes of variance \sigma^2 and correlation \rho between two patients of the same cluster (the intracluster correlation). The variance of the mean over the n = km patients is \mathrm{Var}(\bar Y) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\,\big(1 + (m - 1)\rho\big).

Proof. The sum over one cluster has variance m\sigma^2 + m(m-1)\rho\sigma^2: m variance terms and m(m-1) covariance terms.

Clusters are independent, so the sum over k clusters has k times this variance. Dividing by n^2 = k^2m^2 gives the result. ∎

The factor 1 + (m-1)\rho multiplies the sample size of Theorem 2.7. With clusters of m = 20 and \rho = 0.05, it equals 1.95: the trial of Example 2.8 needs 759 patients per arm instead of 389.

In a crossover trial, each patient receives both treatments in random order, separated by a washout period. Each patient is his or her own control, which removes the between-patient variance. The design suits chronic, stable conditions and treatments without lasting (carry-over) effects.

8.5 Subgroup analyses

Trials often report effects in subgroups (men and women, age classes). These analyses are fragile.

Proposition 8.8 — Spurious subgroup findings Suppose a treatment has no effect at all, and the null hypothesis of no effect is tested at level 0.05 in each of K disjoint subgroups. The probability that at least one subgroup is “significant” is 1 - 0.95^K, \qquad \text{which is } 0.40 \text{ for } K = 10 .

Proof. Under the null hypothesis, the tests in disjoint subgroups are independent, each with a rejection probability of 0.05. The probability that none rejects is 0.95^K. ∎

The proper analysis tests the interaction, the difference between subgroup effects, for a few pre-specified subgroups. In the ISIS-2 trial (ISIS-2 Collaborative Group, 1988), aspirin appeared useless for patients born under Gemini or Libra, a result reported precisely to show how chance produces such findings.

8.6 Restricted mean survival time

When hazards are not proportional, a single hazard ratio summarizes the difference poorly. A summary that is always interpretable is the area under the survival curve up to a horizon.

Definition 8.9 — Restricted mean survival time For a horizon \tau, \mathrm{RMST}(\tau) = E\big[\min(T, \tau)\big] = \int_0^\tau S(t)\,dt .

The equality holds because E[Y] = \int_0^\infty P(Y > t)\,dt for any non-negative Y, and P(\min(T, \tau) > t) = S(t) for t < \tau.

The RMST is estimated by the area under the Kaplan–Meier curve. The difference between two arms is the mean event-free time gained over the horizon, in months, a quantity that patients understand (Royston and Parmar, 2013).

Example 8.10 — RMST by hand For the curve of Example 4.9 with \tau = 12 months, the area is a sum of rectangles: 3 \times 1 + 2 \times 0.9 + 3 \times 0.675 + 3 \times 0.54 + 1 \times 0.36 = 8.805 \text{ months}.

Over the first year, these patients live on average 8.8 months.

8.7 Sample size of a survival trial

For a time-to-event endpoint, the information in a trial is carried by the events, not by the patients.

Theorem 8.11 — Number of events (Schoenfeld, 1983) Let a proportion \pi of patients be allocated to the experimental arm, and let the hazards be proportional with hazard ratio \mathrm{HR}. The two-sided log-rank test at level \alpha has power 1 - \beta when the number of events is D = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2}{\pi(1 - \pi)\,(\log \mathrm{HR})^2} .

Proof. Sketch. Under alternatives close to the null, the proportions of the two arms in the risk sets stay near \pi. Each event then adds about \pi(1 - \pi) to the variance V of Theorem 4.11.

Each event also adds about \pi(1-\pi)\log\mathrm{HR} to the expectation of O_1 - E_1. The statistic (O_1 - E_1)/\sqrt V is therefore approximately normal with mean \sqrt{D\pi(1-\pi)}\,\log\mathrm{HR} and variance 1.

The power computation of Theorem 2.7 then gives the formula. ∎

Example 8.12 — Sizing a cancer trial To detect \mathrm{HR} = 0.75 with 1:1 allocation, \alpha = 0.05 and power 0.80, D = \frac{(1.960 + 0.842)^2}{0.25 \times (\log 0.75)^2} = \frac{7.85}{0.25 \times 0.0828} = 379.4, \quad \text{so } 380 \text{ events}.

If half of the patients are expected to have an event during the planned accrual and follow-up, the trial needs about 760 patients. With power 0.90 the number of events becomes 508.

The expected proportion of events depends on the accrual rate, the follow-up and the control survival; trials therefore fix the number of events and analyse when it is reached.

8.8 Competing risks

A patient waiting for a transplant can die, or receive the transplant; an elderly patient followed for cancer recurrence can die of another cause. When one type of event prevents the others, the events compete.

Definition 8.13 — Cause-specific hazards and cumulative incidence Let T be the time to the first event and K \in \{1, \dots, k\} its cause. The cause-specific hazard of cause j is h_j(t) = \lim_{dt \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t + dt,\ K = j \mid T \ge t)}{dt} .

The overall survival is S(t) = \exp\big(-\sum_j H_j(t)\big), and the cumulative incidence function of cause j is F_j(t) = P(T \le t,\ K = j) = \int_0^t S(u^-)\, h_j(u)\, du .

Proposition 8.14 — One minus Kaplan–Meier overestimates the cumulative incidence Treat competing events as censored. Then one minus the Kaplan–Meier estimator of cause 1 targets 1 - e^{-H_1(t)}. This quantity satisfies 1 - e^{-H_1(t)} \ \ge\ F_1(t), with strict inequality as soon as competing events can occur before t.

Proof. The Kaplan–Meier estimator built from the events of cause 1 alone estimates \exp(-H_1(t)), by Proposition 4.2 applied to the cause-specific hazard. Since S(u) = e^{-H_1(u) - H_2(u)} \le e^{-H_1(u)}, F_1(t) = \int_0^t S(u)\, h_1(u)\, du \le \int_0^t e^{-H_1(u)}\, h_1(u)\, du = 1 - e^{-H_1(t)} .

Equality requires H_2 = 0 wherever h_1 > 0 on [0, t]. ∎

The quantity 1 - e^{-H_1(t)} describes a hypothetical world in which the competing event has been abolished, not the risk that patients actually face. The real-world risk is estimated by the Aalen–Johansen estimator (Aalen and Johansen, 1978), \hat F_j(t) = \sum_{i:\ t_i \le t} \hat S(t_i^-)\, \frac{d_{j,i}}{n_i}, where \hat S is the all-cause Kaplan–Meier estimator and d_{j,i} counts the events of cause j at t_i.

Two regression models answer two questions. The cause-specific Cox model, a Cox model for h_j with competing events censored, describes the aetiology. The Fine–Gray model (Fine and Gray, 1999) puts proportional hazards on the subdistribution hazard of F_j and is used to predict cumulative incidences (Putter, Fiocco and Geskus, 2007; Austin, Lee and Fine, 2016).

Code 8.15 — Aalen–Johansen against one minus Kaplan–Meier

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)

# Two competing causes with constant cause-specific hazards, plus censoring
h1, h2, n = 0.05, 0.10, 5000
T = rng.exponential(1 / (h1 + h2), n)
cause = np.where(rng.random(n) < h1 / (h1 + h2), 1, 2)
C = rng.uniform(0, 30, n)
X = np.minimum(T, C)
status = np.where(T <= C, cause, 0)                 # 0 = censored

order = np.argsort(X)
X, status = X[order], status[order]
at_risk = n - np.arange(n)                          # no ties with continuous times
S_all = np.cumprod(1 - (status > 0) / at_risk)      # all-cause KM
S_prev = np.r_[1.0, S_all[:-1]]
cif1 = np.cumsum(S_prev * (status == 1) / at_risk)  # Aalen-Johansen, cause 1
one_minus_km = 1 - np.cumprod(1 - (status == 1) / at_risk)

k = np.searchsorted(X, 10.0) - 1
true_cif = h1 / (h1 + h2) * (1 - np.exp(-(h1 + h2) * 10))
print(f"t = 10: 1-KM {one_minus_km[k]:.3f} (target {1-np.exp(-h1*10):.3f}), "
      f"Aalen-Johansen {cif1[k]:.3f} (true CIF {true_cif:.3f})")
# t = 10: 1-KM 0.387 (target 0.393), Aalen-Johansen 0.253 (true CIF 0.259)

With constant hazards 0.05 and 0.10, the true cumulative incidence of cause 1 at 10 years is F_1(10) = \tfrac13\big(1 - e^{-1.5}\big) = 0.259, while one minus Kaplan–Meier targets 1 - e^{-0.5} = 0.393: a 50 % overestimate.

8.9 Validating survival models, external validation and updating

A prognostic model for a time to event predicts \hat S(\tau \mid x) at a horizon \tau, for instance with the Breslow estimator of Definition 5.4. Its validation adapts the measures of ch. 6 to censoring.

  • Discrimination: Harrell’s or Uno’s C index (section 6.2), or the time-dependent AUC at \tau, which compares subjects with an event before \tau to those still event-free at \tau (Heagerty, Lumley and Pepe, 2000; Blanche, Dartigues and Jacqmin-Gadda, 2013).
  • Calibration: within groups of predicted risk, compare the mean of 1 - \hat S(\tau \mid x) with the Kaplan–Meier estimate of the observed risk at \tau.
  • Overall error: the Brier score at \tau with inverse probability of censoring weights (Graf et al., 1999), defined below.

Definition 8.16 — Brier score at a horizon Let \hat G be the Kaplan–Meier estimator of the censoring distribution. The IPCW Brier score at \tau is \mathrm{BS}(\tau) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ \frac{\hat S(\tau \mid x_i)^2\, \mathbb{1}\{X_i \le \tau,\ \delta_i = 1\}}{\hat G(X_i)} + \frac{\big(1 - \hat S(\tau \mid x_i)\big)^2\, \mathbb{1}\{X_i > \tau\}}{\hat G(\tau)} \right].

Subjects censored before \tau get weight zero, and the others are up-weighted to represent them.

On patients from another time, place or setting, the case mix changes. A narrower range of patients lowers the AUC even for a correct model, and a different baseline risk shifts all predictions. Models are therefore updated in increasing order of complexity: the intercept (or the baseline hazard), then the calibration slope of Definition 6.5, then selected coefficients (Steyerberg, 2019). Royston and Altman (2013) describe these steps for Cox models.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • An estimand fixes how intercurrent events are handled; the treatment-policy and hypothetical strategies answer different questions.
  • Under one-sided non-compliance, the ITT effect is the proportion of compliers times the effect among them.
  • Two looks at 1.96 give a type I error of 0.083; group-sequential boundaries such as O’Brien–Fleming restore 0.05.
  • Clustering multiplies the sample size by 1 + (m-1)\rho; subgroup effects are judged by interaction tests.
  • The restricted mean survival time is the area under the survival curve up to a horizon; the information of a survival trial is its number of events (Schoenfeld’s formula).
  • With competing risks, one minus Kaplan–Meier overestimates the real-world risk; the Aalen–Johansen estimator does not.
  • Survival models are validated at a horizon with IPCW versions of the C index, AUC and Brier score.

9. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It surveys questions that are open at the time of writing, with entry points into the literature; each section ends with a question that could start a project or an internship.

9.1 Estimands and principal strata

The ICH E9(R1) addendum (section 8.1) made intercurrent events explicit, but several of its strategies target quantities that are not identified without strong assumptions. The principal stratum strategy, for instance, targets the effect among patients who would not experience the intercurrent event under either treatment (Frangakis and Rubin, 2002). Membership of such a stratum is never observed, so estimation relies on untestable assumptions or on sensitivity analyses.

Open question: which estimands for trials with frequent treatment discontinuation and deaths are both clinically meaningful and estimable under assumptions that can be checked?

9.2 Machine learning for time-to-event data

Random survival forests (Ishwaran et al., 2008) relax both the linearity and the proportional hazards assumptions of ch. 5. Neural extensions of the Cox model such as DeepSurv (Katzman et al., 2018) replace the linear predictor by a network but keep proportional hazards. On low-dimensional clinical data, a systematic review by Christodoulou et al. (2019) found no performance benefit of machine learning over logistic regression for binary outcomes, and the gains reported for survival are similarly debated.

The censoring mechanism complicates both training and evaluation. Conformal methods give distribution-free lower prediction bounds on survival times under censoring (Candès, Lei and Ren, 2023), and the IPCW measures of section 8.9 remain the standard for evaluation.

Open question: when do flexible survival models improve calibrated individual predictions, and how should their uncertainty be reported to clinicians?

9.3 Heterogeneous treatment effects

The average effect of ch. 7 hides differences between patients. Causal forests (Wager and Athey, 2018), meta-learners (Künzel et al., 2019) and double machine learning with cross-fitting (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) estimate conditional average treatment effects; Proposition 7.7 explains why products of slowly converging errors remain acceptable.

The PATH statement (Kent et al., 2020) recommends a cautious, risk-based approach in trials: estimate a baseline risk model first, then model the treatment effect across risk strata. Validating a model of individual effects is hard, since individual effects are never observed.

Open question: how can a model of treatment-effect heterogeneity be validated externally, with calibration and discrimination measures analogous to those of ch. 6?

9.4 Real-world evidence and transportability

Target trial emulation (section 7.7) is now applied to large health-care databases. The RCT DUPLICATE initiative compared emulations with the trials they mimicked and reported results that agree for some questions and not for others (Franklin et al., 2021).

When trial results must be carried to a different target population, transportability methods reweight or standardize the trial to the covariate distribution of that population (Dahabreh et al., 2020). Both lines rest on exchangeability assumptions that sensitivity analyses, such as the E-value, can only partly probe.

Open question: which features of a clinical question and of a database predict whether an emulated target trial will reproduce the randomized result?

9.5 Prediction models with artificial intelligence

A review of prediction models for COVID-19 (Wynants et al., 2020) found that almost all were at high risk of bias, mostly because of small samples, poor handling of missing data and no proper validation, the failures that ch. 6 warns against. TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al., 2024) extends the reporting rules to machine-learning models.

Two further problems are specific to deployment. Dataset shift between hospitals or over time degrades calibration first. And a model can be accurate while encoding inequities: Obermeyer et al. (2019) showed that an algorithm that used health-care costs as a proxy for health needs underestimated the needs of Black patients.

Open question: how should a deployed clinical prediction model be monitored and updated so that its calibration stays acceptable in every patient group?

9.6 Adaptive and platform trials

Group-sequential designs (section 8.3) are the simplest adaptive designs. Platform trials evaluate several treatments under a master protocol, adding and dropping arms as evidence accrues (Woodcock and LaVange, 2017). The RECOVERY trial used this design during the COVID-19 pandemic and showed that dexamethasone reduced mortality among hospitalized patients receiving respiratory support (RECOVERY Collaborative Group, 2021). Bayesian adaptive methods (Berry et al., 2010) adjust allocation probabilities or stopping rules as the posterior evolves.

Open question: how should type I error, time trends in the control arm and the sharing of controls between arms be handled when a platform trial runs for years?

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Several estimands of the ICH E9(R1) framework, such as principal strata, are identified only under untestable assumptions.
  • Flexible survival models must beat the Cox model on calibrated predictions, evaluated with the IPCW measures of section 8.9.
  • Heterogeneous treatment effects can be estimated with causal forests, meta-learners or double machine learning, but are hard to validate.
  • Target trial emulation and transportability extend trial evidence to real-world data, under exchangeability assumptions.
  • Deployed prediction models need monitoring for dataset shift and for calibration in every patient group; platform trials raise new questions of error control.

The trial of Example 8.12 (HR 0.75, power 0.80) adds an interim analysis after 190 of the 380 events, with O’Brien–Fleming boundaries 2.797 and 1.977. (a) Simulate the log-rank statistics at both looks, using Theorem 8.11 and the correlation of Proposition 8.4, and estimate the type I error, the power, the probability of stopping early and the expected number of events. (b) Then simulate full trials with exponential survival and uniform accrual and compare.

    Hints and expected result: in the normal approximation, the type I error is 0.050, the power 0.797 (0.801 for the fixed design), the probability of stopping at the interim 0.21 under the alternative, and the expected number of events 340 instead of 380. Full simulations should agree within Monte Carlo error.

  18. ★★★ Doubly robust estimation with machine learning. Replace the parametric models of Code 7.13 by random forests or gradient boosting from scikit-learn, (a) fitted and evaluated on the same data, and (b) with two-fold cross-fitting, as in double machine learning. Over 100 replications with n = 2000, compare the bias, the standard deviation and the coverage of the Wald interval based on the influence function.

    Hints and expected result: the variance is estimated by the empirical variance of the terms in the sum of Proposition 7.7, divided by n. Without cross-fitting, overfitted outcome models leave residuals too small and the coverage falls below 95 %; with cross-fitting it should be close to 95 % (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). Clip the estimated propensity scores, for instance to [0.01, 0.99].

Projects and assessment

The official assessment (master-mva.com, “Mode de validation”) combines a final exam (60%) and a home project with its presentation (40%); the home projects are carried out in groups and presented in the final 3-hour session. The group subjects below are proposals sized for that home project, the solo subjects suit individual practice or a smaller variant, and the written exam is a proposal in the spirit of the final exam. All use public or simulated data and run on a laptop.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Immortal time bias, reproduced. Paper study of Suissa (2008), “Immortal time bias in pharmaco-epidemiology”. Simulate cohorts in which a drug without effect is started during follow-up, and compare the analyses described in the paper: time-fixed “ever users”, exclusion of the immortal time, landmark, and time-dependent Cox model.
    • Success criterion: the naive rate ratios match Proposition 3.10 within Monte Carlo error, and the 95 % interval of the time-dependent hazard ratio covers 1 in 93–97 % of 500 replications.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 3, 5. Evaluation: the rubric below.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Internal validation strategies compared. Paper study of Steyerberg et al. (2001). On the Wisconsin diagnostic breast cancer data (569 patients, bundled with scikit-learn as load_breast_cancer), draw development samples of 100 patients, fit a penalized logistic model, and estimate its AUC and calibration slope by split-sample, 10-fold cross-validation and bootstrap optimism correction.
    • Success criterion: using the remaining patients as a test set, report the bias and root mean squared error of each method over 200 draws, and check whether the bootstrap has the smallest error, as the paper concludes.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report with a decision curve, a 10-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 0, 6. Evaluation: the rubric below.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Competing risks in primary biliary cholangitis. Use the Mayo Clinic PBC trial data (Fleming and Harrington, 1991, appendix D; distributed as pbc with the R package survival, from which it exports to a CSV file), with death and liver transplantation as competing events.
    • Work: implement the Aalen–Johansen estimator and the cause-specific Cox model from Codes 5.7 and 8.15, with tied times handled, compare with one minus Kaplan–Meier, and interpret the effect of bilirubin on each cause.
    • Success criterion: your curves match an established implementation (survfit of the R package survival, or AalenJohansenFitter of lifelines, which breaks ties by a small jitter) to three decimals, and the overestimation of Proposition 8.14 is quantified.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 4, 5, 8. Evaluation: the rubric below.

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 2 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet and a calculator allowed, four exercises.

  1. Question (4 points). Define confounding and immortal-time bias. For each, give a clinical example, say in which direction it biases the estimate, and name one design and one analysis that remove it.

    Expected: Definition 3.5 with confounding by indication as example, either direction; Definition 3.9 with the “ever treated” example, bias in favour of treatment (Proposition 3.10). Remedies: randomization or stratification, regression, standardization, IPW for confounding; time-dependent covariates, landmark or target trial emulation with an aligned time zero for immortal time.

  2. Question (6 points). Group A has survival times 3, 5, 7+, 9, 11+ and group B has 1, 2, 4, 6+, 8 (months). (a) Compute the Kaplan–Meier curves and the median survival in each group. (b) Compute the log-rank statistic and conclude at the 5 % level. (c) Give one reason why a hazard ratio from a Cox model could be misleading here.

    Expected: (a) A: 0.8, 0.6, 0.3 at 3, 5, 9, median 9; B: 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0 at 1, 2, 4, 8, median 4. (b) O_B = 4, E_B = 2.41, V = 1.42, statistic 1.77, p = 0.18: not significant. (c) Ten patients, few events, and nothing to check proportional hazards with.

  3. Question (5 points). A trial compares two strategies on mortality at one year, expected to be 30 % with the standard and 20 % with the new strategy. (a) Compute the number of patients per arm for \alpha = 0.05 and power 0.90. (b) The trial is finally randomized by hospital, with 40 patients per hospital and an intracluster correlation of 0.01: correct the sample size. (c) Explain why an interim analysis at half the patients cannot use the threshold 1.96.

    Expected: (a) 389 per arm (Example 2.8). (b) Design effect 1 + 39 \times 0.01 = 1.39, so 541 per arm, about 14 hospitals. (c) Two looks at 1.96 give a type I error of about 0.083 (Proposition 8.4); use, for example, O’Brien–Fleming boundaries.

  4. Question (5 points). Under consistency, conditional exchangeability given L and positivity, prove that E[Y(1)] = E[AY/e(L)] with e(L) = P(A = 1 \mid L). Then prove that A \perp L \mid e(L).

    Expected: the three-line proof of Theorem 7.4 with each assumption named where it is used; the balancing property of Theorem 7.6 through the tower property.
