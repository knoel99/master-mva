Audience: an M2 student with a solid M1 in probability and statistics who must say something reliable about events rarer than anything in the data: a 100-year flood, a record insurance loss, a failure that a monitoring system has never recorded.

Empirical first: take fifty years of daily data (18 250 values) from a Student law with 4 degrees of freedom. A Gaussian fitted to them puts the 100-year daily level at 5.7, while the true value is 18.1; the 1000-year level, 32.3, lies far beyond the largest observation (about 17). Modelling the tail instead of the bulk, with the tools of ch. 1–4, recovers the right order of magnitude from the few largest values.

Extreme value theory (EVT) is the part of probability and statistics that studies the tails of distributions. This course starts from its classical core, block maxima, peaks over thresholds and regular variation, then moves to its recent statistical-learning side: non-asymptotic guarantees for rare events, sparsity and dimension reduction in high-dimensional tails, anomaly detection and supervised learning in extreme regions. It uses the learning theory of Introduction to Statistical Learning and shares simulation methods with Computational Statistics.

  • Train and analyse classifiers and dimension-reduction methods that target the extreme region.
  • Read and present a recent research article on statistical learning with extreme values.

Let X be a random element (variable, vector or function), \|\cdot\| a semi-norm and t a large threshold. A major goal of EVT is to describe and infer the conditional law of t^{-1}X given that \|X\| is large.

The relevant thresholds may be as high as the largest of n observations, so probabilistic extrapolation must use the k_n \ll n observations with the largest norms.

This requires regular variation and maximum domains of attraction, which ensure that a limit \mu = \lim_{t \to \infty} \mathrm{Law}\big(t^{-1}X \,\big|\, \|X\| > t\big) exists up to standardisation, with applications in climate science, insurance and industrial monitoring. The course aims to introduce the most recent statistical-learning viewpoints on EVT: recent learning theory for rare events, the probabilistic background on EVT and regular variation, and algorithms for anomaly detection, extreme event classification and dimension reduction in tails.

0. Review: quantiles, maxima and concentration

Extreme value statistics uses four M1 tools in a specific form: generalised inverses of distribution functions, convergence in distribution, the Poisson approximation of rare counts, and concentration inequalities that see the variance. This chapter states each tool as it is used later, with short proofs.

Background is in the refresher Probability and Statistics for MVA.

0.1 Quantile functions

Definition 0.1 — Quantile function, endpoint, tail quantile function For a distribution function F, the quantile function and the right endpoint are F^{\leftarrow}(p) = \inf\{x \in \mathbb{R} : F(x) \ge p\}, \quad 0 < p < 1, \qquad x_F = \sup\{x : F(x) < 1\} \le +\infty . The tail quantile function is U(t) = F^{\leftarrow}(1 - 1/t), \qquad t > 1, so that U(t) is exceeded with probability about 1/t.

Proposition 0.2 — Quantile transform For 0 < p < 1 and real x, F^{\leftarrow}(p) \le x if and only if p \le F(x). Consequently:

  • if U is uniform on (0,1), then F^{\leftarrow}(U) has distribution function F;
  • if F is continuous and X \sim F, then F(X) is uniform, and V = 1/(1 - F(X)) is standard Pareto: P(V > v) = 1/v, \qquad v \ge 1 .

Proof. If p \le F(x), then x belongs to the set that defines F^{\leftarrow}(p), so F^{\leftarrow}(p) \le x.

Conversely, right-continuity gives F(F^{\leftarrow}(p)) \ge p, and monotonicity then gives F(x) \ge p for every x \ge F^{\leftarrow}(p).

The first consequence follows: P(F^{\leftarrow}(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x). For continuous F, F(F^{\leftarrow}(p)) = p, hence P(F(X) < p) = P(X < F^{\leftarrow}(p)) = p. ∎

The Pareto standardisation V = 1/(1 - F(X)) is the one used for multivariate extremes in ch. 5.

0.2 Convergence in distribution

Random variables X_n converge in distribution to X when F_n(x) \to F(x) at every continuity point x of F.

Equivalently (portmanteau theorem), \mathbb{E} f(X_n) \to \mathbb{E} f(X) for every bounded continuous f.

Lemma 0.3 — Pólya If F_n(x) \to F(x) for every x and F is continuous, the convergence is uniform: \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |F_n(x) - F(x)| \longrightarrow 0 .

Proof. Fix m and points x_1 < \dots < x_{m-1} with F(x_i) = i/m; set x_0 = -\infty and x_m = +\infty. For x \in [x_{i-1}, x_i], monotonicity gives F_n(x_{i-1}) - F(x_i) \le F_n(x) - F(x) \le F_n(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) . Since F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) = 1/m, the supremum is at most \max_i |F_n(x_i) - F(x_i)| + 1/m, which tends to 1/m. Let m \to \infty. ∎

0.3 Maxima and rare counts

Let X_1, \dots, X_n be independent with distribution function F, and M_n = \max(X_1, \dots, X_n). Then P(M_n \le x) = F(x)^n , which tends to 0 for x < x_F and to 1 for x \ge x_F: without rescaling, the maximum has a degenerate limit. The next result says which thresholds u_n keep P(M_n \le u_n) away from 0 and 1.

Proposition 0.4 — Poisson approximation Let 0 \le \tau \le \infty and let (u_n) be real numbers. Then P(M_n \le u_n) \longrightarrow e^{-\tau} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad n\,\big(1 - F(u_n)\big) \longrightarrow \tau . Moreover the number N_n of exceedances of u_n is binomial with parameters n and 1 - F(u_n). It converges in law to a Poisson variable of mean \tau when \tau < \infty, and \{M_n \le u_n\} = \{N_n = 0\} .

Proof. Write p_n = 1 - F(u_n).

  • If n p_n \to \tau < \infty, then p_n \to 0 and \log P(M_n \le u_n) = n \log(1 - p_n) = -n p_n (1 + o(1)) \to -\tau .
  • If n p_n \to \infty, then \log(1 - p) \le -p gives P(M_n \le u_n) \le e^{-n p_n} \to 0.
  • Conversely, if n p_n does not tend to \tau, a subsequence has a limit \tau' \ne \tau in [0, \infty]. Along it, the first two steps give the limit e^{-\tau'} \ne e^{-\tau}, a contradiction. ∎

0.4 Concentration that sees the variance

Estimating the probability p of a rare event from n observations means estimating a small number whose natural scale is k = np, the expected count. Inequalities that ignore the variance give errors of order 1/\sqrt{n}, which is useless when p itself is smaller than that.

Theorem 0.5 — Hoeffding and Bernstein Let \hat p be the frequency of an event of probability p in n independent trials, and \varepsilon > 0. Then P(|\hat p - p| \ge \varepsilon) \le 2 e^{-2 n \varepsilon^2} \quad \text{(Hoeffding)}, \qquad P(|\hat p - p| \ge \varepsilon) \le 2 \exp\Big(- \frac{n \varepsilon^2}{2p(1-p) + 2\varepsilon/3}\Big) \quad \text{(Bernstein)} .

Both are proved by bounding the moment generating function and optimising Markov’s inequality (Boucheron, Lugosi and Massart, 2013, ch. 2). Solving the Bernstein bound for \varepsilon gives the form used throughout ch. 6.

Key result — relative error of a rare frequency With probability at least 1 - \delta, |\hat p - p| \le \sqrt{\frac{2 p \log(2/\delta)}{n}} + \frac{2 \log(2/\delta)}{3n}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{|\hat p - p|}{p} \lesssim \sqrt{\frac{2 \log(2/\delta)}{k}}, \quad k = np .

The relative error depends on the expected number k of rare events, not on n. This is why every estimator of the course is driven by the k largest observations.

Example 0.6 — One rare frequency Take n = 10^4, p = 0.01 (so k = 100) and \delta = 0.05. Hoeffding’s radius is \sqrt{\log 40 / (2n)} = 0.0136, larger than p itself. Bernstein’s radius is 0.0030 , a relative error of 30 %. A simulation gives the true 95 % deviation, 0.0019.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, p, delta = 10_000, 0.01, 0.05
L = np.log(2 / delta)
hoeffding = np.sqrt(L / (2 * n))
bernstein = np.sqrt(2 * p * L / n) + 2 * L / (3 * n)

# empirical (1 - delta)-quantile of |p_hat - p| over 20 000 data sets
p_hat = rng.binomial(n, p, size=20_000) / n
empirical = np.quantile(np.abs(p_hat - p), 1 - delta)
print(f"Hoeffding {hoeffding:.4f}  Bernstein {bernstein:.4f}  "
      f"empirical {empirical:.4f}  (p = {p})")
# Hoeffding 0.0136  Bernstein 0.0030  empirical 0.0019  (p = 0.01)

Learning needs the same control uniformly over a class of events. A class \mathcal{A} of sets has Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension V if V is the largest size of a point set on which \mathcal{A} realises all 2^V subsets.

Theorem 0.7 — VC inequality There is a universal constant C such that, for every class \mathcal{A} of VC dimension V and every n \ge 1, the empirical measure P_n of n independent observations satisfies \mathbb{E} \sup_{A \in \mathcal{A}} |P_n(A) - P(A)| \le C \sqrt{\frac{V}{n}} . By the bounded-differences inequality, the supremum exceeds its mean by more than \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(2n)} with probability at most \delta.

The proof (symmetrisation and chaining) is in Boucheron, Lugosi and Massart (2013, ch. 13); complexity measures of this kind are part of the programme of Introduction to Statistical Learning. Chapter 6 makes this bound see the variance, as Bernstein does for a single event.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • F^{\leftarrow}(p) \le x if and only if p \le F(x); for continuous F, 1/(1 - F(X)) is standard Pareto.
  • Pointwise convergence to a continuous distribution function is uniform (Pólya).
  • P(M_n \le u_n) \to e^{-\tau} exactly when the expected number of exceedances n(1 - F(u_n)) tends to \tau.
  • Bernstein’s inequality gives a relative error of order \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/k} for an event with expected count k; Hoeffding’s does not.

1. Block maxima and the extremal types theorem

The central limit theorem describes sums; it says nothing about the largest observation. This chapter finds every possible limit of a normalised maximum, shows that these limits form a one-parameter family, and turns the result into a first statistical method: fitting that family to block maxima.

1.1 Context: return levels, risk and anomalies

Three applications share the same question.

  • Hydrology and climate. A dyke is designed for the level exceeded on average once in 100 or 1000 years, often beyond fifty years of records.
  • Insurance and finance. Capital requirements are set by high quantiles of a loss distribution, the value-at-risk, at levels such as 99.5 %.
  • Monitoring and anomaly detection. In the health monitoring of an aircraft engine or a bridge, the extreme sensor readings carry the critical information, and an alarm threshold must have a controlled false-alarm rate.

Definition 1.1 — Return level Let G be the distribution function of the maximum over one period (a year, say). The T-period return level is z_T = G^{\leftarrow}(1 - 1/T), the level exceeded on average once every T periods.

In all three cases the target lies near or beyond the edge of the sample. Extrapolation needs a model justified by a limit theorem, exactly as the central limit theorem justifies Gaussian confidence intervals.

1.2 Max-stability and the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem

Definition 1.2 — Maximum domain of attraction A distribution function F is in the maximum domain of attraction of a non-degenerate distribution function G, written F \in D(G), if there are constants a_n > 0 and b_n such that P\Big(\frac{M_n - b_n}{a_n} \le x\Big) = F^n(a_n x + b_n) \longrightarrow G(x) at every continuity point x of G.

Definition 1.3 — Max-stable laws and the GEV family A non-degenerate G is max-stable if for every n \ge 1 there are \alpha_n > 0 and \beta_n with G^n(\alpha_n x + \beta_n) = G(x). The generalised extreme value (GEV) distribution with index \gamma \in \mathbb{R} is G_\gamma(x) = \exp\big(-(1 + \gamma x)^{-1/\gamma}\big), \quad 1 + \gamma x > 0, \qquad G_0(x) = \exp(-e^{-x}) . It is max-stable: one checks that G_\gamma^n\big(n^\gamma x + (n^\gamma - 1)/\gamma\big) = G_\gamma(x) .

The sign of \gamma separates three types.

  • \gamma > 0, Fréchet type: polynomial right tail, infinite endpoint.
  • \gamma = 0, Gumbel type: exponentially light tail.
  • \gamma < 0, reversed Weibull type: finite right endpoint -1/\gamma.

Lemma 1.4 — Convergence of types Let F_n be distribution functions and G, G^* non-degenerate. If F_n(a_n x + b_n) \to G(x) and F_n(\alpha_n x + \beta_n) \to G^*(x) at continuity points, with a_n, \alpha_n > 0, then \frac{\alpha_n}{a_n} \to A \in (0, \infty), \qquad \frac{\beta_n - b_n}{a_n} \to B, \qquad G^*(x) = G(Ax + B) .

The lemma says that the limit is unique up to location and scale; its proof uses quantile functions and is in Resnick (2008, ch. 0).

Theorem 1.5 — Fisher–Tippett (1928), Gnedenko (1943) If F \in D(G), then G is max-stable, and there are \sigma > 0, \mu and \gamma such that G(x) = G_\gamma\Big(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\Big) . Conversely, every GEV distribution is in its own domain of attraction.

Proof. Step 1: the limit is max-stable. Fix a real t > 0 and set n_t = \lfloor n t \rfloor. Since n_t \to \infty and n / n_t \to 1/t, F^n(a_{n_t} x + b_{n_t}) = \big[F^{n_t}(a_{n_t} x + b_{n_t})\big]^{n/n_t} \longrightarrow G(x)^{1/t} . The same functions F^n also converge to G under the normalisation (a_n, b_n). Lemma 1.4 gives G^{1/t}(x) = G(A x + B) for some A = A(t) > 0 and B = B(t).

Equivalently, for every t > 0 there are \alpha(t) > 0 and \beta(t) with G^t\big(\alpha(t) x + \beta(t)\big) = G(x) .

Step 2: a functional equation. To keep the sketch short, assume that G is continuous and increasing where 0 < G < 1 (de Haan and Ferreira, 2007, ch. 1, treat the general case). Set \psi(x) = -\log(-\log G(x)) and let H be its inverse. Taking -\log(-\log) of the last display gives \psi(\alpha(t)x + \beta(t)) = \psi(x) + \log t, that is H(s + u) = \alpha(e^u)\, H(s) + \beta(e^u) \qquad \text{for all real } s, u .

After a shift we may take H(0) = 0. Then s = 0 gives \beta(e^u) = H(u), so with a(u) = \alpha(e^u), H(s + u) - H(u) = a(u)\, H(s) . Exchanging s and u and subtracting yields H(s)\,(1 - a(u)) = H(u)\,(1 - a(s)).

Step 3: solving it.

  • If a \equiv 1, H is additive and monotone, so H(s) = c s with c > 0; then \psi(x) = x/c and G(x) = \exp(-e^{-x/c}), the Gumbel type.
  • Otherwise a(u_0) \ne 1 for some u_0, so H(s) = c\,(1 - a(s)) with c \ne 0.
  • Substituting gives a(s + u) = a(s) a(u). A positive measurable solution is a(s) = e^{\gamma s} with \gamma \ne 0.
  • Hence H(s) = c'(e^{\gamma s} - 1)/\gamma with c' > 0, so \psi(x) = \gamma^{-1}\log(1 + \gamma x / c'), and G(x) = \exp\big(-(1 + \gamma x/c')^{-1/\gamma}\big) , a GEV law. The converse is the max-stability identity of Definition 1.3. ∎

The index \gamma is the extreme value index. It is the only parameter that matters for extrapolation; location and scale only fix units.

1.3 Domains of attraction and norming constants

Proposition 0.4, applied with u_n = a_n x + b_n and e^{-\tau} = G_\gamma(x), turns the definition of a domain of attraction into a statement about expected numbers of exceedances.

Proposition 1.6 — Domains via exceedance counts F \in D(G_\gamma) with constants a_n, b_n if and only if, for every x with 1 + \gamma x > 0, n\,\big(1 - F(a_n x + b_n)\big) \longrightarrow (1 + \gamma x)^{-1/\gamma} .

Example 1.7 — Norming constants

  • Exponential, 1 - F(x) = e^{-x}: with a_n = 1 and b_n = \log n, n e^{-x - \log n} = e^{-x}, so the limit is Gumbel (\gamma = 0).
  • Pareto, 1 - F(x) = x^{-\alpha} for x \ge 1: with \gamma = 1/\alpha, b_n = n^\gamma and a_n = \gamma n^\gamma, the expected count is exactly n\,\big(n^\gamma(1 + \gamma x)\big)^{-1/\gamma} = (1 + \gamma x)^{-1/\gamma} .
  • Uniform on (0,1): with a_n = 1/n and b_n = 1 - 1/n, the count is n(1/n - x/n) = 1 - x, the case \gamma = -1.
  • Gaussian: a_n = (2\log n)^{-1/2} and b_n = \sqrt{2 \log n} - \frac{\log\log n + \log 4\pi}{2\sqrt{2\log n}} give a Gumbel limit, with an error of order 1/\log n only.
  • Poisson or geometric: no non-degenerate limit exists, because the jumps of F do not vanish relative to its tail (Leadbetter, Lindgren and Rootzén, 1983, ch. 1).

The tutorial illustrates the weak convergence numerically. Because P(M_n \le x) = F^n(x) is explicit, the distance to the limit can be computed exactly instead of simulated.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

x = np.linspace(-2, 8, 4001)
gumbel = np.exp(-np.exp(-x))
for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10**4, 10**6]:
    # exponential: P(M_n - log n <= x) = (1 - e^{-x}/n)^n
    u = x + np.log(n)
    f_exp = np.exp(n * np.log1p(-np.minimum(np.exp(-u), 1.0)))
    # Gaussian with the classical norming constants
    ln = np.log(n)
    a_n = 1 / np.sqrt(2 * ln)
    b_n = np.sqrt(2 * ln) - (np.log(ln) + np.log(4 * np.pi)) / (2 * np.sqrt(2 * ln))
    f_gau = np.exp(n * np.log1p(-stats.norm.sf(a_n * x + b_n)))
    print(f"n = {n:>7}:  sup|F^n - Gumbel|  exponential {np.abs(f_exp - gumbel).max():.1e}"
          f"   Gaussian {np.abs(f_gau - gumbel).max():.3f}")
# exponential: 2.8e-02, 2.7e-03, 2.7e-04, 2.7e-05, 2.7e-07
# Gaussian:    0.064,   0.059,   0.048,   0.040,   0.031

The exponential maximum converges at rate 1/n; the Gaussian maximum is still 3 % away from its limit after a million observations. The rate of convergence, not only the limit, decides how well a GEV model fits finite blocks.

1.4 The method of block maxima

Split n = m k observations into k blocks of size m and keep the k block maxima. If m is large, they are approximately independent GEV variables with parameters (\mu, \sigma, \gamma).

Writing z_i = (M_{(i)} - \mu)/\sigma, the log-likelihood for \gamma \ne 0 is \ell(\mu, \sigma, \gamma) = -k \log \sigma - \Big(1 + \frac1\gamma\Big) \sum_{i=1}^k \log(1 + \gamma z_i) - \sum_{i=1}^k (1 + \gamma z_i)^{-1/\gamma} , on the set where every 1 + \gamma z_i > 0. The support depends on the parameters, so the usual regularity theory does not apply directly.

Smith (1985) shows that the maximum-likelihood estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal, with the usual rate, when \gamma > -1/2. This range contains almost all applications.

Key result — GEV return level Inverting G(z_p) = 1 - p with y_p = -\log(1 - p) gives z_p = \mu - \frac{\sigma}{\gamma}\Big(1 - y_p^{-\gamma}\Big), \qquad \gamma \ne 0 .

Example 1.8 — A 100-year return level We simulate 50 years of daily data from a Student law with 4 degrees of freedom, whose extreme value index is \gamma = 1/4. The true 100-year return level solves F^{365}(z) = 0.99. The GEV fitted to the 50 annual maxima gives \hat\gamma = 0.17 and a return level of 16.2, against a true value of z_{100} = 18.1 . The estimate lies below the largest annual maximum, 16.7: with 50 maxima, the shape parameter is poorly determined, and the return level inherits that uncertainty.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
years, m, nu = 50, 365, 4                      # 50 "years" of daily Student-t(4) data
x = rng.standard_t(nu, size=(years, m))
annual_max = x.max(axis=1)

# scipy's genextreme uses the shape c = -gamma
c, loc, scale = stats.genextreme.fit(annual_max)
gamma = -c
z100 = stats.genextreme.isf(0.01, c, loc=loc, scale=scale)
true_z100 = stats.t.ppf(0.99 ** (1 / m), nu)   # F^365(z) = 0.99
print(f"gamma_hat = {gamma:.3f} (true 1/nu = {1/nu})")
print(f"100-year return level: GEV {z100:.2f}, truth {true_z100:.2f}, "
      f"largest of the 50 annual maxima {annual_max.max():.2f}")
# gamma_hat = 0.166 (true 1/nu = 0.25)
# 100-year return level: GEV 16.21, truth 18.08, largest of the 50 annual maxima 16.68

1.5 Choosing the block size

With n fixed, the block size m trades bias against variance.

  • Large blocks make the GEV approximation accurate (low bias) but leave few maxima (high variance).
  • Small blocks give many maxima, but each is far from its limit law (high bias).
  • In environmental case studies, one-year blocks are the default: they remove seasonality and make the blocks comparable.

Example 1.9 — Bias and variance in the block size For the Student law of Example 1.8 and n = 18\,250, fifty repetitions give the estimates of \gamma = 0.25 below. The root-mean-square error is smallest for blocks of one to three months, not one year.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • The only possible limits of normalised maxima are the GEV laws G_\gamma, up to location and scale (Theorem 1.5); they are exactly the max-stable laws.
  • F \in D(G_\gamma) if and only if n(1 - F(a_n x + b_n)) \to (1 + \gamma x)^{-1/\gamma}.
  • Norming constants follow from the tail of F: Gumbel for exponential tails, Fréchet for Pareto tails, reversed Weibull for finite endpoints; convergence can be slow (Gaussian).
  • Block maxima are fitted by maximum likelihood, regular for \gamma > -1/2; return levels follow from the key formula; the block size trades bias against variance.

2. Peaks over thresholds and the generalised Pareto distribution

Block maxima keep one value per block. The peaks-over-threshold (POT) approach keeps every observation above a high threshold u and models the excesses X - u. This chapter derives their limit law, links it to block maxima, and discusses the choice of the threshold.

2.1 The generalised Pareto limit

Definition 2.1 — Generalised Pareto distribution The generalised Pareto distribution (GPD) with scale \sigma > 0 and index \gamma has distribution function H_{\sigma,\gamma}(y) = 1 - \Big(1 + \frac{\gamma y}{\sigma}\Big)^{-1/\gamma}, \qquad y > 0,\ 1 + \gamma y/\sigma > 0 , with H_{\sigma,0}(y) = 1 - e^{-y/\sigma}. For \gamma > 0 it is a shifted Pareto law, for \gamma = 0 the exponential law, and for \gamma < 0 its support ends at -\sigma/\gamma.

The conditional law of the excess over u is F_u(y) = P(X - u \le y \mid X > u).

Theorem 2.2 — Balkema and de Haan (1974), Pickands (1975) F \in D(G_\gamma) if and only if there is a positive function \sigma(u) such that \lim_{u \uparrow x_F}\ \sup_{0 \le y < x_F - u} \big|F_u(y) - H_{\sigma(u),\gamma}(y)\big| = 0 . The index \gamma is the same as for block maxima.

Proof. We prove the direct implication along the thresholds u = b_n; the converse runs the same argument backwards with u = U(n).

  • By Proposition 1.6, n(1 - F(b_n + a_n y)) \to (1 + \gamma y)^{-1/\gamma}, and at y = 0, n(1 - F(b_n)) \to 1.
  • Dividing the two limits, P(X - b_n > a_n y \mid X > b_n) = \frac{1 - F(b_n + a_n y)}{1 - F(b_n)} \longrightarrow (1 + \gamma y)^{-1/\gamma} = 1 - H_{1,\gamma}(y) .
  • So the excess over u = b_n, divided by \sigma(u) = a_n, converges in law to the standard GPD. Since the limit is continuous, Lemma 0.3 makes the convergence uniform in y.
  • Passing from the sequence b_n to a continuous threshold uses the monotonicity of F and the facts a_{n+1}/a_n \to 1 and (b_{n+1} - b_n)/a_n \to 0 (de Haan and Ferreira, 2007, ch. 1). ∎

2.2 Properties of the GPD

Proposition 2.3 — Threshold stability and mean excess Let Y follow the GPD with parameters (\sigma, \gamma) and let v \ge 0 be in its support.

Then Y - v given Y > v follows the GPD with parameters (\sigma + \gamma v, \gamma). For \gamma < 1 the mean excess is linear in v: e(v) = \mathbb{E}[Y - v \mid Y > v] = \frac{\sigma + \gamma v}{1 - \gamma} .

Proof. For y > 0, P(Y - v > y \mid Y > v) = \frac{(1 + \gamma (v + y)/\sigma)^{-1/\gamma}}{(1 + \gamma v/\sigma)^{-1/\gamma}} = \Big(1 + \frac{\gamma y}{\sigma + \gamma v}\Big)^{-1/\gamma} . When \gamma < 1, the mean of a GPD is \int_0^\infty \Big(1 + \frac{\gamma y}{\sigma}\Big)^{-1/\gamma} dy = \frac{\sigma}{1 - \gamma} ; apply it with scale \sigma + \gamma v. ∎

Threshold stability is the POT analogue of max-stability: raising the threshold changes the scale but not the index. For \gamma > 0, the moment of order r of a GPD is finite if and only if r < 1/\gamma, so heavy tails with \gamma \ge 1/2 have infinite variance; for \gamma \le 0 all moments are finite.

2.3 POT and block maxima: the Poisson–GPD model

The two approaches describe the same tail. Suppose that, within one period, the number of exceedances of u is Poisson with mean \lambda, and that the excesses are independent GPD variables, independent of their number.

Proposition 2.4 — From Poisson–GPD to GEV Under this model, the maximum M over one period satisfies, for x \ge 0, P(M \le u + x) = \exp\Big(-\lambda \Big(1 + \frac{\gamma x}{\sigma}\Big)^{-1/\gamma}\Big) , a GEV distribution with the same index \gamma, scale \tilde\sigma = \sigma \lambda^\gamma and location \tilde\mu = u + \sigma(\lambda^\gamma - 1)/\gamma.

Proof. Condition on the number N of exceedances. The maximum stays below u + x exactly when every excess is at most x: P(M \le u + x) = \sum_{j \ge 0} e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^j}{j!} H_{\sigma,\gamma}(x)^j = \exp\big(-\lambda\,(1 - H_{\sigma,\gamma}(x))\big) . For the GEV form, a direct computation gives \Big(1 + \gamma\,\frac{u + x - \tilde\mu}{\tilde\sigma}\Big)^{-1/\gamma} = \Big(\frac{\sigma + \gamma x}{\sigma \lambda^\gamma}\Big)^{-1/\gamma} = \lambda \Big(1 + \frac{\gamma x}{\sigma}\Big)^{-1/\gamma} . ∎

In practice one fits the POT model, which uses more data, and reports return levels on the annual scale through Proposition 2.4 (Exercise 8).

2.4 Threshold choice and inference

Let u be a high threshold exceeded by k of the n observations. The excesses y_1, \dots, y_k are fitted by maximising the GPD log-likelihood \ell(\sigma, \gamma) = -k \log \sigma - \Big(1 + \frac1\gamma\Big) \sum_{i=1}^k \log\Big(1 + \frac{\gamma y_i}{\sigma}\Big) . Combining the empirical exceedance rate k/n with the fitted GPD gives a model for the whole tail beyond u: 1 - F(x) \approx \frac{k}{n}\Big(1 + \gamma\,\frac{x - u}{\sigma}\Big)^{-1/\gamma}, \qquad x > u .

Key result — POT quantile Solving 1 - F(x_p) = p in the tail model gives x_p = u + \frac{\sigma}{\gamma}\Big[\Big(\frac{k}{np}\Big)^{\gamma} - 1\Big] , valid even when p < 1/n, that is beyond the range of the data.

The threshold trades bias against variance, as the block size did. Three diagnostics guide its choice.

  • Mean residual life plot: the empirical mean excess over v should be roughly linear in v above a good threshold (Proposition 2.3).
  • Parameter stability plot: above a good threshold, \hat\gamma and the modified scale \hat\sigma(v) - \hat\gamma v should stay constant, up to sampling noise.
  • Quantile–quantile plot of the excesses against the fitted GPD.

Example 2.5 — POT on the daily data We reuse a sample of 18 250 values of the Student law with 4 degrees of freedom, and estimate the daily level exceeded with probability p = 1/36\,500, whose true value is 18.1. As the threshold rises from the 90 % to the 99 % sample quantile, \hat\gamma moves from 0.17 to 0.20 and the estimate from 15.5 to 17.0.

The empirical mean excess grows from 0.90 to 1.49 between v = 1 and v = 4, an average slope of about 0.2. The exact mean excess of this law has slope 0.13 between 1 and 2 and 0.27 between 3 and 4: it approaches the asymptotic value \gamma/(1 - \gamma) = 1/3 only slowly, and the same second-order effect makes \hat\gamma too small.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
nu, n = 4, 18_250                      # 50 years of daily data, as in ch. 1
x = rng.standard_t(nu, size=n)
p = 1 / (365 * 100)                    # daily exceedance probability of the 100-year level
truth = stats.t.isf(p, nu)

for q in [0.90, 0.95, 0.99]:
    u = np.quantile(x, q)
    exc = x[x > u] - u
    k = exc.size
    gam, _, sig = stats.genpareto.fit(exc, floc=0)   # scipy's shape c is gamma
    x_p = u + sig / gam * ((k / (n * p)) ** gam - 1)
    print(f"u = {u:.2f} (k = {k:>4}): gamma {gam:.3f}, sigma {sig:.3f}, "
          f"x_p {x_p:.2f}  [truth {truth:.2f}]")

# empirical mean excess: asymptotically linear in u, slope gamma / (1 - gamma) = 1/3
for u in [1, 2, 3, 4]:
    print(f"mean excess over {u}: {np.mean(x[x > u] - u):.2f}")
# u = 1.54 (k = 1825): gamma 0.172, sigma 0.779, x_p 15.54  [truth 18.10]
# u = 2.12 (k =  913): gamma 0.197, sigma 0.843, x_p 16.61  [truth 18.10]
# u = 3.68 (k =  183): gamma 0.201, sigma 1.176, x_p 17.00  [truth 18.10]
# mean excess over 1, 2, 3, 4: 0.90, 1.03, 1.28, 1.49

Both POT and block maxima underestimate \gamma for the Student law at moderate thresholds: its tail is Pareto only up to a correction term, a second-order effect quantified in ch. 4.3.

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • Excesses over a high threshold converge to a generalised Pareto law with the same index \gamma as block maxima (Theorem 2.2).
  • The GPD is threshold-stable: raising the threshold by v changes the scale to \sigma + \gamma v, and the mean excess is linear in v.
  • A Poisson number of GPD excesses produces a GEV maximum (Proposition 2.4): the two approaches describe one tail.
  • The POT quantile u + (\sigma/\gamma)[(k/(np))^\gamma - 1] extrapolates beyond the data; the threshold is chosen with mean-excess and stability plots.

3. Regular variation and tail measures

For heavy tails, the whole of extreme value theory rests on one property: the tail behaves like a power function at large scales. This chapter develops that property, regular variation, and the measure-theoretic language of tail measures, which is the one that extends to vectors (ch. 5) and to learning (ch. 6–8).

3.1 Regularly varying functions

Definition 3.1 — Regular and slow variation A measurable function f : (0, \infty) \to (0, \infty) is regularly varying at infinity with index \rho, written f \in RV_\rho, if \lim_{t \to \infty} \frac{f(tx)}{f(t)} = x^\rho \qquad \text{for every } x > 0 . It is slowly varying if \rho = 0. Every f \in RV_\rho writes f(x) = x^\rho L(x) with L slowly varying.

Example 3.2 — Slowly varying or not

  • \log x, \log\log x and \exp(\sqrt{\log x}) are slowly varying; for the last one, \sqrt{\log tx} - \sqrt{\log t} \to 0.
  • 2 + \sin x is not: the ratio (2 + \sin tx)/(2 + \sin t) keeps oscillating.
  • The Pareto, Student (\nu degrees of freedom), Cauchy and Fréchet laws have tails 1 - F in RV_{-\alpha} with \alpha equal to the shape, \nu, 1 and the shape.
  • Gaussian and exponential tails are not regularly varying: their ratio tends to 0 for every x > 1.

Theorem 3.3 — Uniform convergence theorem If L is slowly varying, then for every 0 < a < b, \sup_{x \in [a, b]} \Big|\frac{L(tx)}{L(t)} - 1\Big| \longrightarrow 0 \qquad (t \to \infty) .

The proof is a measure-theoretic argument based on Egorov’s theorem (Bingham, Goldie and Teugels, 1987, Theorem 1.2.1). Uniformity is what makes the representation of ch. 3.3 possible.

3.2 Vague convergence and the tail measure

Tail measures live on spaces from which the origin has been removed: E = (0, \infty] in dimension one, E = [0, \infty]^d \setminus \{0\} for vectors. A measure on E is a Radon measure if it is finite on Borel sets bounded away from the origin; it may have infinite total mass.

Definition 3.4 — Vague convergence Radon measures \mu_n on E converge vaguely to \mu if \int f\,d\mu_n \to \int f\,d\mu for every continuous bounded f \ge 0 that vanishes on a neighbourhood of the origin.

Equivalently, \mu_n(A) \to \mu(A) for every Borel set A bounded away from the origin with \mu(\partial A) = 0.

Hult and Lindskog (2006) extend this notion, under the name M_0-convergence, to general metric spaces such as function spaces.

Proposition 3.5 — Three faces of a regularly varying tail Let X \sim F with x_F = \infty, \alpha > 0 and U the tail quantile function. The following are equivalent.

  1. 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha}.
  2. The tail measure exists: t\,P(X/U(t) \in \cdot\,) \to \nu_\alpha vaguely on (0, \infty], where \nu_\alpha((x, \infty]) = x^{-\alpha}.
  3. The rescaled exceedances converge to a Pareto law: P(X > ux \mid X > u) \to x^{-\alpha} for every x \ge 1.

Proof.

  1. \Leftrightarrow (3): (3) is (1) restricted to x \ge 1. For x < 1, write the ratio as the inverse of the ratio at the threshold ux and the level 1/x.

  2. \Rightarrow (2): by definition of U, 1 - F(y) > 1/t \ge 1 - F(U(t)) for every y < U(t). Hence, for 0 < \varepsilon < 1, 1 \ \ge\ t\,(1 - F(U(t)))\ \ge\ \frac{1 - F(U(t))}{1 - F((1 - \varepsilon)U(t))} \longrightarrow (1 - \varepsilon)^{\alpha} , so t(1 - F(U(t))) \to 1. Then t\,P(X > U(t)x) \to x^{-\alpha}, and the half-lines (x, \infty] determine vague convergence on (0, \infty]. The converse is similar. ∎

Statement 3 is the one-dimensional case of the objective of the course: the law of t^{-1}X given a large norm has a limit \mu, here a Pareto law. In practice the limit is estimated from the observations that are large relative to an intermediate level.

Definition 3.6 — Tail empirical measure Let k = k_n satisfy k \to \infty and k/n \to 0, an intermediate sequence. The tail empirical measure is \nu_n = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{X_i / U(n/k)} .

Proposition 3.7 — Consistency of the tail empirical measure If 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha}, then for every x > 0, \nu_n((x, \infty]) \to x^{-\alpha} in probability.

Proof. The count k\,\nu_n((x, \infty]) is binomial with parameters n and p_n = 1 - F(U(n/k)x). Its normalised mean (n/k)p_n tends to x^{-\alpha} by Proposition 3.5 with t = n/k. Its variance satisfies \mathrm{Var}\,\nu_n((x, \infty]) = \frac{n p_n (1 - p_n)}{k^2} \le \frac{1}{k} \cdot \frac{n p_n}{k} \longrightarrow 0 , and Chebyshev’s inequality concludes. ∎

Resnick (2007, ch. 4) shows more: \nu_n \to \nu_\alpha vaguely in probability, as random measures. The variance bound has the same structure as Bernstein’s inequality: the error is of order 1/\sqrt{k}.

Example 3.8 — The Hill estimator, informally For every observation and every level b > 0, \int_1^\infty \mathbb{1}\{X_i/b > y\}\,\frac{dy}{y} = \log\Big(\frac{X_i}{b}\Big)_+ . Integrating \nu_n((y, \infty]) against dy/y therefore gives \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^n \log\Big(\frac{X_i}{b}\Big)_+ \approx \int_1^\infty y^{-\alpha}\,\frac{dy}{y} = \frac1\alpha , \qquad b = U(n/k) .

The level b is unknown, but about k observations exceed it, so it is replaced by the (k+1)-th largest observation. The result is the Hill estimator of \gamma = 1/\alpha, studied in ch. 4.

Example 3.9 — Seeing the tail measure converge For the absolute value of a Student variable with 3 degrees of freedom, compare \nu_n((x, \infty]) with x^{-3}.

With k/n = 0.05, the value at x = 0.5 is 4.1 instead of 8: the level U(n/k)/2 is not yet in the tail.

With k/n = 0.001, the values at x = 1, 2, 4 agree with the limit within two standard deviations of the counts. The relative noise is largest where the counts are small (x = 4).

A gap remains at x = 0.5. The exact expectation there is 7.5, not 8: the level U(n/k)/2 \approx 6.5 is only moderately far in the tail. This is the second-order bias of ch. 4.3.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
nu = 3                                   # |Student-t(3)|: 1 - F is regularly varying, alpha = 3
grid = np.array([0.5, 1, 2, 4])
print("x       :", "  ".join(f"{t:7.2f}" for t in grid))
print("x^-3    :", "  ".join(f"{t ** -3.0:7.4f}" for t in grid))
for n, k in [(10_000, 500), (1_000_000, 1_000)]:
    x = np.abs(rng.standard_t(nu, size=n))
    b = stats.t.isf(k / (2 * n), nu)     # U(n/k): P(|X| > b) = k/n
    nu_n = [np.sum(x > b * t) / k for t in grid]
    print(f"k/n={k/n:.3f}:", "  ".join(f"{v:7.4f}" for v in nu_n))
# x^-3     :  8.0000   1.0000   0.1250   0.0156
# k/n=0.050:  4.1040   1.0140   0.1720   0.0240
# k/n=0.001:  7.2720   0.9380   0.1250   0.0220

3.3 Karamata’s representation and its consequences

Theorem 3.10 — Karamata representation L is slowly varying if and only if there are t_0 > 0, a measurable c(x) \to c \in (0, \infty) and a measurable \varepsilon(u) \to 0 such that L(x) = c(x) \exp\Big(\int_{t_0}^{x} \frac{\varepsilon(u)}{u}\,du\Big), \qquad x \ge t_0 .

Proof. If. We have \frac{L(tx)}{L(t)} = \frac{c(tx)}{c(t)} \exp\Big(\int_t^{tx} \frac{\varepsilon(u)}{u}\,du\Big) . The first factor tends to 1. The integral is at most |\log x| times the supremum of |\varepsilon| beyond \min(t, tx), which tends to 0.

Only if. Set h(y) = \log L(e^y), so that h(y + s) - h(y) \to 0 uniformly for s \in [0, 1] (Theorem 3.3); in particular h is locally bounded for large y. Decompose h(y) = \underbrace{\int_0^1 \big(h(y) - h(y + s)\big)\,ds}_{=:\,\delta(y)\ \to\ 0} + \underbrace{\int_y^{y+1} h(s)\,ds}_{=:\,g(y)} . A change of variables gives the exact identity g(y) = g(y_0) + \int_{y_0}^{y} e(s)\,ds, with e(s) = h(s + 1) - h(s) \to 0.

Return to x = e^y and substitute s = \log u: L(x) = \underbrace{e^{\delta(\log x) + g(y_0)}}_{c(x)} \exp\Big(\int_{e^{y_0}}^{x} \frac{e(\log u)}{u}\,du\Big), which is the representation with \varepsilon(u) = e(\log u) and t_0 = e^{y_0}. ∎

Corollary 3.11 — Potter bounds Let L be slowly varying and \delta > 0. There is t_0 such that, for all t \ge t_0 and tx \ge t_0, (1 - \delta) \min(x^{\delta}, x^{-\delta}) \ \le\ \frac{L(tx)}{L(t)} \ \le\ (1 + \delta) \max(x^{\delta}, x^{-\delta}) . In particular x^{-\delta}L(x) \to 0 and x^{\delta}L(x) \to \infty: a slowly varying function is negligible against any power.

The proof takes t_0 so large that c(tx)/c(t) \in [1 - \delta, 1 + \delta] and |\varepsilon| \le \delta beyond t_0; the integral is then at most \delta |\log x|. Potter bounds supply the integrable dominating functions of the next proof and of ch. 4.

Proposition 3.12 — Karamata’s theorem for tail integrals If f \in RV_\rho with \rho < -1 and f is locally bounded on [t_0, \infty), then \int_t^\infty f(u)\,du \ \sim\ \frac{t f(t)}{-\rho - 1} \qquad (t \to \infty) .

Proof. Substitute u = tx: the integral equals t f(t) \int_1^\infty \frac{f(tx)}{f(t)}\,dx. The integrand tends to x^\rho.

For \delta > 0 with \rho + \delta < -1, Corollary 3.11 bounds it by (1 + \delta)x^{\rho + \delta}, which is integrable on [1, \infty). Dominated convergence gives the limit \int_1^\infty x^\rho dx = 1/(-\rho - 1). ∎

Applied to f(u) = (1 - F(u))/u \in RV_{-\alpha - 1}, it makes Example 3.8 rigorous at the population level: \mathbb{E}\big[\log(X/t) \,\big|\, X > t\big] = \frac{1}{1 - F(t)} \int_t^\infty \frac{1 - F(u)}{u}\,du \longrightarrow \frac{1}{\alpha} .

3.4 Regular variation and domains of attraction

Theorem 3.13 — Gnedenko (1943), Fréchet domain Let \gamma > 0 and \alpha = 1/\gamma. Then F \in D(G_\gamma) if and only if x_F = \infty and 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha}.

One may then take a_n = U(n) and b_n = 0: P\Big(\frac{M_n}{U(n)} \le x\Big) \longrightarrow \Phi_\alpha(x) = \exp(-x^{-\alpha}), \qquad x > 0 .

If the tail is regularly varying, Proposition 3.5 with t = n gives n(1 - F(U(n)x)) \to x^{-\alpha}, and Proposition 0.4 turns this into the Fréchet limit. Conversely, if F \in D(G_\gamma), Proposition 1.6 gives regular variation along the integers, and one interpolates between them using that a_{n+1}/a_n \to 1 (de Haan and Ferreira, 2007, ch. 1). The Fréchet law is the GEV law up to location and scale: \Phi_\alpha(x) = G_\gamma((x - 1)/\gamma).

Theorem 3.14 — All domains of attraction F \in D(G_\gamma) if and only if there is a positive function a such that, for every x > 0, \frac{U(tx) - U(t)}{a(t)} \longrightarrow \frac{x^\gamma - 1}{\gamma} \qquad (t \to \infty), read as \log x when \gamma = 0. For \gamma > 0 this is equivalent to U \in RV_\gamma.

This characterisation through the tail quantile function covers the three types at once (de Haan and Ferreira, 2007, ch. 1). It is the starting point of the estimators of ch. 4, which all estimate U at levels t beyond n.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • f \in RV_\rho when f(tx)/f(t) \to x^\rho; slowly varying functions are negligible against any power (Potter bounds).
  • Karamata: L(x) = c(x)\exp\int_{t_0}^x \varepsilon(u)\,du/u with c \to c_\infty and \varepsilon \to 0.
  • A regularly varying tail has three equivalent faces: 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha}, a tail measure \nu_\alpha, and Pareto limits of rescaled exceedances.
  • The tail empirical measure built on the k largest observations is consistent, with error of order 1/\sqrt{k}.
  • The Fréchet domain is exactly the set of regularly varying tails, with a_n = U(n).

4. The Hill estimator and extreme quantiles

Chapter 3 suggested an estimator of the extreme value index built on the k largest observations. This chapter proves that it works, explains how to choose k, and uses it to estimate quantiles far beyond the sample.

4.1 The Hill estimator

Write X_{(1)} \ge X_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge X_{(n)} for the order statistics in decreasing order.

Definition 4.1 — Hill estimator (Hill, 1975) For 1 \le k < n and positive observations, H_{k,n} = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^{k} \log X_{(i)} - \log X_{(k+1)} . It estimates \gamma = 1/\alpha when 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha}.

Proposition 4.2 — Exact Pareto case If 1 - F(x) = x^{-\alpha} for x \ge 1, then k \alpha H_{k,n} follows the Gamma law with shape k and rate 1. Hence \mathbb{E} H_{k,n} = \frac1\alpha, \qquad \mathrm{Var}\, H_{k,n} = \frac{1}{k \alpha^2}, \qquad \sqrt{k}\,(\alpha H_{k,n} - 1) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N(0, 1) .

Proof. The variables E_i = \alpha \log X_i are independent standard exponentials, and k\alpha H_{k,n} = \sum_{i \le k} (E_{(i)} - E_{(k+1)}).

Given E_{(k+1)} = s, the k largest values are distributed as k independent copies of E conditioned on E > s.

By the memoryless property, each is s plus a standard exponential. The sum of the k excesses is therefore Gamma with shape k, whatever s. The central limit theorem gives the last claim. ∎

4.2 Weak consistency

Theorem 4.3 — Weak consistency (Mason, 1982) Let F be continuous with 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha}, and let k \to \infty with k/n \to 0. Then H_{k,n} \xrightarrow{\ P\ } \gamma = \frac1\alpha .

Proof. Let b = U(n/k), let \nu_n be the tail empirical measure of Definition 3.6, and Z = X_{(k+1)}/b.

Step 1: Z \to 1 in probability. Fix 0 < \varepsilon < 1. If Z > 1 + \varepsilon, at least k + 1 observations exceed b(1 + \varepsilon), so \nu_n((1 + \varepsilon, \infty]) > 1. This event has vanishing probability, because Proposition 3.7 gives \nu_n((1 + \varepsilon, \infty]) \to (1 + \varepsilon)^{-\alpha} < 1 . Symmetrically, Z < 1 - \varepsilon forces \nu_n((1 - \varepsilon, \infty]) \le 1, while its limit exceeds 1.

Step 2: an integral formula. The k largest observations are exactly those above X_{(k+1)} = bZ. As in Example 3.8, H_{k,n} = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^n \log\Big(\frac{X_i}{bZ}\Big)_+ = \int_Z^\infty \nu_n((y, \infty])\,\frac{dy}{y} .

Step 3: three pieces. Fix M > 1 and split the integral at 1 and M.

  • The first piece satisfies \Big|\int_Z^1 \nu_n((y, \infty])\,\frac{dy}{y}\Big| \le \nu_n\big((\min(Z,1), \infty]\big)\,|\log Z| . With probability tending to one, Z > 1/2, the first factor is at most \nu_n((1/2, \infty]), which is bounded in probability, and \log Z \to 0.
  • On [1, M], \mathbb{E}|\nu_n((y, \infty]) - y^{-\alpha}| \to 0 by the bias–variance bound of Proposition 3.7, and these expectations are at most 2. By dominated convergence, \int_1^M \nu_n\,dy/y \to (1 - M^{-\alpha})/\alpha in L^1.
  • The tail piece has expectation \mathbb{E}\int_M^\infty \nu_n((y, \infty])\,\frac{dy}{y} = \frac{n}{k}\big(1 - F(b)\big) \int_M^\infty \frac{1 - F(by)}{1 - F(b)}\,\frac{dy}{y} \longrightarrow \frac{M^{-\alpha}}{\alpha}, by Potter bounds and dominated convergence, as in Proposition 3.12.

Given 0 < \eta < 1, choose M with M^{-\alpha}/\alpha < \eta^2. Markov’s inequality on the tail piece then gives \limsup_{n \to \infty} P\big(|H_{k,n} - 1/\alpha| > 4\eta\big) \le \eta , and \eta is arbitrary. ∎

The proof uses only the consistency of the tail empirical measure and the integrability supplied by Karamata’s theory. The same route proves the consistency of many tail estimators (Resnick, 2007, ch. 4).

4.3 Bias, variance and the choice of k

Consistency says nothing about the error at a given k. Two errors compete.

  • Variance: about \gamma^2/k, as in the exact Pareto case; it decreases with k.
  • Bias: the k largest observations follow the Pareto approximation only if U(n/k) is far enough in the tail; the bias grows with k.

The bias is described by a second-order condition: a function A(t) \to 0, with |A| regularly varying of index \rho \le 0, such that \frac{1}{A(t)}\Big(\frac{U(tx)}{U(t)} - x^\gamma\Big) \longrightarrow x^\gamma\, \frac{x^\rho - 1}{\rho} .

Theorem 4.4 — Asymptotic normality Under the second-order condition with \gamma > 0 and \rho < 0, if k \to \infty, k/n \to 0 and \sqrt{k}\,A(n/k) \to \lambda \in \mathbb{R}, then \sqrt{k}\,\big(H_{k,n} - \gamma\big) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal N\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1 - \rho},\ \gamma^2\Big) .

The proof is in de Haan and Ferreira (2007, ch. 3). If |A(t)| behaves like t^\rho, minimising the asymptotic mean squared error \gamma^2/k + A(n/k)^2/(1 - \rho)^2 gives k_{\mathrm{opt}} \asymp n^{2|\rho|/(1 + 2|\rho|)} . Since \rho is unknown, k is chosen in practice from a Hill plot: H_{k,n} against k, looking for a stable region between the noisy small-k end and the biased large-k end.

4.4 Extreme quantile estimation

The target is x_p = U(1/p) for a probability p so small that np is below 1, often far below. Fewer than one observation is then expected above x_p, and the empirical quantile carries no information about it.

Definition 4.5 — Weissman estimator (Weissman, 1978) With d_n = k/(np), \hat x_p = X_{(k+1)}\, d_n^{\,H_{k,n}} . It rests on U \in RV_\gamma: U(1/p) / U(n/k) \approx d_n^{\gamma}, while X_{(k+1)} \approx U(n/k).

Proposition 4.6 — Where the error comes from Exactly, \log \frac{\hat x_p}{x_p} = \log\frac{X_{(k+1)}}{U(n/k)} + \big(H_{k,n} - \gamma\big) \log d_n - \log \frac{U(1/p)}{U(n/k)\, d_n^{\gamma}} .

Proof. Add and subtract \log U(n/k) + \gamma \log d_n in the identity \log \hat x_p - \log x_p = \log X_{(k+1)} + H_{k,n}\log d_n - \log U(1/p) . ∎

The first term is of order 1/\sqrt{k} and the last one is a deterministic bias. The middle term dominates: it has standard deviation about \gamma \log(d_n)/\sqrt{k}, and it multiplies the bias of the Hill estimator by \log d_n. Consistency of \hat x_p / x_p therefore requires \log(d_n)/\sqrt{k} \to 0 in addition to a vanishing bias (de Haan and Ferreira, 2007, ch. 4).

Example 4.7 — Hill and Weissman in practice Take n = 10^4 Student observations with 3 degrees of freedom (\gamma = 1/3) and p = 10^{-5}, so x_p = 47.9. Since np = 0.1, the sample maximum stays below x_p in about 90 % of data sets.

With k = 200, d_n = 2000 and \log d_n = 7.6.

  • Over 300 data sets, the Hill estimator has mean 0.370 and standard deviation 0.025, against \gamma/\sqrt{k} = 0.024: the variance is as predicted, and the bias is 0.037.
  • The ratio \hat x_p / x_p has quartiles 1.05, 1.22 and 1.40. The standard deviation of its logarithm, 0.19, matches \gamma \log(d_n)/\sqrt{k} = 0.18.
  • The median overestimation comes from the Hill bias amplified by \log d_n, partly offset by the other two terms.
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
nu, n, p = 3, 10_000, 1e-5               # Student-t(3): gamma = 1/3
truth = stats.t.isf(p, nu)

def hill(x_desc, k):
    """Hill estimator from a sample sorted in decreasing order."""
    return np.mean(np.log(x_desc[:k])) - np.log(x_desc[k])

x = np.sort(rng.standard_t(nu, size=n))[::-1]
print("Hill plot of one data set:",
      ", ".join(f"k={k}: {hill(x, k):.3f}" for k in [50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000]))

# 300 data sets, k = 200: Hill and the Weissman estimator of x_p
k, g, ratio = 200, [], []
for _ in range(300):
    xs = np.sort(rng.standard_t(nu, size=n))[::-1]
    g.append(hill(xs, k))
    ratio.append(xs[k] * (k / (n * p)) ** g[-1] / truth)
g, ratio = np.array(g), np.array(ratio)
print(f"Hill, k = {k}: mean {g.mean():.3f}, sd {g.std():.3f} (gamma/sqrt(k) = {1/3/np.sqrt(k):.3f})")
print(f"Weissman x_p / truth ({truth:.1f}): quartiles "
      + ", ".join(f"{q:.2f}" for q in np.quantile(ratio, [0.25, 0.5, 0.75]))
      + f"; sd of log-ratio {np.log(ratio).std():.3f}")
# Hill plot of one data set: k=50: 0.423, k=100: 0.391, k=200: 0.408, k=500: 0.416, k=1000: 0.475, k=2000: 0.606
# Hill, k = 200: mean 0.370, sd 0.025 (gamma/sqrt(k) = 0.024)
# Weissman x_p / truth (47.9): quartiles 1.05, 1.22, 1.40; sd of log-ratio 0.193

The Hill estimator requires \gamma > 0. For an index of any sign, the moment estimator of Dekkers, Einmahl and de Haan (1989) combines the first two moments of the log-excesses, M_j = \frac1k \sum_{i \le k} \big(\log X_{(i)} - \log X_{(k+1)}\big)^j, \quad j = 1, 2, \qquad \hat\gamma_M = M_1 + 1 - \frac12\Big(1 - \frac{M_1^2}{M_2}\Big)^{-1} . It is consistent over the whole range \gamma \in \mathbb{R} for positive data, and it is compared with Hill and with the GPD likelihood in the first solo project.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • The Hill estimator averages the log-excesses of the k largest observations over the (k+1)-th; for exact Pareto data, k\alpha H_{k,n} is Gamma(k, 1).
  • It is weakly consistent as soon as 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha}, k \to \infty and k/n \to 0 (Theorem 4.3).
  • Its error is a variance \gamma^2/k plus a second-order bias that grows with k; k is chosen on a Hill plot.
  • The Weissman estimator X_{(k+1)}(k/(np))^{H_{k,n}} extrapolates quantiles beyond the data; its relative error is about \gamma\log(k/(np))/\sqrt{k} plus an amplified bias.

5. Multivariate extremes

Floods hit several rivers at once, and a portfolio loses on several assets at once. For a random vector, chapters 1–4 handle each marginal tail; what is new is the dependence between large values. This chapter separates margins from dependence and describes every possible limit.

Vector inequalities and operations are componentwise: x \le y means x_j \le y_j for every j, and M_n is the vector of coordinatewise maxima.

5.1 Componentwise maxima and reduction to the standard case

Definition 5.1 — Multivariate domain of attraction F on \mathbb{R}^d is in the domain of attraction of G, with non-degenerate margins, if there are a_n \in (0, \infty)^d and b_n \in \mathbb{R}^d such that, at continuity points, F^n(a_n x + b_n) \longrightarrow G(x) . Each margin G_j is then a GEV law (Theorem 1.5), and G is max-stable by the argument of Step 1 of that proof.

Increasing transformations commute with maxima. This makes it possible to put all margins on a common scale and study the dependence alone.

Proposition 5.2 — Reduction to the standard case Let F have continuous margins F_j, and set V^j = 1/(1 - F_j(X^j)), so that each V^j is standard Pareto (Proposition 0.2). Then F \in D(G) if and only if

  1. each F_j \in D(G_j), and
  2. P\big(\max_{i \le n} V_i / n \le x\big) \to G_*(x), where G_* has standard Fréchet margins e^{-1/x_j}.

In that case G(x) = G_*\Big(\frac{-1}{\log G_1(x_1)}, \dots, \frac{-1}{\log G_d(x_d)}\Big) .

Proof sketch. The event \{M_n \le a_n x + b_n\} is the event that every \max_i V_i^j stays below 1/(1 - F_j(a_{nj}x_j + b_{nj})). By Proposition 1.6 these thresholds behave like -n/\log G_j(x_j). The full proof is in Resnick (2008, ch. 5). G_* carries all the dependence; it is called simple max-stable.

5.2 Max-infinitely divisible distributions

Definition 5.3 — Max-id distributions A distribution function G on \mathbb{R}^d is max-infinitely divisible (max-id) if G^t is a distribution function for every t > 0.

In dimension one every distribution function is max-id. In dimension d \ge 2 this fails in general, because G^t may violate the rectangle inequality. Max-stable laws are max-id, since G^{1/n} is G up to an affine change of variables.

Theorem 5.4 — Characterisation of max-id laws (Balkema and Resnick, 1977) Let \ell \in [-\infty, \infty)^d be the lower endpoint of G and E_\ell = [\ell, \infty] \setminus \{\ell\}. Then G is max-id if and only if there is a Radon measure \mu on E_\ell, the exponent measure, such that G(x) = \exp\big(-\mu(E_\ell \setminus [\ell, x])\big), \qquad x \ge \ell,\ x \ne \ell .

Proof sketch. Let N be a Poisson random measure with mean measure \mu and Y the componentwise maximum of its points. Then \{Y \le x\} is the event that N has no point outside [\ell, x], so P(Y \le x) = \exp\big(-\mu(E_\ell \setminus [\ell, x])\big) . Replacing \mu by t\mu gives G^t, again a distribution function.

Conversely, n(G^{1/n} - 1) \to \log G. The measures n P_n, with P_n the law of distribution function G^{1/n}, converge vaguely to a measure \mu with the required values (Resnick, 2008, ch. 5).

5.3 Simple max-stable distributions and the angular measure

Proposition 5.5 — Simple max-stable laws A distribution function G_* with standard Fréchet margins is max-stable if and only if G_*(x) = \exp(-\mu([0, x]^c)) for an exponent measure \mu on E = [0, \infty]^d \setminus \{0\} that is homogeneous of order -1 and normalised: \mu(tA) = t^{-1}\mu(A) \quad (t > 0), \qquad \mu(\{y : y_j > 1\}) = 1 \quad (j = 1, \dots, d) .

Proof. Max-stability with Fréchet margins reads G_*^t(tx) = G_*(x), that is t\,\mu(t[0, x]^c) = \mu([0, x]^c). Complements of rectangles determine \mu, which gives homogeneity. The margin \exp(-\mu(\{y_j > x_j\})) = e^{-1/x_j} gives the normalisation, and the converse is the same computation read backwards.

Theorem 5.6 — Angular representation (de Haan and Resnick, 1977) Fix a norm and let S = \{\theta \in [0, \infty)^d : \|\theta\| = 1\}. A homogeneous \mu factorises in polar coordinates r = \|x\|, \theta = x/\|x\|: for a finite measure \Phi on S, the angular measure, \mu\big(\{x : \|x\| > r,\ x/\|x\| \in B\}\big) = r^{-1}\,\Phi(B) . The margins impose \int_S \theta_j\,\Phi(d\theta) = 1 for each j, and G_*(x) = \exp\Big(-\int_S \max_{j} \frac{\theta_j}{x_j}\,\Phi(d\theta)\Big) .

Proof. Set \Phi(B) = \mu(\{\|x\| > 1, x/\|x\| \in B\}).

The set \{\|x\| > r, x/\|x\| \in B\} is r times the set at radius 1, so homogeneity gives the factor r^{-1}. Thus \mu is the product of r^{-2}dr and \Phi.

A point r\theta lies outside [0, x] when r\theta_j > x_j for some j, that is when r > \min_j x_j/\theta_j. Since \int_{r_0}^\infty r^{-2}dr = 1/r_0, \mu([0, x]^c) = \int_S \Big(\min_j \frac{x_j}{\theta_j}\Big)^{-1} \Phi(d\theta) = \int_S \max_j \frac{\theta_j}{x_j}\,\Phi(d\theta) . Taking x_j = 1 and the other coordinates infinite gives the margin constraint. ∎

With the L^1 norm, the constraints add up to \Phi(S) = d. Any finite measure on S that satisfies them is an angular measure: the class of limits is infinite-dimensional, which is why non-parametric and learning methods are natural here.

Definition 5.7 — Stable tail dependence function The stable tail dependence function (STDF) is \ell(x) = \mu\big([0, (1/x_1, \dots, 1/x_d)]^c\big) = \int_S \max_j\, \theta_j x_j\, \Phi(d\theta), \qquad x \in [0, \infty)^d . For data with continuous margins in a domain of attraction, \ell(x) = \lim_{t \to \infty} t\, P\Big(\exists j : 1 - F_j(X^j) \le \frac{x_j}{t}\Big) .

Proposition 5.8 — Properties of the STDF \ell is convex and homogeneous of order 1, and \max_j x_j \ \le\ \ell(x)\ \le\ \sum_j x_j . The upper bound holds everywhere if and only if \Phi sits on the axes (asymptotic independence); the lower bound holds everywhere if and only if \Phi is a point mass at the centre of S (complete dependence).

Proof. Convexity and homogeneity hold for each x \mapsto \max_j \theta_j x_j and survive integration. Bounding \max_j \theta_j x_j above by \sum_j \theta_j x_j and below by \theta_i x_i, then using the margin constraint, gives the two bounds. The extremal coefficient \ell(1, \dots, 1) \in [1, d] counts the “effective number of independent variables” among the extremes.

Example 5.9 — Two models

  • Logistic model (Gumbel, 1960): \ell(x) = \big(\sum_j x_j^{1/\beta}\big)^{\beta} with 0 < \beta \le 1. Independence is \beta = 1, complete dependence the limit \beta \to 0, and the extremal coefficient is d^\beta.
  • Beta angular model in dimension 2 with the L^1 norm: \Phi is twice the law of (W, 1 - W), with W \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a, a). For a = 2, \ell(1, 1) = 2\,\mathbb{E}\max(W, 1 - W) = 1.375 .

The exponent measure also gives an exact simulation method. By the mapping theorem, the points (d/\Gamma_i)\Theta_i form a Poisson random measure with mean measure \mu, when the \Gamma_i are the arrival times of a unit-rate Poisson process and the \Theta_i are independent with law \Phi/d.

Algorithm 5.10 — Exact simulation of a simple max-stable vector (L^1 norm)

  1. Set Z = 0 and \Gamma = 0.
  2. Repeat: draw E \sim \mathrm{Exp}(1) and \Theta \sim \Phi/d; set \Gamma \leftarrow \Gamma + E and Z \leftarrow \max(Z, (d/\Gamma)\Theta).
  3. Stop as soon as d/\Gamma \le \min_j Z_j, and return Z.

Since every coordinate of \Theta is at most 1 and \Gamma increases, no later point can raise any coordinate: the stopping rule is exact. By Theorem 5.4, P(Z \le x) = \exp(-\mu([0, x]^c)).

For the Beta model, 200 000 simulated vectors give P(Z^1 \le 1) = 0.3681, against e^{-1} = 0.3679, and P(Z \le (1, 1)) = 0.2541, against e^{-1.375} = 0.2528.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)

def simulate_max_stable(n, d, sample_angle):
    """Exact simulation of Z = max_i (d / Gamma_i) Theta_i (L1-norm angles, Phi(S) = d)."""
    z = np.zeros((n, d))
    gam = np.zeros(n)
    active = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
    while active.any():
        idx = np.flatnonzero(active)
        gam[idx] += rng.exponential(size=idx.size)          # next Poisson arrival
        r = d / gam[idx]
        z[idx] = np.maximum(z[idx], r[:, None] * sample_angle(idx.size))
        # angles have coordinates <= 1: no later point can raise any coordinate
        active[idx] = r > z[idx].min(axis=1)
    return z

def beta_angle(m, a=2.0):
    w = rng.beta(a, a, size=m)
    return np.column_stack([w, 1 - w])

z = simulate_max_stable(200_000, 2, beta_angle)
print(f"P(Z1 <= 1) = {np.mean(z[:, 0] <= 1):.4f}   (standard Frechet: {np.exp(-1):.4f})")
print(f"P(Z1 <= 1, Z2 <= 1) = {np.mean((z <= 1).all(axis=1)):.4f}   "
      f"(exp(-l(1,1)) = {np.exp(-1.375):.4f})")
# P(Z1 <= 1) = 0.3681   (standard Frechet: 0.3679)
# P(Z1 <= 1, Z2 <= 1) = 0.2541   (exp(-l(1,1)) = 0.2528)

5.4 Multivariate regular variation and peaks over thresholds

Definition 5.11 — Multivariate regular variation A vector V with standard Pareto margins is regularly varying with exponent measure \mu if t\,P(V/t \in \cdot\,) \to \mu vaguely on E. In polar form, for r \ge 1 and \Phi(\partial B) = 0, P\Big(\|V\| > tr,\ \frac{V}{\|V\|} \in B \ \Big|\ \|V\| > t\Big) \longrightarrow r^{-1}\, \frac{\Phi(B)}{\Phi(S)} .

This is the limit \mu of the course objective. Given a large norm, the rescaled radius is asymptotically standard Pareto, and the angle is asymptotically independent of it, with law \Phi/\Phi(S). Under the conditions of Proposition 5.2, the standardised vector V is regularly varying with the exponent measure of G_* (Resnick, 2008, ch. 5).

The multivariate peaks-over-threshold model keeps the observations that are large in at least one coordinate.

Rootzén and Tajvidi (2006) define the multivariate generalised Pareto distributions as the limits of (X - b(t))/a(t) given that X exceeds b(t) in some coordinate.

On the standardised scale, this is the law of V/t given \|V\|_\infty > t, that is \mu restricted to \{\|x\|_\infty > 1\} and normalised.

Proposition 5.12 — Tail dependence coefficient In dimension 2, with continuous margins, \chi = \lim_{u \uparrow 1} P\big(F_2(X^2) > u \,\big|\, F_1(X^1) > u\big) = 2 - \ell(1, 1) .

Proof. By inclusion–exclusion, P(F_1 > u, F_2 > u) = 2(1 - u) - P(F_1 > u \text{ or } F_2 > u). The last probability is (1 - u)\,\ell(1, 1)\,(1 + o(1)) by Definition 5.7 with t = 1/(1 - u). Divide by P(F_1 > u) = 1 - u. ∎

When \chi = 0 the variables are asymptotically independent: \Phi sits on the axes, joint extremes are negligible at the first order, and the exponent measure says nothing about them. The Gaussian law with correlation \rho < 1 is the main example (Exercise 10); finer tools are mentioned in ch. 9.

5.5 Non-parametric estimation in moderate dimension

The margins are unknown, so standardisation uses ranks. Let R_i^j be the rank of X_i^j among X_1^j, \dots, X_n^j (rank 1 is the smallest).

Definition 5.13 — Rank-based estimators With k the number of extremes used, \hat V_i^j = \frac{n}{n + 1 - R_i^j}, \qquad \hat\ell_k(x) = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{1}\big\{\exists j : R_i^j > n - k x_j\big\}, \hat\Phi_k(B) = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{1}\Big\{\|\hat V_i\| > \frac{n}{k},\ \frac{\hat V_i}{\|\hat V_i\|} \in B\Big\} .

Each estimator replaces t = n/k and the unknown F_j by the empirical distribution functions in the limits of Definitions 5.7 and 5.11. In moderate dimension, the angular measure can then be smoothed.

  • Histogram: count the angles \hat\Theta_i of the extremes in the cells of a partition of S.
  • Kernel estimate of the angular density, with reflection or Beta kernels at the boundary of the simplex.
  • Both need many extremes per cell, which limits them to a handful of dimensions; ch. 7 takes over beyond that.

Example 5.14 — Estimating tail dependence from ranks Simulate n = 50\,000 vectors V = R\,(2W, 2 - 2W), with R standard Pareto and W \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2, 2), then transform the margins arbitrarily: ranks do not see this. With k = 500:

  • \hat\ell_k(1, 1) = 1.386 and \hat\chi = 0.614, against 1.375 and 0.625;
  • about \Phi(S)\,k = 1000 extremes, whose angle histogram on five bins is close to the Beta(2,2) probabilities;
  • the reflected kernel estimate of the angular density at 1/2 is 1.32 against 1.50, a smoothing bias at the mode.
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n, k = 50_000, 500
# V = R (2W, 2 - 2W): R standard Pareto, W ~ Beta(2, 2); l(1, 1) = 1.375
r = 1 / rng.uniform(size=n)
w = rng.beta(2, 2, size=n)
v = r[:, None] * np.column_stack([2 * w, 2 - 2 * w])
x = np.column_stack([np.log(v[:, 0]), np.sqrt(v[:, 1])])   # margins changed, ranks unchanged

ranks = stats.rankdata(x, axis=0)                 # 1 = smallest
v_hat = n / (n + 1 - ranks)                       # rank standardisation to Pareto margins

l_hat = np.mean(np.any(ranks > n - k, axis=1)) * n / k
print(f"l_hat(1, 1) = {l_hat:.3f}   (truth 1.375),  chi_hat = {2 - l_hat:.3f}  (truth 0.625)")

ext = v_hat.sum(axis=1) > n / k                   # extremes in L1 norm
theta = v_hat[ext, 0] / v_hat[ext].sum(axis=1)    # angles in [0, 1]
edges = np.linspace(0, 1, 6)
hist = np.histogram(theta, bins=edges)[0] / theta.size
true = np.diff(stats.beta.cdf(edges, 2, 2))
print(f"{theta.size} extremes; bin masses", np.round(hist, 3), "truth", np.round(true, 3))
kde = stats.gaussian_kde(np.concatenate([theta, -theta, 2 - theta]))   # reflection at 0 and 1
print(f"kernel density at 1/2: {3 * kde(0.5)[0]:.2f}  (Beta(2,2) density: 1.50)")
# l_hat(1, 1) = 1.386   (truth 1.375),  chi_hat = 0.614  (truth 0.625)
# 1000 extremes; bin masses [0.098 0.264 0.278 0.263 0.097] truth [0.104 0.248 0.296 0.248 0.104]
# kernel density at 1/2: 1.32  (Beta(2,2) density: 1.50)

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Ranks or the transform 1/(1 - F_j) separate margins from dependence; the dependence of extremes is a simple max-stable law (Proposition 5.2).
  • Max-id laws are exactly those of the form \exp(-\mu(\cdot)) for an exponent measure; simple max-stable laws have a homogeneous one.
  • The angular measure \Phi on the unit sphere, with \int \theta_j\,d\Phi = 1, describes all possible dependence; the STDF \ell(x) = \int \max_j \theta_j x_j\,d\Phi summarises it.
  • Given a large norm, the radius is Pareto and the angle has law \Phi/\Phi(S): this is the limit \mu of the course objective.
  • Rank-based estimators of \ell and \Phi use the k most extreme observations; histograms and kernels work in a few dimensions only.

6. Statistical learning guarantees for rare events

Chapters 3–5 prove consistency as k \to \infty. Learning algorithms need more: bounds valid at finite n, uniformly over a class of sets. This chapter shows that the right bounds scale with k, the number of extreme observations, and not with n.

6.1 Why standard bounds fail

In this section X, X_1, \dots, X_n have standard Pareto margins, as after the standardisation of ch. 5; throughout the chapter, V denotes a VC dimension. A tail quantity is a limit \mu(A) = \lim_t t\,P(X \in tA), for a set A bounded away from the origin. Its natural estimator, with t = n/k, is \mu_n(A) = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{1}\Big\{X_i \in \frac{n}{k}A\Big\} = \frac{n}{k}\,P_n\Big(\frac{n}{k}A\Big), and its error splits into a stochastic term and a bias: \mu_n(A) - \mu(A) = \frac{n}{k}\,(P_n - P)\Big(\frac{n}{k}A\Big) + \Big[\frac{n}{k}\,P\Big(X \in \frac{n}{k}A\Big) - \mu(A)\Big] .

The sets (n/k)A have probability of order k/n. Theorem 0.7 bounds the stochastic term by (n/k)\,C\sqrt{V/n} = C\sqrt{nV}/k, which does not even tend to zero unless k \gg \sqrt{n}. The cure is the one of Example 0.6: use the variance, which is at most the probability of the rare region.

6.2 Vapnik-type inequalities for rare events

Theorem 6.1 — Finite class of rare sets Let B_1, \dots, B_N be sets with P(B_j) \le p. With probability at least 1 - \delta, \max_{j \le N} |P_n(B_j) - P(B_j)| \le \sqrt{\frac{2p\log(2N/\delta)}{n}} + \frac{2\log(2N/\delta)}{3n} .

Proof. Apply Bernstein’s inequality (Theorem 0.5) to each B_j at level \delta/N: the variance P(B_j)(1 - P(B_j)) is at most p, and the bound increases with the variance. A union bound over the N sets concludes. ∎

If p \approx c\,k/n, multiplying by n/k gives an error of order \sqrt{2c\log(2N/\delta)/k} on the tail scale. The next theorem removes the finiteness.

Theorem 6.2 — VC class of rare sets, in expectation Let \mathcal{A} have VC dimension V, let \mathbb{A} = \bigcup_{A \in \mathcal{A}} A and p = P(X \in \mathbb{A}) > 0. With C the constant of Theorem 0.7, \mathbb{E} \sup_{A \in \mathcal{A}} |P_n(A) - P(A)| \le \big(C\sqrt{V} + 1\big)\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}} .

Proof. Let N be the number of observations in \mathbb{A}; it is binomial with parameters n and p.

Given N, these observations are independent with law P_{\mathbb{A}} = P(\cdot \mid \mathbb{A}); let P'_N be their empirical measure.

Every A \in \mathcal{A} is contained in \mathbb{A}, so P_n(A) = (N/n)P'_N(A) and P(A) = p\,P_{\mathbb{A}}(A). Hence \sup_{A} |P_n(A) - P(A)| \le \frac{N}{n}\, \sup_A |P'_N(A) - P_{\mathbb{A}}(A)| + \Big|\frac{N}{n} - p\Big| .

  • Given N \ge 1, Theorem 0.7 bounds the conditional expectation of the first supremum by C\sqrt{V/N}. The first term thus has expectation at most C\sqrt{V}\,\mathbb{E}\sqrt{N}/n \le C\sqrt{Vp/n}, by Jensen’s inequality.
  • The second term has expectation at most \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(N)}/n \le \sqrt{p/n}. ∎

The argument is transparent: only the N \approx np observations in the rare region matter, and the classical VC rate 1/\sqrt{N} applies to them.

Theorem 6.3 — VC class of rare sets, with high probability There is a universal constant C' such that, for 0 < \delta < 1/e, with probability at least 1 - \delta, \sup_{A \in \mathcal{A}} |P_n(A) - P(A)| \le C'\Big(\sqrt{\frac{p\,V \log(1/\delta)}{n}} + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}\Big) .

Proof sketch. Talagrand’s concentration inequality, in the form of Bousquet (2002), controls the supremum around its mean with a variance term \sup_A \mathrm{Var}\,\mathbb{1}_A(X) \le p. Combine it with Theorem 6.2 and the inequality \sqrt{ab} \le (a + b)/2. Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2015) prove a bound of this form; Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2022) derive related uniform concentration bounds for frequencies of rare events.

Key result — the rate of learning in the tail If the rare region has probability p \le c\,k/n, then with probability at least 1 - \delta the stochastic error on the tail scale satisfies \frac{n}{k}\,\sup_{A} |P_n(A) - P(A)| \le C'\Big(\sqrt{\frac{c\,V \log(1/\delta)}{k}} + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{k}\Big) .

6.3 Application: tail dependence

For the stable tail dependence function, the rare region is \{\exists j : 1 - F_j(X^j) \le kT/n\}, of probability at most dTk/n, and the sets involved form a VC class of dimension of order d. The ranks add marginal errors of the same nature, each controlled by the same argument.

Theorem 6.4 — Non-asymptotic bound for the empirical STDF (Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon, 2015) For T > 0 and \delta \in (0, 1) satisfying mild conditions given in the paper, with probability at least 1 - \delta, \sup_{x \in [0, T]^d} \big|\hat\ell_k(x) - \ell(x)\big| \le C d\,\sqrt{\frac{T}{k}\log\frac{d + 3}{\delta}} + \mathrm{bias}\Big(\frac{n}{k}, T, d\Big), where C is universal and the bias is the supremum, over a box larger than [0, T]^d (its size is given in the paper), of the deterministic error of Definition 5.7 at t = n/k.

For the angular measure, Theorem 6.3 applies directly when the margins are known. Let X, X_1, \dots, X_n have standard Pareto margins, let \mathcal B be a class of Borel subsets of the sphere S with VC dimension V, and define the empirical angular measure \Phi_n(B) = \frac1k \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{1}\Big\{\|X_i\| > \frac{n}{k},\ \frac{X_i}{\|X_i\|} \in B\Big\} .

Corollary 6.5 — Empirical angular measure, known margins Let p = P(\|X\| > n/k), so that (n/k)\,p \to \Phi(S) by Definition 5.11 and Theorem 5.6. For 0 < \delta < 1/e, with probability at least 1 - \delta, \sup_{B \in \mathcal B} \big|\Phi_n(B) - \mathbb{E}\,\Phi_n(B)\big| \le C'\Big(\sqrt{\frac{np}{k} \cdot \frac{V \log(1/\delta)}{k}} + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{k}\Big) . The bias \mathbb{E}\,\Phi_n(B) - \Phi(B) tends to 0 for every B with \Phi(\partial B) = 0.

Proof. Apply Theorem 6.3 to the sets A_B = \{x : \|x\| > n/k,\ x/\|x\| \in B\}. Their union is \{\|x\| > n/k\}, of probability p.

Each A_B is a fixed set intersected with the preimage of B under the fixed map x \mapsto x/\|x\|. Neither operation increases the VC dimension, so the class of the A_B has VC dimension at most V.

Since \Phi_n(B) = (n/k)P_n(A_B) and \mathbb{E}\,\Phi_n(B) = (n/k)P(A_B), multiplying the bound of Theorem 6.3 by n/k gives the claim. The bias statement is Definition 5.11 with t = n/k and r = 1. ∎

With the L^1 norm, \Phi(S) = d, so the error is of order \sqrt{d\,V\log(1/\delta)/k}. With ranks instead of known margins, Clémençon, Jalalzai, Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2023) prove bounds of the same order 1/\sqrt{k} for the rank-based estimator \hat\Phi_k, uniformly over VC classes of subsets of the sphere. These results are the theoretical basis of the learning algorithms of ch. 7 and 8.

As before, k trades the stochastic error, decreasing like k^{-1/2}, against the bias, increasing with k.

6.4 Numerical illustration

Example 6.6 — The 1/\sqrt{k} rate In the Beta model of Example 5.14 the bias vanishes for n/k \ge 2, so the error of \hat\ell_k(1, 1) is purely stochastic. Over 200 data sets of size n = 20\,000, the root-mean-square error times \sqrt{k} stays near 0.42 for k from 25 to 1600.

With known margins, the count of extremes would be binomial and the error \sqrt{\ell(1,1)/k} \approx 1.17/\sqrt{k}. Ranks do better here, because each margin has exactly k exceedances of its rank threshold.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n, reps = 20_000, 200
ks = [25, 100, 400, 1600]
err = {k: [] for k in ks}
for _ in range(reps):
    r = 1 / rng.uniform(size=n)
    w = rng.beta(2, 2, size=n)
    v = r[:, None] * np.column_stack([2 * w, 2 - 2 * w])    # l(1, 1) = 1.375, no bias for n/k >= 2
    ranks = v.argsort(axis=0).argsort(axis=0) + 1
    for k in ks:
        l_hat = np.mean(np.any(ranks > n - k, axis=1)) * n / k
        err[k].append(l_hat - 1.375)
for k in ks:
    rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(err[k])))
    print(f"k = {k:>4}: RMSE {rmse:.4f}   sqrt(k) * RMSE {np.sqrt(k) * rmse:.3f}")
# k =   25: RMSE 0.0870   sqrt(k) * RMSE 0.435
# k =  100: RMSE 0.0397   sqrt(k) * RMSE 0.397
# k =  400: RMSE 0.0211   sqrt(k) * RMSE 0.422
# k = 1600: RMSE 0.0104   sqrt(k) * RMSE 0.415

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Tail estimators are rescaled empirical measures of rare sets; their error is a stochastic term plus a bias.
  • Classical VC bounds are useless in the tail; bounds that use the variance, at most the probability p of the rare region, give errors of order \sqrt{p V/n}.
  • On the tail scale this is \sqrt{V\log(1/\delta)/k}: learning in the tail behaves like learning from the k extremes alone.
  • Non-asymptotic bounds of this type hold for the empirical STDF and the empirical angular measure.

7. High-dimensional extremes: sparsity, PCA and anomaly detection

In dimension d, the angular measure lives on a (d-1)-dimensional sphere, and histograms need exponentially many cells. In applications, however, only a few groups of variables tend to be large together. This chapter exploits that sparsity to find structure in the extremes and to detect anomalies among them.

7.1 Notions of sparsity

Definition 7.1 — Subcones and faces For a non-empty \alpha \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}, let C_\alpha = \{x \in [0, \infty)^d : x_j > 0 \text{ for } j \in \alpha,\ x_j = 0 \text{ for } j \notin \alpha\}, \qquad \Omega_\alpha = C_\alpha \cap S . The 2^d - 1 cones partition [0, \infty)^d \setminus \{0\}, so \mu = \sum_\alpha \mu(\,\cdot \cap C_\alpha).

A cone with \mu(C_\alpha) > 0 is a group \alpha of variables that can be extreme together while the others stay of smaller order. The exponent measure is sparse when few cones carry mass: asymptotic independence puts mass on the d axes only, complete dependence on the full cone only.

Other notions of sparsity lead to other methods: sparse regular variation through Euclidean projections onto the simplex (Meyer and Wintenberger, 2021), conditional independence in graphical models for extremes (Engelke and Hitz, 2020), and concentration on low-dimensional subspaces (ch. 7.3). Engelke and Ivanovs (2021) review them.

7.2 The DAMEX algorithm

Data never lie exactly on a face: all coordinates are positive. The faces are therefore thickened by a tolerance \varepsilon, measured relative to the extreme threshold.

Algorithm 7.2 — DAMEX (Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon, 2016)

  1. Standardise by ranks: \hat V_i as in Definition 5.13.
  2. Keep the extremes: \|\hat V_i\|_\infty > n/k.
  3. Give each extreme its face \alpha_i = \{j : \hat V_i^j > \varepsilon\, n/k\}.
  4. Estimate the mass \hat M(\alpha) of each face by the proportion of extremes with \alpha_i = \alpha.
  5. Keep the faces whose mass exceeds a small threshold.

The cost is dominated by the ranks, O(dn\log n), and at most one face per extreme has positive empirical mass, however large d is. Non-asymptotic guarantees combine the bounds of ch. 6 with the error due to the thickening \varepsilon.

7.3 PCA and subspace clustering of extremes

A second notion of sparsity is linear: the extreme angles may lie close to a low-dimensional subspace.

Proposition 7.3 — Support of the angles and second moments Let \Theta be a random unit vector (Euclidean norm) with law \Phi/\Phi(S), and \Sigma = \mathbb{E}[\Theta\Theta^\top]. Then the range of \Sigma is the linear span of the support of \Theta.

Proof. For a vector u, u^\top\Sigma u = \mathbb{E}[(u^\top\Theta)^2] vanishes if and only if u is orthogonal to \Theta almost surely. So the kernel of \Sigma is the orthogonal complement of the support, and the range is its span.

The top eigenvectors of the empirical second-moment matrix \hat\Sigma of the angles \hat\Theta_i of the k largest standardised observations therefore estimate the subspace that carries the extremes. Drees and Sabourin (2021) give risk bounds for this PCA of extremes; Cooley and Thibaud (2019) propose a related decomposition of a tail pairwise dependence matrix.

After standardisation every coordinate is heavy-tailed, so a coordinate whose extremes are independent of the others puts mass on its own axis. The subspace must account for every coordinate.

Example 7.4 — PCA of extreme angles In dimension 10, the heavy-tailed part of the data lies in a plane spanned by two block vectors, and light-tailed noise is added to every coordinate. Among the 500 largest standardised observations, the second-moment matrix has eigenvalue shares 0.815, 0.184 and then 0.000; the estimated plane is within 0.2 degrees of the true one. The bulk of the data is full-rank: 21.5 % of its second moment lies outside its top two directions.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from scipy.linalg import subspace_angles

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
n, d, k = 50_000, 10, 500
u1 = np.r_[np.ones(5), np.zeros(5)] / np.sqrt(5)       # after standardisation every coordinate
u2 = np.r_[np.zeros(5), np.ones(5)] / np.sqrt(5)       # must be heavy-tailed: the plane spans all
phi = rng.uniform(0, np.pi / 2, size=n)
theta = np.cos(phi)[:, None] * u1 + np.sin(phi)[:, None] * u2
x = 3 * np.abs(rng.standard_normal((n, d))) + (1 / rng.uniform(size=n))[:, None] * theta

v = n / (n + 1 - stats.rankdata(x, axis=0))
norms = np.linalg.norm(v, axis=1)
plane = np.column_stack([u1, u2])
for name, sel in [("extremes", norms >= np.sort(norms)[-k]), ("bulk", norms <= np.median(norms))]:
    th = v[sel] / norms[sel, None]
    evals, evecs = np.linalg.eigh(th.T @ th / sel.sum())
    share = evals[::-1] / evals.sum()
    ang = np.degrees(subspace_angles(evecs[:, -2:], plane)).max()
    print(f"{name:>8}: eigenvalue shares {np.round(share[:3], 3)}, "
          f"mass beyond two {share[2:].sum():.3f}, top-2 plane vs truth {ang:.1f} degrees")
# extremes: eigenvalue shares [0.815 0.184 0.   ], mass beyond two 0.001, top-2 plane vs truth 0.2 degrees
#     bulk: eigenvalue shares [0.754 0.031 0.028], mass beyond two 0.215, top-2 plane vs truth 5.9 degrees

When the extremes concentrate on several faces or subspaces, one subspace is not enough: this is multiple subspace clustering. The angles of the extremes are first clustered, for example by spherical k-means (Janßen and Wan, 2020), and a PCA is then run within each cluster. The faces found by DAMEX give a natural initialisation.

7.4 Anomaly detection in extreme regions

General-purpose detectors, such as isolation forests or one-class support vector machines, estimate level sets of a density dominated by the bulk. In the tail they see few points and tend to flag every extreme. Extreme value theory separates the question in two: an extreme observation is normal if its direction is one that extremes commonly take, and anomalous otherwise.

Definition 7.5 — Face-mass anomaly score For an extreme observation with standardised value v and face \alpha(v), s(v) = \frac{\hat M(\alpha(v))}{\|v\|_\infty} , and small scores are the most abnormal: a large norm in a direction of small mass.

The DAMEX paper ranks extremes with a score of this kind, increasing in the mass of the face and decreasing in the norm; Definition 7.5 is the simplest such choice.

Thomas, Clémençon, Gramfort and Sabourin (2017) replace the faces by minimum-volume sets on the sphere: an empirical-risk-minimisation estimate of the smallest set of angular mass at least 1 - a, computed from the angles of the k extremes; extreme observations whose angle falls outside it are flagged. The bounds of ch. 6 control the mass and volume of the learnt set. Chiapino, Clémençon, Feuillard and Sabourin (2020) go further and cluster and visualise the anomalies found in extreme regions.

Example 7.6 — DAMEX on a monitoring-like data set In dimension 6, extremes occur on the faces \{1, 2\}, \{3, 4, 5\} and \{6\} (indices from 0 to 5 in the code), over a light-tailed bulk. Twenty anomalies are large on the unusual pair \{1, 6\}. With k = 500 and \varepsilon = 0.1, the three true faces carry 88 % of the 1802 extremes; every other face has less than 2 %. The score of Definition 7.5 separates the anomalies from the normal extremes with an area under the ROC curve of 0.990.

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n, d, k, eps = 50_000, 6, 500, 0.1
faces = [(0, 1), (2, 3, 4), (5,)]
x = rng.exponential(size=(n, d))                       # light-tailed bulk
lab = rng.choice(3, size=n, p=[0.4, 0.4, 0.2])
r = 1 / rng.uniform(size=n)                            # heavy-tailed radius
for f, face in enumerate(faces):
    idx = np.flatnonzero(lab == f)
    x[np.ix_(idx, face)] += r[idx, None] * rng.dirichlet(5 * np.ones(len(face)), size=idx.size)
n_an = 20                                              # anomalies: large on the unseen face {0, 5}
x[:n_an] = rng.exponential(size=(n_an, d))
x[:n_an, [0, 5]] += 1e3 * rng.uniform(1, 3, size=(n_an, 1)) * np.array([0.5, 0.5])

v = n / (n + 1 - stats.rankdata(x, axis=0))            # rank standardisation
ext = v.max(axis=1) > n / k                            # extreme region in sup-norm
alpha = [tuple(int(j) for j in np.flatnonzero(row > eps * n / k)) for row in v[ext]]
mass = Counter(alpha)
top = sorted(mass.items(), key=lambda t: -t[1])[:6]
print("largest face masses (share of extremes):", ", ".join(f"{a}: {c / ext.sum():.3f}" for a, c in top))

# face-mass score of Definition 7.5: (mass of the face) / (sup-norm); small = abnormal
score = np.array([mass[a] / k for a in alpha]) / v[ext].max(axis=1)
is_an = np.flatnonzero(ext) < n_an
auc = stats.mannwhitneyu(score[~is_an], score[is_an]).statistic / (is_an.sum() * (~is_an).sum())
print(f"{ext.sum()} extremes, {is_an.sum()} anomalies among them, AUC of the score = {auc:.3f}")
# largest face masses (share of extremes): (2, 3, 4): 0.361, (0, 1): 0.291, (5,): 0.226, (0, 5): 0.017, ...
# 1802 extremes, 20 anomalies among them, AUC of the score = 0.990

Key takeaways — chapter 7

  • The 2^d - 1 subcones split the exponent measure; sparsity means few groups of variables are extreme together.
  • DAMEX finds the charged faces from rank-standardised extremes with a thickening \varepsilon, at cost O(dn\log n).
  • The second-moment matrix of the extreme angles has the span of their support as range: PCA of extremes recovers a low-dimensional tail subspace; clustering the angles first handles several subspaces.
  • Anomalies in extreme regions are large observations in rare directions; face masses or minimum-volume sets on the sphere score them.

8. Supervised learning with extreme values

Supervised learning minimises an average risk, dominated by the bulk of the data. When the decisions that matter are taken in the extreme region, for the largest covariates or the largest responses, the bulk can mislead. This chapter adapts classification and dimension reduction to extremes.

8.1 Classification in extreme regions

Let (X, Y) \in \mathbb{R}^d \times \{-1, +1\}, with X heavy-tailed, and write \Theta = X/\|X\| for its angle.

Definition 8.1 — Risk in the extreme region For a classifier g and a threshold t, L_t(g) = P\big(g(X) \ne Y \,\big|\, \|X\| > t\big), \qquad L_\infty(g) = \limsup_{t \to \infty} L_t(g) .

Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin (2018) assume that, given each class, X is regularly varying with the same index. The law of (\|X\|/t, \Theta, Y) given \|X\| > t then converges to a limit law P_\infty under which the radius is Pareto and independent of (\Theta, Y).

Proposition 8.2 — Angular classifiers are enough Let \eta_\infty(\theta) = P_\infty(Y = 1 \mid \Theta = \theta). Among angular classifiers g(x) = h(x/\|x\|) whose decision boundary has P_\infty-probability zero, the asymptotic risk is minimised, when the set \{\eta_\infty = 1/2\} is also P_\infty-null, by g^*_\infty(x) = \mathrm{sign}\big(\eta_\infty(x/\|x\|) - 1/2\big) .

Proof. For such an h, L_t(h(\Theta)) converges to P_\infty(h(\Theta) \ne Y) by weak convergence, since the discontinuities of the indicator have probability zero in the limit. The limit is the risk of h for the pair (\Theta, Y) under P_\infty. Its minimiser is the Bayes classifier of that pair. ∎

Under their assumptions, Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin (2018) show that an optimal classifier for L_\infty can be taken angular. Learning therefore uses only the angles of the extremes: minimise the empirical risk over a VC class \mathcal G of angular classifiers, using the k observations with the largest norms.

Theorem 8.3 — Excess risk in the extreme region (Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin, 2018) Let \hat h_k minimise the empirical risk on the k largest observations over \mathcal G, of VC dimension V.

Let t_{n,k} be the (1 - k/n)-quantile of \|X\|. With probability at least 1 - \delta, the excess risk at that level, L_{t_{n,k}}(\hat h_k) - \inf_{h \in \mathcal G} L_{t_{n,k}}(h), is bounded by a term of order \sqrt{V\log(1/\delta)/k}. For the asymptotic risk L_\infty, a bias term L_{t_{n,k}} - L_\infty is added; it vanishes as n/k \to \infty.

The proof combines the rare-event inequalities of ch. 6 with the convergence of the conditional law: once again, the effective sample size is k.

Example 8.4 — A rule that flips in the tail In this synthetic model, the class decides the angle of X, but the rule is reversed beyond radius 5, where about 4 % of the data lie. Logistic regression fitted to all 20 000 training points learns the bulk rule and misclassifies 92.8 % of the test points in the extreme region. Empirical risk minimisation over angular threshold rules, on the 400 largest training points only, misclassifies 7.0 %.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

def sample(n):
    y = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=n)
    r = 1 / rng.uniform(size=n) ** 0.5                 # Pareto radius, alpha = 2
    up = (y == 1) == (r > 5)                           # the rule flips between bulk and tail
    phi = np.where(up, rng.uniform(0.25, 0.5, n), rng.uniform(0.0, 0.25, n)) * np.pi
    phi = np.clip(phi + 0.15 * rng.standard_normal(n), 0, np.pi / 2)
    return r[:, None] * np.column_stack([np.cos(phi), np.sin(phi)]), y

x, y = sample(20_000)
x_te, y_te = sample(200_000)
norm, norm_te = np.linalg.norm(x, axis=1), np.linalg.norm(x_te, axis=1)
k = 400
t = np.sort(norm)[-k]                                  # threshold: k-th largest training norm
ext_te = norm_te >= t

clf = LogisticRegression().fit(x, y)                   # fitted on all data
err_all = np.mean(clf.predict(x_te[ext_te]) != y_te[ext_te])

# ERM over angular threshold rules h(theta) = s * sign(phi - c) on the k largest points
phi_tr = np.arctan2(x[norm >= t, 1], x[norm >= t, 0])
grid = np.linspace(0, np.pi / 2, 181)
risk = [(np.mean(s * np.sign(phi_tr - c) != y[norm >= t]), c, s) for c in grid for s in (-1, 1)]
_, c, s = min(risk)
phi_te = np.arctan2(x_te[ext_te, 1], x_te[ext_te, 0])
err_ang = np.mean(s * np.sign(phi_te - c) != y_te[ext_te])
print(f"error on the extreme region (||x|| >= {t:.1f}, {ext_te.sum()} test points):")
print(f"  logistic regression on all data {err_all:.3f};  angular ERM on the k = {k} largest {err_ang:.3f}")
# error on the extreme region (||x|| >= 7.1, 3985 test points):
#   logistic regression on all data 0.928;  angular ERM on the k = 400 largest 0.070

The model is deliberately extreme, but the mechanism is general: a global fit weights the extreme region by its probability, here 2 %, and spends its capacity elsewhere.

8.2 Dimension reduction with extreme targets

Now let Y \in \mathbb{R} be a heavy-tailed response and X \in \mathbb{R}^p covariates. The goal is to find a few directions of X that explain whether Y is extreme.

Definition 8.5 — Tail sufficient directions A p \times q matrix B is tail sufficient if, for every t \ge t_0, the event \{Y > t\} is conditionally independent of X given B^\top X.

The classical sliced inverse regression of Li (1991) reverses the roles of X and Y: it looks at the mean of X given slices of Y. Its tail version looks only at the slice of the largest responses.

Proposition 8.6 — Tail inverse regression Let \Sigma = \mathrm{Cov}(X) be invertible, Z = \Sigma^{-1/2}(X - \mathbb{E}X), and B tail sufficient.

Assume the linearity condition: \mathbb{E}[X \mid B^\top X] is affine in B^\top X, as for Gaussian or elliptical X. Then, for t \ge t_0, \mathbb{E}[Z \mid Y > t] \in \mathrm{span}\big(\Sigma^{1/2} B\big) .

Proof. Write \bar X = X - \mathbb{E}X. Under the linearity condition, \mathbb{E}[\bar X \mid B^\top X] = P\bar X with P = \Sigma B (B^\top\Sigma B)^{-1} B^\top (Li, 1991). By the tower property and the conditional independence, \mathbb{E}[\bar X \mid Y > t] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[\bar X \mid B^\top X] \,\big|\, Y > t\big] = \mathbb{E}[P\bar X \mid Y > t] \in \mathrm{span}(\Sigma B) . Multiplying by \Sigma^{-1/2} gives the claim. ∎

The estimator whitens the covariates, averages them over the k largest responses, and maps the result back with \hat\Sigma^{-1/2}. Several tail slices or second moments give more than one direction. This tail inverse regression is studied by Aghbalou, Portier, Sabourin and Zhou (2024), with consistency as k \to \infty and k/n \to 0. A symmetric link makes the mean vanish (Exercise 13).

Example 8.7 — One direction out of ten With X standard Gaussian in dimension 10 and Y = (1 + e^{\beta^\top X})\,W, where W is Pareto with index 3, the event \{Y > t\} depends on X through \beta^\top X only.

Using the 200 largest of 20 000 responses, the estimated direction has cosine 0.996 with \beta.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
n, p, k = 20_000, 10, 200
beta = np.r_[1.0, -1.0, 0.5, np.zeros(p - 3)]
beta /= np.linalg.norm(beta)
x = rng.standard_normal((n, p))
w = 1 / rng.uniform(size=n) ** (1 / 3)                 # Pareto noise, tail index 3
y = (1 + np.exp(x @ beta)) * w                         # P(Y > t | X) depends on beta'X only

L = np.linalg.cholesky(np.cov(x.T))                    # Sigma_hat = L L'
z = np.linalg.solve(L, (x - x.mean(axis=0)).T).T       # whitened covariates
top = np.argsort(y)[-k:]                               # the k largest responses
m_hat = z[top].mean(axis=0)                            # tail inverse regression mean E[Z | Y > t]
b_hat = np.linalg.solve(L.T, m_hat)                    # back to the x scale: beta ~ L'^{-1} m
b_hat /= np.linalg.norm(b_hat)
print(f"|cos(b_hat, beta)| = {abs(b_hat @ beta):.3f}")
print("b_hat =", np.round(b_hat, 2))
# |cos(b_hat, beta)| = 0.996
# b_hat = [ 0.7  -0.64  0.32 -0.04 -0.02  0.01 -0.01 -0.02  0.06  0.01]

The code whitens with a Cholesky factor L instead of \hat\Sigma^{1/2}; the span is mapped back with (L^\top)^{-1}, which is equivalent.

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • The risk that matters may be the conditional risk given a large norm; the bulk can impose the wrong rule there.
  • Under regular variation, angular classifiers suffice asymptotically; empirical risk minimisation on the k largest observations has excess risk of order \sqrt{V/k}.
  • For extreme responses, the mean of the whitened covariates given Y > t lies in the tail sufficient subspace under the linearity condition: tail inverse regression estimates it from the k largest responses.

9. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study, about four hours, and serves as the reading list of the seminar session. Each section names a direction, the work that opened it and a few questions that are still open. The official references of the course are marked in the References.

9.1 Graphical models for extremes

The exponent measure of ch. 5 lives on a space that is not a product space, so the usual notion of conditional independence does not apply to it directly. Engelke and Hitz (2020) define conditional independence for multivariate Pareto distributions and show that, for the Hüsler–Reiss family (Hüsler and Reiss, 1989), the graph is encoded by zeros of a precision-type matrix built from the variogram. Hitz and Evans (2016) study a related one-component approach to regular variation and graphical modelling.

For tree-structured dependence, Engelke and Volgushev (2022) learn the tree from data with minimum spanning trees built on empirical extremal dependence. Open questions include consistent structure learning beyond trees in growing dimension, and graphical models when some pairs of variables are asymptotically independent.

9.2 Sparsity and dimension reduction

The review of Engelke and Ivanovs (2021) organises the field around sparse structures: faces of the simplex (Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon, 2016), sparse regular variation (Meyer and Wintenberger, 2021), low-rank structures (Cooley and Thibaud, 2019; Drees and Sabourin, 2021), clustering (Janßen and Wan, 2020; Chiapino, Clémençon, Feuillard and Sabourin, 2020) and graphical models.

Open questions:

  • choosing the tolerance \varepsilon and the number k of extremes jointly, with guarantees, instead of by eye (Exercise 14);
  • support recovery when the dimension grows with n, where the number of candidate faces is exponential;
  • the relation between face sparsity, which is combinatorial, and subspace sparsity, which is linear.

9.3 Non-asymptotic theory

The bounds of ch. 6 come from Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2015), Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2022) and Clémençon, Jalalzai, Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2023). They are mostly upper bounds.

In fixed dimension, Drees and Huang (1998) give best attainable rates for estimators of the stable tail dependence function. Matching lower bounds in high dimension, that is minimax rates for the estimation of the angular measure, are largely open, as is a data-driven choice of k with non-asymptotic guarantees. Most results also assume independent observations; extremes of time series cluster in time, which changes the effective number of extremes.

9.4 Regular variation in general spaces

Hult and Lindskog (2006) define regular variation for measures on metric spaces, which covers random functions such as daily temperature curves or trajectories. Learning algorithms for such functional extremes (dimension reduction, anomaly detection, classification) need the concentration tools of ch. 6 in infinite dimension, where the choice of the norm itself becomes part of the model.

9.5 Supervised and generative learning

Classification in extreme regions (Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin, 2018) and tail inverse regression (Aghbalou, Portier, Sabourin and Zhou, 2024) are first steps. Model selection is a further difficulty: cross-validation estimates a risk on the extreme region from very few points, and its guarantees must again scale with k.

Generative models raise a structural question. If a generator G is L-Lipschitz and its latent noise Z is Gaussian, Gaussian concentration gives P\big(\|G(Z) - \mathbb{E}G(Z)\| > s\big) \le 2\,e^{-s^2/(2L^2)} up to dimension-dependent constants: such a model cannot produce heavy tails at all. How to build generators that reproduce a given index \gamma and a given angular measure is an active question.

9.6 Asymptotic independence and hidden regular variation

When \chi = 0 (Proposition 5.12), the exponent measure is concentrated on the axes and says nothing about joint extremes, which still occur. Ledford and Tawn (1996) model the joint tail through a coefficient of tail dependence \eta \in (0, 1]: P\big(F_1(X^1) > 1 - s,\ F_2(X^2) > 1 - s\big) = s^{1/\eta} L(1/s), \qquad L \text{ slowly varying} . For the Gaussian law with correlation \rho, \eta = (1 + \rho)/2. Resnick (2002) develops hidden regular variation, a second exponent measure on a smaller cone. Statistical learning in this regime, with even fewer effective observations, is largely unexplored.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Graphical models, sparse structures and low-rank structures are three ways to make high-dimensional tails learnable; their joint theory is open.
  • Non-asymptotic bounds exist as upper bounds; minimax rates, data-driven choices of k and dependent data remain open.
  • Functional extremes, model selection in the tail and heavy-tailed generative models are active questions.

  5. ★ Regularly varying or not. (ch. 3) Which of these functions are regularly varying, and with which index: \log(1 + x), \exp(\sqrt{\log x}), 2 + \sin x, \exp(\sin \log x), x^{-1}\log x?

  6. ★ Hill for Pareto data. (ch. 4) For exact Pareto data with k = 100, the Hill estimator equals 0.52. Give an exact 95 % confidence interval for \gamma using Proposition 4.2, and compare with the normal approximation.

  7. ★ The logistic model. (ch. 5) For the bivariate logistic STDF with \beta = 1/2, compute the extremal coefficient, \chi, and P(Z \le (1, 1)) for the simple max-stable vector Z; compare with independence. Check the bounds of Proposition 5.8.

  8. ★★ From POT to annual return levels. (ch. 2) A river exceeds u = 20 on average \lambda = 5 times a year, with GPD excesses of parameters \sigma = 3, \gamma = 0.1. Find the GEV law of the annual maximum and its 100-year return level, then check the result directly from the Poisson–GPD model.

  9. ★★ Tail moments by Karamata. (ch. 3) Let 1 - F \in RV_{-\alpha} and 0 < \beta < \alpha. Show that \mathbb{E}\big[X^\beta \mathbb{1}\{X > t\}\big] \sim \frac{\alpha}{\alpha - \beta}\, t^\beta\, (1 - F(t)), and that \mathbb{E}X_+^\beta = \infty for \beta > \alpha.

    Solution. Integrate by parts: the expectation equals t^\beta(1 - F(t)) + \int_t^\infty \beta u^{\beta - 1}(1 - F(u))\,du .

    The integrand is in RV_{\beta - 1 - \alpha} with index below -1, so Proposition 3.12 makes the integral equivalent to \beta t^\beta(1 - F(t))/(\alpha - \beta). Adding the two terms gives the factor \alpha/(\alpha - \beta).

    For \beta > \alpha, the Potter lower bound makes the integrand at least of order u^{\beta - 1 - \alpha - \delta}, whose exponent exceeds -1 for small \delta: the integral diverges.

  10. ★★ The Gaussian law is asymptotically independent. (ch. 5) Let (X^1, X^2) be standard Gaussian with correlation \rho \in (-1, 1). Show that \chi = 0.

    Solution. Since both margins are \Phi, \chi = \lim_u P(X^1 > u, X^2 > u)/\bar\Phi(u).

    The joint event implies X^1 + X^2 > 2u, and X^1 + X^2 is centred Gaussian with variance 2 + 2\rho. Hence the ratio is at most \bar\Phi(cu)/\bar\Phi(u) with c = \sqrt{2/(1 + \rho)} > 1.

    By the Mills ratio \bar\Phi(u) \sim \varphi(u)/u, this tends to 0 like e^{-(c^2 - 1)u^2/2}.

    • Success criterion: the true faces are recovered in at least 90 % of 50 repetitions for a stated (n, k, \varepsilon); ROC curves on the extreme region against the baseline, reported honestly.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Concentration of rare frequencies. Do uniform deviations over a VC class of rare sets really scale like \sqrt{p/n}? A paper study of Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2015) and Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2022).
    • Implement: the exact supremum of |P_n - P| over the class of complements of rectangles [0, x] with x beyond a high quantile, in dimension 2. Use two data models with known P: independent standard Pareto coordinates, and the Beta model of Example 5.14.
    • Prove: Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 in full, in the report.
    • Deliverables: code, 4–6 page report, 10-minute talk. Chapters: ch. 0, 6.
    • Success criterion: log–log slopes of the mean supremum equal to 1/2 in p and -1/2 in n within \pm 0.1, compared with the slope of the classical VC bound.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Binary classification in extreme regions. Do classifiers trained on the angles of the largest observations beat global classifiers on the extreme region, as theory predicts? A paper study of Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin (2018).
    • Implement: angular empirical risk minimisation with logistic regression and random forests on the angles of the k largest points, against the same classifiers trained on all data.
    • Data: the synthetic models of the paper and of Example 8.4, then one public data set with heavy-tailed features, chosen by the group.
    • Split: member A writes the simulation and the standardisation; member B the classifiers and the evaluation on the extreme region; member C the theory (Theorem 8.3) and the bias study of Exercise 15.
    • Deliverables: code, 6–8 page report, 20-minute talk at the seminar. Chapters: ch. 6, 8.
    • Success criterion: on the model of Example 8.4, an angular error below 0.10 on the extreme region against more than 0.5 for global logistic regression, with error bars over 20 repetitions. Also required: a figure of the error against k, and an explanation of why a global random forest, a local method, does far better than global logistic regression there.
  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Anomaly detection with minimum-volume sets on the sphere. How do angular minimum-volume sets compare with face masses and with general detectors? A paper study of Thomas, Clémençon, Gramfort and Sabourin (2017).
    • Implement: empirical minimum-volume sets on the sphere over unions of cells of a partition, learnt from the angles of the extremes; the face-mass score of Definition 7.5; an isolation forest.
    • Data: synthetic data in dimension 3 to 10 with planted anomalies in rare directions.
    • Split: member A writes the minimum-volume-set estimator; member B the face-mass score and the baselines; member C the evaluation protocol and the guarantees on mass and volume (ch. 6).
    • Deliverables: code, 6–8 page report, 20-minute talk at the seminar. Chapters: ch. 5, 6, 7.
    • Success criterion: the test mass of the learnt set within \pm 0.02 of its target; precision–recall curves on the extreme region for the three methods.
  3. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] PCA and clustering of extremes. Can several low-dimensional tail subspaces be recovered from the extremes alone? A paper study of Drees and Sabourin (2021) and Janßen and Wan (2020).
    • Implement: PCA of the extreme angles with a choice of k; spherical k-means on the extreme angles followed by PCA within clusters.
    • Data: synthetic mixtures of two or three planes as in Example 7.4, then a public multivariate series chosen by the group (daily returns of several indices, or river flows).
    • Split: member A writes PCA and its risk as a function of k; member B the clustering; member C the real data and the visualisation.
    • Deliverables: code, 6–8 page report, 20-minute talk at the seminar. Chapters: ch. 5, 7.
    • Success criterion: planted subspaces recovered with principal angles below 5 degrees at n = 50\,000, k = 500; a stated criterion for the number of clusters.

Written exam (proposal)

Two hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, a basic calculator allowed; four independent questions.

  1. Question (5 points). Define the maximum domain of attraction and max-stability. Show that the GEV law G_\gamma is max-stable, state the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem, and give norming constants and limits for the exponential and Pareto laws.

    Expected: Definitions 1.2–1.3 and the identity \big(1 + \gamma(n^\gamma x + (n^\gamma - 1)/\gamma)\big)^{-1/\gamma} = n^{-1}(1 + \gamma x)^{-1/\gamma}, multiplied by n inside the exponential. Theorem 1.5 with its three types. Example 1.7: (1, \log n) and Gumbel; (\gamma n^\gamma, n^\gamma) or (n^{1/\alpha}, 0) and Fréchet.

  2. Question (5 points). Out of n = 20\,000 daily observations, k = 400 exceed u = 10, and the GPD fit gives \hat\sigma = 2, \hat\gamma = 0.2.

    1. Estimate P(X > 20). (b) Estimate the level exceeded with probability 10^{-5}. (c) Give the model’s mean excess over 15, and say how a mean-excess plot would check the threshold.

    Expected: (a) the tail model of ch. 2.4 gives \frac{k}{n}\Big(1 + \frac{0.2 \times 10}{2}\Big)^{-5} = \frac{0.02}{32} = 6.25 \times 10^{-4} .

    1. 10 + 10\,(2000^{0.2} - 1) = 45.7. (c) Proposition 2.3 with v = 5: (2 + 0.2 \times 5)/0.8 = 3.75; above a good threshold the empirical mean excess is roughly linear with slope \gamma/(1 - \gamma) = 0.25.

  3. Question (6 points). (a) For exact Pareto data with index \alpha, prove that k\alpha H_{k,n} follows the Gamma law with shape k.

    1. Deduce the mean and variance of H_{k,n}. (c) For a general regularly varying tail, explain in a few lines why H_{k,n} is biased and how k should be chosen.

    Expected: (a) the exponential transform, the conditional law of the top k given the (k+1)-th and the memoryless property (Proposition 4.2).

    1. 1/\alpha and 1/(k\alpha^2). (c) The second-order term (Theorem 4.4): variance \gamma^2/k against a bias growing with k; a Hill plot or the rate n^{2|\rho|/(1 + 2|\rho|)}.

  4. Question (4 points). In dimension 2, let the angular measure (L^1 norm) be twice the law of (W, 1 - W) with W uniform on [0, 1].

    Compute \ell(1, 1) and \chi, and check the bounds of Proposition 5.8. Write the rank-based estimator \hat\ell_k(1, 1) and give the order of its stochastic error.

    Expected: \ell(1, 1) = 2\,\mathbb{E}\max(W, 1 - W) = 2 \times 3/4 = 1.5 and \chi = 0.5; 1 \le 1.5 \le 2. Definition 5.13; error of order 1/\sqrt{k} by the Bernstein argument of ch. 6.

Total: 5 + 5 + 6 + 4 = 20 points.
