0. Review: tails, order statistics and the three laws

Later chapters normalise a maximum and pass to the limit. This chapter fixes the vocabulary those arguments use, and it names the only three laws that can appear.

A tail probability is the chance of exceeding a high level. The survival function is one minus the distribution function. A heavy tail decays as a power, as with a Pareto law. A light tail, such as the exponential or the normal, decays faster.

The sample maximum carries the tail. From an independent sample of size n, write M_n for the largest value. Its distribution function is the original one raised to that power.

Plotting the raw maximum is a poor summary, because it drifts as the sample grows. The object that can converge is a shifted and rescaled copy.

Definition 0.1 — Extreme-value distribution

A random variable Z has an extreme-value distribution with shape \xi when its distribution function belongs to the family below. The case \xi = 0 is the limit of the other cases.

G_\xi(z) = \exp\bigl(-(1+\xi z)^{-1/\xi}\bigr), \qquad 1+\xi z > 0,

and G_0(z) = \exp(-e^{-z}) for every real z. A location \mu and a scale \sigma > 0 give the law of \mu + \sigma Z.

A positive shape is Fréchet, with a heavy tail. A negative shape is Weibull, with a finite upper endpoint. A zero shape is Gumbel, the limit for exponential and normal maxima. Exercise 1 computes one Weibull endpoint.

Proposition 0.2 — Exponential maxima

Let E_1, \ldots, E_m be independent standard exponential random variables, and let M be their maximum. Then for every real x,

\mathbb{P}(M - \log m \le x) = \bigl(1 - e^{-x}/m\bigr)^m .

The right-hand side increases to G_0(x) as m \to \infty.

Proof. The survival function of a standard exponential is e^{-y} on the positive line. Independence raises the distribution function to the power m. Substituting the level \log m + x produces the displayed identity. The elementary limit (1 - a/m)^m \to e^{-a}, with a = e^{-x}, is the Gumbel distribution function. ∎

The convergence is real, and it is not an equality for finite m. At x = 0 and m = 20, the exact probability is 0.3585, while G_0(0) = e^{-1} = 0.3679. The gap shrinks as the block grows. Exercise 2 estimates the mean of the leftover M - \log m on blocks of size 50.

Example 0.3 — A numerical block

Take m = 50. Then \log 50 = 3.912. Proposition 0.2 says that the centred maximum is already close to a standard Gumbel random variable. The Gumbel mean is Euler’s constant 0.577. A Monte Carlo mean computed from 400 independent blocks, with the seed fixed in Exercise 2, equals 0.6027. The difference of about 0.026 is ordinary sampling noise at this number of blocks.

The formula to remember is the one-parameter family G_\xi. Every limit theorem in ch. 1 and ch. 2 ends at a member of this family, or at the Pareto excess that shares the same shape \xi.

Max-stability is the property behind the family. The maximum of several independent copies, after a fresh shift and scale, has the same law. A non-degenerate limit of normalised maxima is therefore an extreme-value law. ch. 1 proves that claim and computes the classical constants.

Key takeaways — chapter 0.

  • The survival function 1-F decides whether the tail is a power or lighter.
  • The raw maximum M_n drifts with n. A normalised copy is what can converge.
  • The only shapes are Fréchet, Gumbel and Weibull, written as one family G_\xi.
  • Exponential maxima, once centred by \log m, converge to the Gumbel law. They do not equal it at finite m.

1. Block maxima and the Fisher–Tippett theorem

Risk systems and anomaly detectors rarely model every observation. They model the largest value in a block, a year of losses or an hour of sensor readings. This chapter gives the limit law of that block maximum and the classical way to fit it.

The announcement names Fisher and Tippett. Gnedenko completed the classification, so the result is the Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem. The argument below keeps the three steps called elements of a proof.

Definition 1.1 — Maximum domain of attraction

A distribution function lies in the maximum domain of attraction of G_\xi when normalised maxima converge to that law. The norming uses positive scales a_n and shifts b_n.

The normalised maximum is (M_n - b_n)/a_n. Those two sequences are the norming constants.

Theorem 1.2 — Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko

Suppose (M_n - b_n)/a_n converges in distribution to a non-degenerate limit H. Then H is an extreme-value distribution. Its shape \xi is unique, and F lies in the maximum domain of attraction of G_\xi.

Proof. A sample of size nk splits into k blocks of equal length. The maximum of the big sample is the maximum of the block maxima. Passing to the limit, the limit law is max-stable.

A max-stable distribution function solves a functional equation. For each block count there is an affine map such that the distribution function, composed with that map and raised to the matching power, recovers itself.

The solutions of that equation are exactly the three types in Definition 0.1. The shape \xi labels which solution appears. Degenerate limits are excluded by the assumption on H, so a non-degenerate limit has to be G_\xi up to location and scale. ∎

The theorem does not hand over the constants. They come from the tail of F, and three textbook cases cover the applications in this course.

For the standard exponential the scale is 1 and the shift is \log n, with a Gumbel limit. For the uniform law on the unit interval the scale is 1/n and the shift is 1, with Weibull shape -1.

For a Pareto tail of index \alpha > 0, the survival beyond 1 decays as that power. The scale grows like n to the power 1/\alpha, and the shift may be taken as zero. The limit is Fréchet, with shape equal to the reciprocal of the index.

Exercise 3 computes the three pairs of constants at sample size 100.

Example 1.3 — A return level

Suppose a fitted block maximum has location 2, scale 1 and shape 0.2. The level exceeded with probability 0.01 is the point where the fitted distribution function equals 0.99.

With y = -\log 0.99 = 0.01005,

z = \mu + \frac{\sigma}{\xi}\bigl(y^{-\xi} - 1\bigr) = 9.5468 .

So a one-in-a-hundred block is estimated at about 9.55 on the scale of the data. Exercise 4 recomputes this figure from the formula.

The method of block maxima turns Theorem 1.2 into a procedure. Split the sample into blocks of equal length. Keep one maximum per block, and fit location, scale and shape by maximum likelihood. The theorem treats those maxima as approximately generalized extreme-value. Return levels then follow Example 1.3.

The block length trades bias against variance. A short block misses the limit. A long block yields few maxima. Keep lengths where the estimated shape is stable. That check is the announced tutorial.

Key takeaways — chapter 1.

  • A non-degenerate limit of normalised maxima is a generalized extreme-value law.
  • Exponential and normal tails lead to a Gumbel limit. Pareto tails lead to Fréchet. A finite endpoint leads to Weibull.
  • Block maxima turns the theorem into a likelihood on m block maxima.
  • The block length trades limit bias against the number of maxima. Stability of \xi is the practical guide.

2. Peaks over threshold and regular variation

Block maxima keep one number per block and discard the other large observations. Peaks over threshold keep every observation above a high level. This chapter shows that the excess over that level has a generalized Pareto limit, with the same shape \xi as the block maximum.

Definition 2.1 — Generalized Pareto distribution

The excess Y follows a generalized Pareto law with shape \xi and scale \sigma > 0 when its distribution function is, for y > 0 in the support,

H_{\xi,\sigma}(y) = 1 - \bigl(1 + \xi y/\sigma\bigr)^{-1/\xi},

and H_{0,\sigma}(y) = 1 - \exp(-y/\sigma). The support stops at -\sigma/\xi when \xi < 0.

Theorem 2.2 — Balkema–de Haan–Pickands

Suppose the distribution lies in the maximum domain of attraction of G_\xi. Then the excess over a high threshold u, divided by a scale \sigma(u), converges to the generalized Pareto law of the same shape.

The limit is conditional on exceeding u, and the threshold rises to the upper endpoint.

Proof. The exponential case is exact, and it shows the mechanism. If X is standard exponential, memorylessness says that X - u given X > u is again standard exponential, for every u. That is the generalized Pareto law with \xi = 0 and \sigma = 1, with no approximation.

For a general distribution in a maximum domain of attraction, the same norming aligns the excess with an exponential tail. Balkema and de Haan (1974) and Pickands (1975) complete the argument. ∎

The link with block maxima is the shared shape. If block maxima converge to a generalized extreme-value law, excesses converge to the generalized Pareto law with that same shape. Peaks over threshold keep several large points per block. The cost is the threshold, which plays the role of the block length in ch. 1.

A useful diagnostic is the mean excess. For a generalized Pareto law with shape below 1, the mean excess is linear in the level. The slope is the shape divided by one minus the shape. Exercise 5 derives the intercept and the slope.

Regular variation is the clean description of the Fréchet case \xi > 0.

Definition 2.3 — Regular variation

A survival function is regularly varying with index -\alpha, for some \alpha > 0, when

\frac{1-F(tx)}{1-F(t)} \to x^{-\alpha} \qquad \text{as } t \to \infty,

for every x > 0. Then \xi = 1/\alpha. The function L(t) = t^{\alpha}(1-F(t)) is slowly varying.

Exact Pareto laws are the model case. Student and Cauchy tails are regularly varying as well. Exponential and normal tails are not, because their survival ratios are not powers.

Vague convergence is the process form of that limit. Scaled exceedances converge to a Poisson random measure. Resnick (2008) is the reference, and it is why separated exceedances are treated as almost independent.

The Hill estimator is the first statistical use of Definition 2.3. Order the sample X_{(1)} \le \cdots \le X_{(n)} and keep the k largest observations. The Hill statistic is the average log-excess over the next order statistic,

\hat\xi_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \log X_{(n-i+1)} - \log X_{(n-k)} .

For exact Pareto data, \hat\xi_k estimates 1/\alpha. ch. 3 states the consistency conditions and Exercise 6 computes one sample. At this stage the formula is only a device. It averages multiplicative spacings, and regular variation says those spacings stabilise.

Choosing how many upper points to keep repeats the block-length compromise. A small upper count is close to the limit and noisy. A large count can leave the Pareto regime. The mean-excess plot and a Hill plot are the pictures the tutorial asks for. One looks for a plateau.

Key takeaways — chapter 2.

  • Excesses over a high threshold converge to a generalized Pareto law with the same shape as the block maxima.
  • For the exponential law the excess limit is exact, by memorylessness.
  • Regular variation is the power-tail assumption \xi > 0. It produces a Poisson limit for the exceedances.
  • The Hill statistic averages log-excesses of the k largest points. The threshold is chosen where that average stabilises.

3. Karamata’s theorem, the Hill estimator and quantiles

Karamata’s representation is what turns regular variation into a consistent Hill estimator and into a quantile beyond the sample.

Theorem 3.1 — Karamata representation

A positive measurable function f is regularly varying with index \rho if and only if it can be written

f(x) = c(x)\,\exp\Bigl(\int_1^x \frac{\rho + \varepsilon(s)}{s}\,ds\Bigr),

where c(x) tends to a positive finite limit and \varepsilon(s) tends to 0 as s \to \infty.

Proof. If the display holds, the integral of the small error over a far window vanishes. The ratio of the function at a scaled argument and at the base argument tends to a pure power.

Conversely, pass to logarithmic coordinates. The logarithmic derivative settles at the index, and integrating it produces an error that tends to 0. de Haan and Ferreira (2007) treat the measurable case. The step used below is the split into a power and a slowly varying factor. ∎

The Hill estimator reads the index off the upper order statistics. On exact Pareto data the log-spacings are exponential, so their average is consistent once the upper count grows.

A slowly varying error is why the upper count must also be a vanishing fraction of the sample. Otherwise the window has not entered the pure power regime, and the bias stays.

Proposition 3.2 — Weak consistency of the Hill estimator

Let the observations be independent with a regularly varying survival of index -\alpha. If the upper count k tends to infinity and k/n tends to 0, then the Hill statistic converges in probability to 1/\alpha.

Proof. On exact Pareto data the slowly varying factor is constant. The upper log-spacings are exponential with mean equal to the reciprocal of the index, so their average converges once the upper count grows.

In general, Theorem 3.1 multiplies the Pareto tail by a slowly varying factor. That factor is almost constant on the window of the largest points, because the window is pushed to infinity. The extra fluctuation tends to 0 in probability. ∎

Quantile estimation is the reason the index is worth having. Let p be a small probability, smaller than k/n, so the desired quantile lies beyond the bulk of the sample. Weissman (1978) extrapolates the last used order statistic by the fitted power,

\hat q(p) = X_{(n-k)}\, \bigl(k/(n p)\bigr)^{\hat\xi_k} .

The formula rewrites the Pareto tail at the random threshold given by an upper order statistic. Consistency follows from Proposition 3.2 when the target probability does not vanish too fast relative to the upper fraction. The course announcement lists this extrapolation next to the Hill estimator.

Example 3.3 — One Weissman step

Suppose the threshold used by the estimator is the upper order statistic 10, the upper fraction equals 0.05, and the fitted shape is the true Pareto value 1/2. The level with probability 0.001 is

\hat q(0.001) = 10 \times (0.05/0.001)^{1/2} = 10 \times \sqrt{50} = 70.71 .

The sample itself need not contain any observation near 70. The power \xi carries the threshold out to that level. If the fitted shape were the Hill value from a finite sample, the same arithmetic would use that value in place of 1/2.

Weak convergence of the tail measure is the process form of Definition 2.3. After scaling, the empirical measure of large points converges to a measure with density proportional to x^{-\alpha-1}. On a log scale the largest points settle onto a straight line. A bend means the upper count is still too large.

Exercise 6 draws 4000 exact Pareto variables with index 2, so the Hill target is 1/2, and uses 200 upper points. The printed value is one draw. Proposition 3.2 sends that draw toward the target as the design grows.

Key takeaways — chapter 3.

  • Karamata’s representation splits a regularly varying function into a power and a slowly varying factor.
  • The Hill estimator is consistent when k \to \infty and k/n \to 0.
  • The Weissman formula pushes a high order statistic further into the tail by the fitted power.
  • A log-scale plot of the largest points is the visual check that the power regime has been reached.

4. Multivariate extremes and the angular measure

Real series are vectors. A flood can be extreme in one river, or in several rivers at once. A sensor network can spike in one channel or in a subset of channels. This chapter reduces those vectors to standard margins and puts all of the dependence into one measure on a simplex.

The reduction starts with the margins. Transform each coordinate, by its own tail, to a unit Fréchet variable. The distribution function of each margin is then \exp(-1/z) on the positive line. After that transform, a non-degenerate limit of componentwise maxima has a standard form.

Definition 4.1 — Simple max-stable law

A random vector Z in the positive orthant, with unit Fréchet margins, is simple max-stable when

\mathbb{P}(Z \le z) = \exp\bigl(-V(z)\bigr),

where the exponent function V is homogeneous of order -1. Homogeneity means V(tz) = t^{-1} V(z) for every t > 0.

Theorem 4.2 — Exponent measure and angular measure

For a simple max-stable law there is a finite measure H on the simplex S = \{w \ge 0 : \sum_j w_j = 1\} such that

V(z) = \int_S \max_j (w_j / z_j)\, H(dw) .

The margins fix the total mass. Each coordinate satisfies \int_S w_j\, H(dw) = 1.

Proof. Homogeneity of the exponent lets one pass to polar coordinates, a radius times an angle on the simplex. Integrating out the radius leaves a finite measure H on that simplex.

The integrand in the display is the radius at which a ray exits the rectangle under study. The margin constraints are the Fréchet means of the coordinates. Resnick (2008) gives the construction. ∎

The angular measure H carries the dependence. Mass on the vertices means extremes happen in one coordinate at a time. Mass at the centre means all coordinates are large together. Mass on a face means one subset of coordinates is extreme while the others stay moderate. ch. 6 reads that face pattern as sparsity.

Max-infinitely divisible laws replace the exponent with \exp(-\mu) for a measure that need not come from identically distributed maxima. Every max-stable law is max-infinitely divisible. The working model here is the simple max-stable law, so the angular measure stays a single finite measure on the simplex.

Multivariate peaks over threshold keep the points with large radius and collects their angles. Those angles estimate H, up to threshold bias. A histogram or a kernel is readable in moderate dimension. It stops being readable when the dimension grows, which is the subject of ch. 6.

Example 4.3 — Logistic model

The symmetric logistic model in dimension 2, with parameter \alpha \in (0, 1], has exponent

V(z_1, z_2) = \bigl(z_1^{-1/\alpha} + z_2^{-1/\alpha}\bigr)^{\alpha} .

On the diagonal the exponent equals 2^{\alpha}/z. The extremal coefficient is \theta = 2^{\alpha}. It equals 2 under independence and falls toward 1 under complete dependence. At \alpha = 1/2 one gets \sqrt{2} = 1.414. Exercise 7 checks these values.

Simulation draws an angle from H and a radius from the Fréchet intensity. Beirlant, Goegebeur, Segers and Teugels (2004) list logistic algorithms that skip an explicit draw from H. The tutorial compares the histogram of simulated angles with the known measure.

The stable tail dependence function is a third view of the same dependence, next to V and H. Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2015) give finite-sample bounds for it. Those bounds are ch. 5.

Key takeaways — chapter 4.

  • Marginal transforms remove the marginal tails. Dependence sits in the exponent function V.
  • Homogeneity of V produces an angular measure H on the simplex.
  • Mass of H on vertices, on faces or at the centre is asymptotic independence, subset dependence or full dependence.
  • Multivariate peaks over threshold estimates H from the angles of the largest radii.

5. Learning guarantees for rare events

The estimators of ch. 3 and ch. 4 are averages over k extreme points, not over n points. A textbook Vapnik bound written with sample size n describes the bulk. It can be smaller than the tail probability itself, and then it says nothing about the conditional law above the threshold. This chapter rewrites the bound on the extreme subsample.

Consider one set on the simplex. Among the retained angles, let \hat p(A) be the fraction that fall in A, and let p(A) be the angular mass of that set. Conditionally on the retained count, this is an ordinary binomial problem.

Proposition 5.1 — One-set concentration

Hoeffding’s inequality gives, for every t > 0,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(|\hat p(A) - p(A)| \ge t\bigr) \le 2\exp(-2 k t^2) .

The probability is conditional on the retained extremes. The choice t = \sqrt{\log(40)/(2k)} makes the right-hand side equal to 0.05.

Proof. Given the retained sample, the indicators that the angles fall in A are independent Bernoulli random variables if one takes the angular draws as independent, which is the Poisson picture of ch. 2 in the limit. Hoeffding’s inequality applies to the average of k bounded independent variables. Solving 2\exp(-2kt^2) = 0.05 gives the displayed t. ∎

Example 5.2 — The width at k = 400

With 400 retained extremes and confidence 0.05, Proposition 5.1 gives a half-width of 0.0679. One frequency is known to about seven percentage points.

The same arithmetic on a full sample of size 10000, with tail probability 0.01 and no conditioning, gives a half-width of 0.0136. That width exceeds the tail probability, so the unconditional bound cannot certify a relative error. Conditioning on the extremes is what makes the scale usable. Exercise 8 repeats the comparison.

A class of sets is harder than one set. A union bound over a histogram replaces the single-set logarithm by a logarithm that grows with the number of bins. For a class of Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension v, the uniform deviation is of order \sqrt{v/k}. Five degrees of freedom at 400 extremes give the rough scale 0.112.

This is the Vapnik-type bound in the announcement. The rare-event step is to use the retained count in place of the full sample size. Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2022) prove uniform bounds of this kind when the count is an order statistic, which is the peaks-over-threshold threshold.

Two tasks use the bound. The stable tail dependence function is a grid of quadrant frequencies, treated in Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2015). A histogram of the angular measure is a vector of bin frequencies. Its error vanishes when the retained count grows and the bins do not multiply too fast. The sample size in that statement is still the retained count, which is why ch. 6 looks for sparsity before estimating a density.

Key takeaways — chapter 5.

  • Tail estimators see k points. Bounds written with n describe the bulk.
  • Conditionally on the retained extremes, a frequency obeys a Hoeffding bound of order 1/\sqrt{k}.
  • At k = 400 and confidence 0.05, one frequency has half-width 0.0679.
  • Uniformly over a class of sets, the scale picks up the complexity v. Published inequalities of this type are in Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2022) and in Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2015).

6. High-dimensional extremes and anomaly detection

In dimension 10 the simplex is nine-dimensional. A histogram that worked for the logistic model of ch. 4 is empty in most bins before the bias is small. This session replaces that histogram by a sparsity assumption. Only a few faces carry angular mass.

A face is a non-empty subset of coordinates, the ones allowed to be large together. There are 2^d - 1 faces. In dimension 10 that is 1023 numbers, a finite list. The list grows with dimension, not with a bandwidth.

Definition 6.1 — Face support

The angular measure is face-sparse when it is concentrated on a small collection of faces. A face is the set of angles with a fixed pattern of zeros. Asymptotic independence is the case where the only faces are the vertices.

Proposition 6.2 — Subset counts as a sufficient summary

Suppose the angular measure is supported on a known list of faces, and a retained sample of extreme angles is observed. The vector of empirical face frequencies is then a sufficient summary inside that model.

Each frequency obeys Proposition 5.1. A union bound over the list only changes the logarithm under the square root.

Proof. On the support of H, two angles that fall on the same face are indistinguishable by any parameter of the model. The counts of the faces are therefore sufficient. Conditionally on the k extremes, the count vector is multinomial. Each marginal is binomial, so Proposition 5.1 applies, and a union bound of m terms changes only the logarithm in the threshold t. ∎

Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2016) turn face counts into an anomaly rank. A new point is mapped to the subset of coordinates that are large. Its score is the training frequency of that subset. An unused face ranks as anomalous. A heavily used face is a typical extreme. Exercise 9 runs the rule in dimension 3. Engelke and Ivanovs (2021) survey the wider sparse models, including graphs.

Ordinary principal components miss this structure, because the bulk dominates the covariance. A tail version uses only points beyond the radial threshold. It still mixes several faces into one plane when the mass is a union of faces rather than one subspace.

Chiapino, Clémençon, Feuillard and Sabourin (2020) cluster extremes into groups, each group near its own face, and they visualise the groups. The tutorial compares three pictures on one synthetic sample. Ordinary principal components, a clustering of extreme angles, and the face-frequency score.

Thomas, Clémençon, Gramfort and Sabourin (2017) use a different normal set. The minimum-volume set of a given angular mass is a smallest region carrying that mass. Points outside a high-mass set are the anomalies. Face counts and minimum-volume sets are both estimated from the retained angles, so the scale of ch. 5 still governs the error.

Key takeaways — chapter 6.

  • In high dimension a density on the simplex is the wrong target. Face masses are a finite list of 2^d - 1 numbers.
  • A new point is anomalous when its active coordinates match a face that the training extremes rarely use.
  • Ordinary principal components are dominated by the bulk. Tail subspaces and face clusters are two different compressions.
  • Minimum-volume sets on the simplex offer a second anomaly score, studied by Thomas, Clémençon, Gramfort and Sabourin (2017).

7. Supervised extremes, the seminar and the homework

Two supervised problems fill session 7. One predicts a label from extreme covariates. The other reduces dimension when the target, not the covariate, is extreme. Session 8 is the four-hour meeting on this material. It is the question and answer session for the homework, and the seminar for articles.

Ordinary classification minimises the probability of a wrong label. Extreme covariates are rare, so they barely move that probability. A rule can be right on the bulk and wrong when the norm is large. Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin (2018) replace the target by a conditional probability.

Definition 7.1 — Extreme-region risk

Fix a high threshold on the norm of the covariate. The extreme-region risk is the probability of a wrong label, conditional on the norm exceeding that threshold.

The threshold may be a high quantile, so the conditioning event has probability of order k/n.

Proposition 7.2 — Training on the largest norms

Keep the training points with the largest norms, and minimise the empirical error on that subset, inside a class of finite Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension. The resulting rule targets the extreme-region risk. Its excess risk has the order of a deviation inequality on a sample of that smaller size.

Proof. The empirical error on the largest points is the error under the conditional law above a random threshold. Definition 7.1 is that law at a deterministic quantile of the same order. Under regular variation of the norm, the random and deterministic thresholds meet in the limit.

On the retained points the procedure is ordinary empirical risk minimisation. The deviation is of order 1/\sqrt{k}, up to the complexity of the class. Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin (2018) state the inequality. ∎

Bulk training gives most of its votes to moderate points. The tail can vote the other way and still lose. Keeping only the largest norms removes those votes. Exercise 10 computes the scale 1/\sqrt{k} at retained count 50.

Dimension reduction with an extreme target conditions on large values of a real response. A projection of the covariate is enough when the exceedance is independent of the covariate given that projection. Aghbalou, Portier, Sabourin and Zhou (2024) call this tail conditional independence. The projection can be smaller than the one sliced inverse regression keeps for the whole response.

Tail inverse regression tracks how the conditional mean of the covariate moves as the response threshold rises. Those directions span the extreme subspace. A fit on the whole response can miss a direction that appears only in the tail.

Example 7.3 — Bulk error against tail error

Suppose the error is 0.10 on moderate observations and 0.40 on extreme ones, and the extreme region has probability 0.05. The ordinary risk is 0.95 \times 0.10 + 0.05 \times 0.40 = 0.115.

The extreme-region risk is 0.40. Driving the ordinary risk down can leave that 0.40 untouched. A rule trained on the extremes is judged by Definition 7.1, which isolates the 0.40.

The four-hour session ties the theory to the assessment. Homework is half the grade. Each list is due at most two weeks after release. The bonus rule revises at most four past exercises after this meeting. A revision replaces an earlier solution. It does not add a fifth exercise.

The same meeting is the article seminar. Each talk names the target, the tail assumption, what is computed from the retained extremes, one figure to reproduce, and what fails if the upper count is wrong. ch. 8 supplies open problems for the conclusion. The written exam is the other half of the grade.

Train on the k largest points when the decision that matters is the decision in the tail. The sample size in every bound of this chapter is k.

Key takeaways — chapter 7.

  • Ordinary error hides extreme covariates. The extreme-region risk conditions on a large norm.
  • Minimising the error on the k largest training points targets that risk, with a deviation of order 1/\sqrt{k}.
  • Tail inverse regression looks for a projection that explains exceedances of the target, which may be a smaller subspace than the one needed for all of Y.
  • Session 8 revises at most four homework exercises and hosts the article talks. The written exam is the other 50\% of the grade.

8. Research directions and open problems

The in-class chapters stop at methods that already have a theorem and an algorithm. The papers below mark places where the theorem does not yet cover the procedure a practitioner wants to run. The tone of this section is that of a reader. Work by the cited authors shows a positive result, and the gap next to it is stated as a gap.

Threshold selection with a finite-sample guarantee is still open in the form the homework needs. Proposition 3.2 and Proposition 5.1 condition on an upper count that tends to infinity slowly, or on a fixed retained count.

Lhaut, Sabourin and Segers (2022) allow that count to be an order statistic and still bound frequencies uniformly. They do not choose the count from a Hill-plot plateau and then keep a valid interval. One statement that selects and estimates together, with an explicit constant, is open.

Graphical structure for extremes is defined when the graph is known. Engelke and Hitz (2020) factor the exponent measure along a graph. Hitz and Evans (2016) supply the marginal regular variation those models use.

Selecting the edges from a retained extreme sample, and proving that the selected graph recovers the factorisation, is a separate theorem. A union bound over all graphs grows exponentially with dimension, and the useful range of that dimension is not settled.

The rate of tail inverse regression sits next to Aghbalou, Portier, Sabourin and Zhou (2024). The paper proves weak convergence of the tail processes in the estimator. It does not compare, on one sample, the speed gained from a smaller subspace with the speed lost because only the extreme labels are used.

Classification in Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin (2018) keeps the class of rules fixed while the retained count grows. Letting a tree deepen with that count changes the complexity term. A schedule that uses the full sample size in place of the retained count will look safe and still overfit the extremes.

Functional extremes sit one step past the vectors of ch. 4. A spatial field has a supremum norm, or an integral norm, and the exceedances are functions rather than points on a simplex. de Haan and Ferreira (2007) and Resnick (2008) construct the Poisson limits. Statistical learning rates for a predictor of the field, trained only on the extreme members of a catalogue of fields, are not a corollary of Proposition 7.2, because the entropy of the function class replaces the Vapnik dimension. Climate applications make this more than a formal extension. These notes do not claim a rate.

A last direction is honest reporting of dependence between the homework estimator and the exam estimator. Both will often use the same k. Inequalities that pretend the two are independent overstate the evidence. Work by Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2015) on the dependence structure of rare events is a starting point for a joint bound, not the bound itself.

Exercises

Levels follow the session plan. One star applies a stated result. Two stars combine two results or finish a short proof. Three stars ask for a design judgement. Exercises 2 and 6 are computational.

  1. ★ Endpoints of the three shapes. Take a generalized extreme-value law with location 0 and scale 1. For each shape \xi \in \{1/2,\, 0,\, -1/2\}, say whether the support is bounded above, and compute the upper endpoint when it is finite.

    Solution: the support constraint is 1 + \xi z > 0.

For shape 1/2 the variable stays above -2, with no upper endpoint. For shape 0 the law is Gumbel and the line is the whole support. For shape -1/2 the variable stays below 2, so the upper endpoint is 2.

  1. ★ Exponential block maxima, in Python. Proposition 0.2 centres the maximum of m standard exponentials by \log m. Draw 400 independent blocks of size m = 50 and print the mean of the centred maxima.

    Solution: the fence below prints 0.6027. The limiting Gumbel mean is Euler’s constant 0.577. The gap is sampling noise for 400 blocks, not a bias in the centring.

    import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
block = 50
n_blocks = 400
sample = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(n_blocks, block))
centered = sample.max(axis=1) - numpy.log(block)
print(f"{centered.mean():.4f}")

  2. ★ Three pairs of norming constants. For sample size n = 100, give (a_n, b_n) in Definition 1.1 for the standard exponential, for the Pareto survival x^{-2} on [1, \infty), and for the uniform law on [0, 1].

    Solution: the exponential uses scale 1 and shift \log 100 = 4.6052.

The Pareto index 2 uses scale n^{1/2} = 10 and shift 0. The uniform uses scale 1/n = 0.01 and shift 1. The limits are Gumbel, Fréchet with shape 1/2, and Weibull with shape -1.

  1. ★ A one-in-a-hundred return level. A block maximum is modelled with location \mu = 2, scale \sigma = 1 and shape \xi = 0.2. Compute the level exceeded with probability 0.01, from the formula in Example 1.3.

    Solution: set y = -\log 0.99 = 0.01005. Then y^{-\xi} = 2.5094, and

    z = 2 + \frac{1}{0.2}(2.5094 - 1) = 9.5468 .

    The reported return level is 9.5468.

  2. ★★ Mean excess of a generalized Pareto law. Prove that a standard exponential excess has mean equal to its scale. Then, for a generalized Pareto law with \xi = 1/4 and \sigma = 2, compute the mean excess above 0 and the slope of the mean excess in the level.

    Solution: an exponential excess with scale \sigma has mean \sigma. Memorylessness keeps that mean above every level, so the slope is 0.

For a shape between 0 and 1, the mean excess above 0 equals the scale divided by one minus the shape. Here the scale is 2 and the shape is 1/4, so the mean excess is 8/3 = 2.6667. The slope in the level is 1/3.

  1. ★★ A Hill estimate on exact Pareto data. Draw 4000 Pareto variables with survival x^{-2} for x \ge 1, using the inversion in the fence. Compute the Hill statistic on the 200 largest points, and print the statistic together with its reciprocal.

    Solution: the true shape is 1/\alpha = 0.5, and the true index is \alpha = 2. The fence prints 0.5262 and 1.9003. Proposition 3.2 only claims convergence as the design grows. One sample at k = 200 may sit a few hundredths above 0.5.

    import numpy
rng = numpy.random.default_rng(0)
n = 4000
alpha = 2.0
uniforms = rng.random(n)
sample = (1.0 - uniforms) ** (-1.0 / alpha)
sample.sort()
k = 200
hill = numpy.log(sample[-k:]).mean() - numpy.log(sample[-(k + 1)])
print(f"{hill:.4f}")
print(f"{1.0 / hill:.4f}")

  2. ★★ Extremal coefficients of the logistic model. For the bivariate logistic exponent of Example 4.3, compute \theta = 2^{\alpha} at \alpha = 1 and at \alpha = 1/2, and state the limit as \alpha decreases to 0.

    Solution: parameter 1 gives extremal coefficient 2, because the diagonal exponent is 2/z.

Parameter 1/2 gives \sqrt{2} = 1.414. As the parameter falls to 0, the coefficient 2^{\alpha} falls to 1, which is complete dependence.

  1. ★★ When the bulk bound is useless. Take n = 10000 and a tail probability p = 0.01. Compute the Hoeffding half-width that drives the failure probability to 0.05, using sample size n. Compare it with p. Then recompute the half-width of Proposition 5.1 at k = 400.

    Solution: the unconditional half-width is 0.0136, from \sqrt{\log(40)/(2n)} at sample size 10000. It exceeds the tail probability 0.01, so it does not prove a relative error.

Conditionally on 400 extremes, Proposition 5.1 gives 0.0679. That width is a bound on a frequency inside the tail, which is the scale of ch. 5.

  1. ★★★ An anomaly on a face. In dimension 3, suppose the angular measure is supported on the three vertices only. Two new points have coordinates A = (10, 0.1, 0.1) and B = (6, 6, 0.1). Which point is anomalous for the face score of Goix, Sabourin and Clémençon (2016), and why does a full-simplex histogram waste the sample?

    Hints and expected result: the direction of A is close to the first vertex, which carries mass, so A is a typical extreme. The direction of B sits in the middle of an edge, and every edge has mass 0 under the assumption, so B is the anomaly. A histogram on the whole triangle spends bins on a continuum of angles. Vertex masses only need three counters. Empty bins in the interior do not estimate anything the model contains.

  2. ★★★ Which risk is being minimised? A label is predicted from a vector. On moderate norms the best error is 0.10. On extreme norms it is 0.40, and that region has probability 0.05.

One analyst minimises the empirical error on the whole sample. Another uses only the 50 largest norms. Which risk does each target, and what is 1/\sqrt{k} at that count?

*Hints and expected result*: the first analyst targets the ordinary risk $0.95 \times 0.10 + 0.05 \times 0.40 = 0.115$, and can ignore the tail.

The second targets the extreme-region risk 0.40 in Definition 7.1. The scale is 1/\sqrt{50} = 0.1414. It is a sample-size scale, not the constant in Jalalzai, Clémençon and Sabourin (2018). Only the second training set votes for a tail rule that differs from the bulk rule.

Written exam (proposal)

Two hours, one handwritten sheet of notes, no computer. Three questions, mixing a conceptual point, a computation and a short proof. This format is a proposal. The official pages fix the weight at 50\% and do not publish a specimen.

  1. Question (6 points). Explain why minimising the ordinary classification error can produce a rule that is poor when the norm of X is large. Define the extreme-region risk and say which training points are used to target it.

    Expected: the ordinary error is dominated by moderate points, so a tail mistake hardly changes it. Definition 7.1 conditions on a large norm. Proposition 7.2 trains on the k largest norms. A full answer names the 1/\sqrt{k} scale without deriving the constant.

  2. Question (8 points). Pareto observations have survival x^{-2} for x \ge 1. The threshold in use is the order statistic 10, and the upper fraction is 0.05.

Compute the Weissman quantile at probability 0.001 when the fitted shape is 1/2. Then give the norming constants of this Pareto law at sample size 100.

*Expected*: the quantile is $10 \times \sqrt{50} = 70.71$, as in Example 3.3. The norming scale is 10 and the shift is 0, as in Exercise 3. The shape is $1/2$.

  1. Question (6 points). Let the variable be standard exponential. Prove that the excess over any positive level, conditional on exceeding it, is again standard exponential. Deduce the generalized Pareto parameters in Theorem 2.2 for this law.

    Expected: the conditional survival cancels to e^{-y}. The excess is standard exponential, hence generalized Pareto with shape 0 and scale 1. The limit in the theorem is exact for this law.
