This course builds the theory behind that idea and the algorithms that follow from it. 0. Review: Hilbert spaces, positive matrices and duality

Kernel methods compute with positive semidefinite matrices, live in Hilbert spaces and are trained by convex optimization. This chapter collects the M1-level facts used later.

The Linear Algebra, Analysis and Optimization refresher covers the same ground at a slower pace.

Positive semidefinite matrices

Definition 0.1 — Positive semidefinite matrix A symmetric matrix K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} is positive semidefinite (PSD) when \alpha^\top K \alpha \;\geq\; 0 \qquad \text{for every } \alpha \in \mathbb{R}^n . It is positive definite when the inequality is strict for every \alpha \neq 0.

Proposition 0.2 — Three characterizations For a symmetric matrix K, the following are equivalent:

  1. K is PSD;
  2. all eigenvalues of K are non-negative;
  3. K = B^\top B for some matrix B, so K is the Gram matrix of the columns of B.

Proof. By the spectral theorem, K = U \Lambda U^\top with U orthogonal and \Lambda diagonal.

  • (1 ⇒ 2): take \alpha = u_i, the i-th column of U; then u_i^\top K u_i = \lambda_i \geq 0.
  • (2 ⇒ 3): take B = \Lambda^{1/2} U^\top.
  • (3 ⇒ 1): \alpha^\top B^\top B \alpha = \|B\alpha\|^2 \geq 0. ∎

Proposition 0.3 — Schur product theorem If K_1 and K_2 are PSD, so is their entrywise (Hadamard) product K_1 \circ K_2.

Proof. Write K_2 = \sum_k \lambda_k u_k u_k^\top with \lambda_k \geq 0. Entry (i, j) of K_1 \circ K_2 is \sum_k \lambda_k (K_1)_{ij} u_{k,i} u_{k,j}, hence \alpha^\top (K_1 \circ K_2)\, \alpha = \sum_k \lambda_k\, (\alpha \circ u_k)^\top K_1\, (\alpha \circ u_k) \;\geq\; 0 . ∎

Hilbert spaces

Definition 0.4 — Hilbert space A Hilbert space \mathcal{H} is a real vector space with an inner product \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle that is complete for the norm \|f\| = \sqrt{\langle f, f \rangle}: every Cauchy sequence converges.

Examples: \mathbb{R}^d; the sequences \ell^2; the square-integrable functions L^2(\mu). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality holds in all of them: |\langle f, g \rangle| \leq \|f\|\, \|g\| .

Theorem 0.5 — Projection and Riesz representation

  1. Projection. Let F be a closed subspace of \mathcal{H}. Every f \in \mathcal{H} writes uniquely f = f_F + f_\perp with f_F \in F and f_\perp \perp F, and \|f\|^2 = \|f_F\|^2 + \|f_\perp\|^2 .
  2. Riesz. For every continuous linear form L on \mathcal{H} there is a unique g \in \mathcal{H} such that L(f) = \langle g, f \rangle \quad \text{for all } f \in \mathcal{H}.

Proof. Sketch. (1) Take a minimizing sequence of \|f - h\| over h \in F. The parallelogram law shows it is Cauchy; completeness and closedness give a minimizer f_F. The first-order condition of the minimization says f - f_F \perp F, and Pythagoras gives the norm identity.

  1. If L = 0, take g = 0. Otherwise F = \ker L is closed and proper; pick z \perp F with L(z) = 1.

For any f, the vector f - L(f) z lies in F, hence is orthogonal to z. This gives \langle z, f \rangle = L(f)\, \|z\|^2, so g = z / \|z\|^2 works. ∎

Finite-dimensional subspaces are closed, so part 1 applies to the span of finitely many vectors, as in the representer theorem of ch. 3.

Lagrange duality

Consider a convex problem \min_x f_0(x) subject to g_i(x) \leq 0 for i = 1, \dots, m, with f_0 and g_i convex.

Proposition 0.6 — Lagrangian, dual problem and KKT conditions The Lagrangian and the dual function are \mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = f_0(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \mu_i\, g_i(x), \qquad q(\mu) = \inf_x \mathcal{L}(x, \mu), \qquad \mu \geq 0 .

  1. Weak duality: q(\mu) \leq f_0(x) for every feasible x and every \mu \geq 0.
  2. Strong duality (Slater): if some x satisfies all g_i(x) < 0, or if all constraints are affine and the problem is feasible, then \max_{\mu \geq 0} q(\mu) = \min f_0 when the minimum is attained.
  3. KKT: with differentiable functions and under the conditions of item 2, (x^\star, \mu^\star) is primal–dual optimal if and only if it satisfies stationarity \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x^\star, \mu^\star) = 0, feasibility, and complementary slackness: \mu_i^\star\, g_i(x^\star) = 0 \qquad \text{for every } i .

Proof. Weak duality: for feasible x, q(\mu) \leq \mathcal{L}(x, \mu) \leq f_0(x) because each \mu_i g_i(x) \leq 0. Strong duality and the KKT characterization are proved by a separating-hyperplane argument; see Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004), §5.2–5.5.

Example 0.7 — Duality on one variable Minimize x^2 subject to 1 - x \leq 0.

  • Lagrangian: \mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = x^2 + \mu (1 - x), minimized at x = \mu / 2.
  • Dual function: q(\mu) = \mu - \mu^2 / 4, maximized at \mu^\star = 2.
  • Dual optimum q(2) = 1, equal to the primal optimum f_0(1) = 1.

Complementary slackness holds, since the constraint is active: \mu^\star (1 - x^\star) = 2 \times 0 = 0.

Key takeaways — chapter 0

  • A symmetric matrix is PSD iff its eigenvalues are non-negative iff it is a Gram matrix; Hadamard products of PSD matrices are PSD.
  • In a Hilbert space, projection onto a closed subspace splits the norm (Pythagoras), and continuous linear forms are inner products (Riesz).
  • Feasible convex problems with affine constraints, or with a strictly feasible point, have no duality gap; complementary slackness tells which constraints are active.

1. Positive definite kernels and the kernel trick

Many learning algorithms use the data only through inner products \langle x_i, x_j \rangle. Positive definite kernels are the functions that can replace them, turning linear algorithms into non-linear ones on any set of objects.

Definition 1.1 — Positive definite kernel Let \mathcal{X} be any non-empty set. A symmetric function K : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R} is a positive definite (p.d.) kernel when, for every n, every x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathcal{X} and every a \in \mathbb{R}^n, \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n a_i\, a_j\, K(x_i, x_j) \;\geq\; 0 . Equivalently, every Gram matrix [K(x_i, x_j)]_{i,j} is PSD (Definition 0.1).

The name is a historical convention: a p.d. kernel only needs PSD Gram matrices, not positive definite ones.

Proposition 1.2 — Feature maps give kernels Let \mathcal{F} be a Hilbert space and \varphi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{F} any map. Then the following function is a p.d. kernel: K(x, y) = \langle \varphi(x), \varphi(y) \rangle_{\mathcal{F}} .

Proof. Symmetry is clear, and \sum_{i,j} a_i a_j \langle \varphi(x_i), \varphi(x_j) \rangle = \Big\| \sum_i a_i \varphi(x_i) \Big\|^2 \geq 0 . ∎

With \varphi(x) = x this gives the linear kernel K(x, y) = x^\top y. Chapter 2 proves the converse; on a finite set it is already visible.

Example 1.3 — Kernels on a finite set If \mathcal{X} = \{1, \dots, n\}, a kernel is a symmetric matrix and it is p.d. iff the matrix is PSD.

By Proposition 0.2, K = B^\top B, so the columns of B = \Lambda^{1/2} U^\top are explicit feature vectors: K(i, j) = \langle b_i, b_j \rangle.

Building kernels

Proposition 1.4 — Closure properties Let K_1, K_2 be p.d. kernels on \mathcal{X}. Then the following are p.d. kernels:

  1. a K_1 + b K_2 for a, b \geq 0;
  2. the product K_1 K_2;
  3. the pointwise limit of a sequence of p.d. kernels, when it exists;
  4. f(x)\, K_1(x, y)\, f(y) for any function f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R};
  5. K_1(\psi(z), \psi(z')) on a set \mathcal{Z}, for any map \psi : \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{X}.

Consequently, P(K_1) is p.d. for every polynomial P with non-negative coefficients, and so is \exp(K_1).

Proof. Work on Gram matrices.

    1. Non-negative combinations of PSD matrices are PSD.
    1. The Gram matrix of K_1 K_2 is the Hadamard product of the two Gram matrices (Proposition 0.3).
    1. A limit of non-negative numbers is non-negative.
    1. The Gram matrix is D K_1 D with D = \mathrm{diag}(f(x_i)), and \alpha^\top D K_1 D \alpha = (D\alpha)^\top K_1 (D\alpha).
    1. The Gram matrix on z_1, \dots, z_n is the Gram matrix of K_1 on \psi(z_1), \dots, \psi(z_n).

The exponential is the limit of its partial sums, which are polynomials with non-negative coefficients. ∎

Example 1.5 — Polynomial and Gaussian kernels

  • Polynomial: K(x, y) = (x^\top y + c)^d with c \geq 0 and integer d is p.d. by items 1–2.
  • Gaussian: with bandwidth \sigma > 0, K(x, y) = \exp\Big(-\frac{\|x - y\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big) = e^{-\|x\|^2 / 2\sigma^2}\; \exp\Big(\frac{x^\top y}{\sigma^2}\Big)\; e^{-\|y\|^2 / 2\sigma^2} . The middle factor is the exponential of a linear kernel (p.d.), and item 4 with f(x) = e^{-\|x\|^2/2\sigma^2} concludes.

The kernel trick

The kernel trick is the following recipe. Write an algorithm for vectors so that it uses the data only through inner products x_i^\top x_j; then replace each of them by K(x_i, x_j). The new algorithm runs the old one on the feature vectors \varphi(x_i), without ever computing them.

Example 1.6 — An explicit feature map On \mathbb{R}^2, the kernel K(x, y) = (x^\top y)^2 has the feature map \varphi(x) = \big(x_1^2,\; \sqrt{2}\, x_1 x_2,\; x_2^2\big) \in \mathbb{R}^3 .

Take x = (1, 2) and y = (3, 1). Then x^\top y = 5, so K(x, y) = 25.

The features are \varphi(x) = (1, 2\sqrt{2}, 4) and \varphi(y) = (9, 3\sqrt{2}, 1), and \langle \varphi(x), \varphi(y) \rangle = 9 + 12 + 4 = 25 .

For the degree-d kernel on \mathbb{R}^p, the feature space has dimension \binom{p + d - 1}{d}; the kernel costs O(p) whatever d.

Distances also kernelize. Expanding the squared norm gives \|\varphi(x) - \varphi(y)\|^2 = K(x, x) + K(y, y) - 2 K(x, y) .

Proposition 1.7 — Distance to a mean in feature space Let C = \{x_1, \dots, x_m\} and let m_C = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j} \varphi(x_j) be its mean in feature space. Then \|\varphi(x) - m_C\|^2 = K(x, x) - \frac{2}{m} \sum_{j=1}^m K(x, x_j) + \frac{1}{m^2} \sum_{j, l = 1}^m K(x_j, x_l) .

Proof. Expand the square and use bilinearity of the inner product; each inner product \langle \varphi(u), \varphi(v) \rangle equals K(u, v). ∎

The kernel mean classifier assigns x to the class with the closest feature-space mean; with the linear kernel it is the nearest-centroid rule:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons

def gauss(A, B, s):
    d2 = (A**2).sum(1)[:, None] + (B**2).sum(1)[None, :] - 2 * A @ B.T
    return np.exp(-d2 / (2 * s**2))

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=600, noise=0.15, random_state=0)
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:300], y[:300], X[300:], y[300:]

def mean_classifier(kern):
    scores = []
    for c in (0, 1):
        C = Xtr[ytr == c]
        # K(x,x) is the same for both classes and cancels; keep the rest
        d = -2 * kern(Xte, C).mean(1) + kern(C, C).mean()
        scores.append(d)
    return np.argmin(np.vstack(scores), axis=0)

lin = lambda A, B: A @ B.T
print("linear  :", (mean_classifier(lin) == yte).mean())
print("gaussian:", (mean_classifier(lambda A, B: gauss(A, B, 0.3)) == yte).mean())

The term K(x, x) is the same for both classes and cancels. On the two moons, the Gaussian kernel raises the test accuracy from 0.84 to 0.98.

Kernels also apply to objects that are not vectors. For finite subsets A, B of a set, K(A, B) = |A \cap B| is the inner product of their indicator vectors, hence p.d.; counting shared words between documents is the bag-of-words kernel.

Key takeaways — chapter 1

  • A p.d. kernel is a symmetric function whose Gram matrices are all PSD; inner products of feature vectors are p.d. kernels.
  • Sums, products, limits, exponentials, rescalings f(x) K f(y) and compositions with maps preserve positive definiteness; the polynomial and Gaussian kernels follow.
  • The kernel trick runs any inner-product algorithm in feature space; distances and means kernelize too.

2. Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

The feature space of a kernel is not unique, but one choice is canonical: a Hilbert space of functions in which evaluation is an inner product with the kernel. The estimators of ch. 3–5 live there, and its norm measures their smoothness.

Definition 2.1 — Reproducing kernel Hilbert space Let \mathcal{H} be a Hilbert space of functions \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}. A function K : \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R} is a reproducing kernel of \mathcal{H} when:

  1. for every x \in \mathcal{X}, the function K_x = K(x, \cdot) belongs to \mathcal{H};
  2. for every f \in \mathcal{H} and every x \in \mathcal{X}, the reproducing property holds.

Key formula — the reproducing property f(x) = \langle f, K_x \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\mathcal{H} is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) when it has a reproducing kernel.

Proposition 2.2 — RKHS = continuous evaluations A Hilbert space of functions \mathcal{H} is an RKHS if and only if every evaluation map \delta_x : f \mapsto f(x) is continuous on \mathcal{H}.

Proof. If K is a reproducing kernel, Cauchy–Schwarz gives |f(x)| = |\langle f, K_x \rangle| \leq \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\, \|K_x\|_{\mathcal{H}} = \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}} \sqrt{K(x, x)} , so \delta_x is continuous. Here \|K_x\|^2 = \langle K_x, K_x \rangle = K_x(x) = K(x, x) by the reproducing property.

Conversely, if \delta_x is continuous, Riesz (Theorem 0.5) gives g_x \in \mathcal{H} with f(x) = \langle g_x, f \rangle. Set K(x, y) = g_x(y); then K_x = g_x \in \mathcal{H} and the reproducing property holds. ∎

Convergence in an RKHS implies pointwise convergence. L^2([0, 1]) is not an RKHS: evaluation at a point is not defined on its elements.

Proposition 2.3 — The reproducing kernel is unique and p.d. If K is a reproducing kernel of \mathcal{H}, then K(x, y) = \langle K_x, K_y \rangle_{\mathcal{H}}; hence K is a p.d. kernel, and it is the only reproducing kernel of \mathcal{H}.

Proof. Apply the reproducing property to f = K_y at the point x: K(y, x) = \langle K_y, K_x \rangle. Positive definiteness follows from Proposition 1.2 with \varphi(x) = K_x.

If K' is another reproducing kernel, then \langle f, K_x - K'_x \rangle = f(x) - f(x) = 0 for every f. Taking f = K_x - K'_x gives K_x = K'_x. ∎

From a kernel to its RKHS

Theorem 2.4 — Moore–Aronszajn If K is a p.d. kernel on \mathcal{X}, there is a unique Hilbert space \mathcal{H}_K of functions on \mathcal{X} with reproducing kernel K. It is the completion of the span of the functions K_x for the inner product defined in step 1 of the proof.

Proof.

  1. Pre-Hilbert space. Let \mathcal{H}_0 = \mathrm{span}\{K_x : x \in \mathcal{X}\}. For f = \sum_i a_i K_{x_i} and g = \sum_j b_j K_{y_j}, set \langle f, g \rangle = \sum_{i, j} a_i\, b_j\, K(x_i, y_j) .

  2. Well defined. The same number equals \sum_j b_j f(y_j) and \sum_i a_i g(x_i). It depends on the functions f and g, not on their representations.

  3. Reproducing on \mathcal{H}_0. With g = K_x the formula gives \langle f, K_x \rangle = f(x). The form is symmetric, bilinear and \langle f, f \rangle \geq 0 because K is p.d.

  4. Definite. Cauchy–Schwarz holds for any symmetric non-negative bilinear form, so |f(x)| = |\langle f, K_x \rangle| \leq \|f\| \sqrt{K(x, x)}. If \|f\| = 0, then f = 0.

  5. Completion. A Cauchy sequence (f_n) of \mathcal{H}_0 is pointwise Cauchy by the bound of step 4. Let f be its pointwise limit; \mathcal{H}_K is the set of such limits, with \|f\| = \lim \|f_n\|.

    This norm is well defined: a Cauchy sequence that tends to 0 pointwise tends to 0 in norm. Write \|f_n\|^2 = \langle f_n, f_n - f_m \rangle + \langle f_n, f_m \rangle . The first term is small for large n and m. For fixed n, the second is a finite combination of values f_m(x_i), which tend to 0 as m \to \infty.

  6. Uniqueness. In any RKHS \mathcal{H} with kernel K, a function orthogonal to every K_x vanishes everywhere, so \mathcal{H}_0 is dense. Its inner product is forced by step 1, so \mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_K. ∎

Corollary 2.5 — Aronszajn’s characterization A symmetric function K is a p.d. kernel if and only if there are a Hilbert space \mathcal{F} and a map \varphi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{F} such that K(x, y) = \langle \varphi(x), \varphi(y) \rangle_{\mathcal{F}} .

One direction is Proposition 1.2; for the other, take \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{H}_K and the canonical feature map \varphi(x) = K_x.

The norm measures smoothness

Proposition 2.6 — Lipschitz bound For every f \in \mathcal{H}_K and x, y \in \mathcal{X}, |f(x) - f(y)| \leq \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_K}\; d_K(x, y), \qquad d_K(x, y) = \|K_x - K_y\|_{\mathcal{H}_K} .

Proof. f(x) - f(y) = \langle f, K_x - K_y \rangle by the reproducing property, then Cauchy–Schwarz. ∎

A function of small norm varies little between points that the kernel finds similar: regularizing by \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_K}^2 is a smoothness prior defined by the kernel.

Example 2.7 — The RKHS of the linear kernel For K(x, y) = x^\top y on \mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{H}_K is the space of linear functions f_w(x) = w^\top x, with norm \|f_w\| = \|w\|.

Check: K_x = f_x, and \langle f_w, K_x \rangle = w^\top x = f_w(x). Ridge regression is regularized learning in this RKHS.

Example 2.8 — The min kernel and a Sobolev space On [0, 1], the kernel K(x, y) = \min(x, y) (the covariance of Brownian motion) has the RKHS \mathcal{H}_K = \Big\{ f \text{ absolutely continuous},\; f(0) = 0,\; f' \in L^2 \Big\}, \qquad \langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^1 f'(t)\, g'(t)\, dt .

  • K_x(t) = \min(x, t) vanishes at 0 and has derivative \mathbb{1}_{[0, x]}(t), so K_x \in \mathcal{H}_K.
  • For f \in \mathcal{H}_K, the reproducing property is the fundamental theorem of calculus: \langle f, K_x \rangle = \int_0^x f'(t)\, dt = f(x) - f(0) = f(x) .
  • Norms: f(t) = t has \|f\|^2 = 1; f(t) = t^2 has \|f\|^2 = \int_0^1 4 t^2\, dt = 4/3.

The RKHS norm is the energy \int f'^2: regularizing with this kernel penalizes slopes.

For f = \sum_i a_i K_{x_i}, the RKHS norm is a quadratic form in the coefficients: \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_K}^2 = a^\top K a .

The code checks this against the energy \int f'^2 for the min kernel:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, 5))           # centres x_i
a = rng.normal(size=5)                       # coefficients a_i
K = np.minimum.outer(x, x)                   # Gram matrix of min(x, y)
norm_from_gram = a @ K @ a

t = np.linspace(0, 1, 200001)
fprime = sum(ai * (t <= xi) for ai, xi in zip(a, x))   # f' = sum a_i 1[0, x_i]
energy = np.trapezoid(fprime**2, t) if hasattr(np, "trapezoid") else np.trapz(fprime**2, t)
print(f"a^T K a = {norm_from_gram:.5f},  integral of f'^2 = {energy:.5f}")

Key takeaways — chapter 2

  • An RKHS is a Hilbert space of functions where evaluation is continuous; then f(x) = \langle f, K_x \rangle for a unique p.d. kernel K.
  • Conversely every p.d. kernel has a unique RKHS (Moore–Aronszajn), the completion of the span of the K_x; so p.d. kernels are exactly inner products of feature maps.
  • |f(x) - f(y)| \leq \|f\|\, d_K(x, y): the RKHS norm is a smoothness measure adapted to the kernel. For the min kernel it is \int f'^2.

3. The representer theorem and kernel ridge regression

Learning in an RKHS minimizes a risk over a usually infinite-dimensional space of functions. The representer theorem reduces it to n unknowns; kernel ridge regression is the simplest instance.

Throughout, the data are pairs (x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n) with x_i \in \mathcal{X}, the Gram matrix is K = [K(x_i, x_j)]_{i,j}, and \mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_K.

Theorem 3.1 — Representer theorem Let \Psi : \mathbb{R}^{n+1} \to \mathbb{R} be strictly increasing in its last argument. Every minimizer over \mathcal{H} of J(f) = \Psi\big(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n),\; \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\big) has the form f = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i\, K_{x_i}, \qquad \alpha \in \mathbb{R}^n .

Proof. Let S = \mathrm{span}\{K_{x_1}, \dots, K_{x_n}\}, a finite-dimensional, hence closed, subspace. By Theorem 0.5, any f \in \mathcal{H} splits as f = f_S + f_\perp with f_\perp \perp S.

  • The data term does not see f_\perp: since f(x_i) = \langle f, K_{x_i} \rangle and f_\perp \perp K_{x_i}, we get f(x_i) = f_S(x_i).
  • The norm does: \|f\|^2 = \|f_S\|^2 + \|f_\perp\|^2, which is larger than \|f_S\|^2 when f_\perp \neq 0.

So if f_\perp \neq 0, then J(f_S) < J(f) and f is not a minimizer. ∎

The theorem goes back to Kimeldorf and Wahba (1971); the general form is due to Schölkopf, Herbrich and Smola (2001). It does not assert existence, which holds for convex losses and \Psi coercive in \|f\|.

With f = \sum_i \alpha_i K_{x_i}, both ingredients of J are explicit: \big(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n)\big) = K\alpha, \qquad \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 = \alpha^\top K \alpha .

Kernel ridge regression

Kernel ridge regression (KRR) minimizes the regularized empirical squared error, with a regularization parameter \lambda > 0: \min_{f \in \mathcal{H}}\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - f(x_i)\big)^2 + \lambda\, \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 .

Proposition 3.2 — Closed form of kernel ridge regression The problem has a unique solution \hat f = \sum_i \alpha_i K_{x_i} with \alpha = (K + n\lambda I)^{-1}\, y, \qquad \hat f(x) = k_x^\top (K + n\lambda I)^{-1} y , where k_x = \big(K(x_1, x), \dots, K(x_n, x)\big)^\top.

Proof. By Theorem 3.1, it suffices to minimize over \alpha the convex function G(\alpha) = \frac{1}{n} \|y - K\alpha\|^2 + \lambda\, \alpha^\top K \alpha .

Its gradient is \frac{2}{n} K \big( (K + n\lambda I)\alpha - y \big), which vanishes at the announced \alpha; the matrix K + n\lambda I is invertible because K is PSD.

If another \alpha' is also optimal, then K(\alpha' - \alpha) = 0, so the two functions differ by g with \|g\|^2 = (\alpha' - \alpha)^\top K (\alpha' - \alpha) = 0. The function \hat f is unique. ∎

Key formula — kernel ridge regression \hat f(x) = k_x^\top\, (K + n\lambda I)^{-1}\, y

Example 3.3 — Two points, Gaussian kernel Take x_1 = 0, x_2 = 1, y = (1, -1), the Gaussian kernel with \sigma = 1, and n\lambda = 0.1.

  • Gram matrix: off-diagonal entry e^{-1/2} = 0.6065, diagonal entries 1.
  • By symmetry, \alpha = (a, -a) with a\,(1.1 - 0.6065) = 1, so \alpha = (2.026,\; -2.026) .
  • Fitted values: \hat f(0) = 2.026 \times (1 - 0.6065) = 0.797 and \hat f(1) = -0.797; the regularization shrinks the targets \pm 1.
  • Elsewhere: \hat f(0.5) = 0 by symmetry, \hat f(2) = 2.026\,(e^{-2} - e^{-1/2}) = -0.955 and \hat f(3) = -0.252.

Far from the data, \hat f returns to 0: the Gaussian RKHS contains no non-zero constant function.

Regularization as spectral filtering

Write the eigendecomposition K = \sum_i \mu_i u_i u_i^\top. The fitted values \hat y = K\alpha are a linear smoother of the targets.

Proposition 3.4 — Shrinkage of each eigendirection The fitted values satisfy \hat y = S_\lambda\, y with S_\lambda = K (K + n\lambda I)^{-1} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\mu_i}{\mu_i + n\lambda}\, u_i u_i^\top .

The component of y along u_i is multiplied by a factor in [0, 1), close to 1 when \mu_i \gg n\lambda and close to 0 when \mu_i \ll n\lambda. The effective degrees of freedom are \mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \mathrm{tr}\, S_\lambda = \sum_i \frac{\mu_i}{\mu_i + n\lambda} .

Proof. K and (K + n\lambda I)^{-1} are diagonal in the same basis, with eigenvalues \mu_i and 1/(\mu_i + n\lambda). ∎

For smooth kernels the eigenvalues decay fast and the eigenvectors with small \mu_i oscillate (ch. 6). KRR keeps the smooth components of y and damps the rough ones; \mathrm{df}(\lambda) counts those kept.

The code fits KRR from scratch, checks it against scikit-learn (whose alpha is n\lambda) and prints the effective degrees of freedom:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.kernel_ridge import KernelRidge

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 80
x = np.sort(rng.uniform(-3, 3, n))
y = np.sin(2 * x) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=n)
xt = np.linspace(-3, 3, 400)
yt = np.sin(2 * xt)

def gauss(a, b, s=0.5):
    return np.exp(-(a[:, None] - b[None, :]) ** 2 / (2 * s**2))

K = gauss(x, x)
mu = np.linalg.eigvalsh(K)
for lam in [1e-4, 1e-2, 1.0]:
    alpha = np.linalg.solve(K + n * lam * np.eye(n), y)
    pred = gauss(xt, x) @ alpha
    df = np.sum(mu / (mu + n * lam))
    sk = KernelRidge(alpha=n * lam, kernel="rbf", gamma=1 / (2 * 0.5**2)).fit(x[:, None], y)
    gap = np.abs(sk.predict(xt[:, None]) - pred).max()
    print(f"lambda={lam:g}: test MSE={np.mean((pred - yt) ** 2):.4f}, df={df:.1f}, |ours-sklearn|={gap:.1e}")

The middle \lambda is best: the smallest spends 16 effective parameters fitting noise, the largest keeps less than one and oversmooths.

Key takeaways — chapter 3

  • Representer theorem: when the objective depends on f only through f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n) and increasingly on \|f\|, the minimizer is \sum_i \alpha_i K_{x_i}.
  • KRR: \alpha = (K + n\lambda I)^{-1} y. It shrinks each eigendirection of K by \mu_i / (\mu_i + n\lambda).

4. Support vector machines

The support vector machine (SVM) is regularized learning in an RKHS with the hinge loss. Its dual involves only the Gram matrix, and its solution depends only on a few training points, the support vectors.

Labels are now y_i \in \{-1, +1\}, and the classifier is the sign of g(x) = f(x) + b, with f \in \mathcal{H} and an unpenalized offset b \in \mathbb{R}.

Definition 4.1 — Soft-margin SVM With the hinge loss \max(0, 1 - y\,g(x)) and a constant C > 0, the SVM solves \min_{f \in \mathcal{H},\, b,\, \xi}\; \frac{1}{2} \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i \big(f(x_i) + b\big) \geq 1 - \xi_i, \;\; \xi_i \geq 0 .

At the optimum \xi_i = \max(0, 1 - y_i g(x_i)). Dividing by nC shows that this is the hinge-loss risk plus \lambda \|f\|^2 with \lambda = 1/(2nC).

Proposition 4.2 — Margin Take the linear kernel, so g(x) = w^\top x + b, and separable data with all \xi_i = 0. The distance from x_i to the hyperplane \{g = 0\} is |g(x_i)| / \|w\|. The constraints y_i g(x_i) \geq 1 therefore guarantee a margin \min_i \frac{|g(x_i)|}{\|w\|} \geq \frac{1}{\|w\|} .

Minimizing \|w\|^2 maximizes this margin. With a general kernel, the same holds for the hyperplane in feature space.

Proof. The distance formula is the projection of x_i on the normal direction w / \|w\|. ∎

The dual problem

Theorem 4.3 — Dual of the SVM The dual of Definition 4.1 is the quadratic program \max_{\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^n}\; \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j\, y_i y_j\, K(x_i, x_j) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 0 \leq \alpha_i \leq C, \;\; \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0 .

There is no duality gap, and the optimal function is \hat f = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i\, y_i\, K_{x_i} .

Proof. Introduce multipliers \alpha_i \geq 0 for the margin constraints and \nu_i \geq 0 for \xi_i \geq 0: \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\|f\|^2 + C \sum_i \xi_i - \sum_i \alpha_i \big( y_i (f(x_i) + b) - 1 + \xi_i \big) - \sum_i \nu_i \xi_i .

Minimize over the primal variables, writing f(x_i) = \langle f, K_{x_i} \rangle:

  1. in f, the gradient f - \sum_i \alpha_i y_i K_{x_i} must vanish, which gives \hat f;
  2. in b, the coefficient \sum_i \alpha_i y_i must vanish, otherwise the infimum is -\infty;
  3. in \xi_i, C - \alpha_i - \nu_i = 0, and \nu_i \geq 0 gives \alpha_i \leq C.

Substituting \hat f gives \|\hat f\|^2 = \alpha^\top Q \alpha with Q_{ij} = y_i y_j K(x_i, x_j), and the dual function is the announced objective. The constraints are affine and the problem is feasible, so strong duality holds (Proposition 0.6). ∎

Proposition 4.4 — Support vectors Complementary slackness reads \alpha_i \big( y_i g(x_i) - 1 + \xi_i \big) = 0 and (C - \alpha_i)\, \xi_i = 0. Hence, for each training point:

  • \alpha_i = 0: then y_i g(x_i) \geq 1, the point is correctly classified, on or outside the margin;
  • 0 < \alpha_i < C: then \xi_i = 0 and y_i g(x_i) = 1, the point lies on the margin;
  • \alpha_i = C: then y_i g(x_i) \leq 1, the point is on or inside the margin, possibly misclassified.

Only the support vectors (\alpha_i > 0) enter \hat f. Any point with 0 < \alpha_i < C gives the offset: b = y_i - \hat f(x_i) .

Proof.

  • If \alpha_i = 0, then \nu_i = C > 0 forces \xi_i = 0, and feasibility gives y_i g(x_i) \geq 1.
  • If 0 < \alpha_i < C, then \nu_i > 0 forces \xi_i = 0, and \alpha_i > 0 makes the margin constraint active.
  • If \alpha_i = C, the margin constraint is active, so y_i g(x_i) = 1 - \xi_i \leq 1. ∎

Example 4.5 — Four points on a line Take the linear kernel on \mathbb{R}, points x = (-2, -1, 1, 2) and labels y = (-1, -1, +1, +1), with C \geq 1/2.

  • Guess that only x = \pm 1 are support vectors, with equal multipliers a. Then \hat f(x) = w x with w = a \cdot 1 + a \cdot 1 = 2a.
  • The margin condition y\, g(x) = 1 at x = 1 and x = -1 gives b = 0 and w = 1, so a = 1/2.
  • Check: the dual objective is \sum_i \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} w^2 = \frac{1}{2}, equal to the primal value \frac{1}{2} w^2 = \frac{1}{2}.

The points \pm 2 satisfy y\, g(x) = 2 > 1 and get \alpha = 0: moving them does not change the classifier.

The code reproduces the introduction’s comparison, then checks Proposition 4.4 on the dual solution:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y01 = make_moons(n_samples=600, noise=0.15, random_state=0)
y = 2 * y01 - 1                                   # labels in {-1, +1}
Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte = X[:300], y[:300], X[300:], y[300:]

def gauss(A, B, s=0.5):
    d2 = (A**2).sum(1)[:, None] + (B**2).sum(1)[None, :] - 2 * A @ B.T
    return np.exp(-d2 / (2 * s**2))

C = 1.0
for name, ker in [("linear", lambda A, B: A @ B.T), ("gaussian", gauss)]:
    svm = SVC(kernel="precomputed", C=C).fit(ker(Xtr, Xtr), ytr)
    print(f"{name:8s} test accuracy {svm.score(ker(Xte, Xtr), yte):.3f}, "
          f"{len(svm.support_)} support vectors")

# KKT check for the Gaussian SVM: alpha_i * y_i are stored in dual_coef_
alpha = np.abs(svm.dual_coef_.ravel())
idx = svm.support_
g = svm.decision_function(gauss(Xtr, Xtr))
free = (alpha > 1e-6) & (alpha < C - 1e-6)
print("sum alpha_i y_i  =", round(float(alpha @ ytr[idx]), 8))
print("y g(x) on free SVs: min %.3f, max %.3f" % ((ytr[idx] * g[idx])[free].min(),
                                                   (ytr[idx] * g[idx])[free].max()))
nonsv = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(300), idx)
print("min y g(x) off the support:", round(float((ytr[nonsv] * g[nonsv]).min()), 3))

Free support vectors sit at y\, g(x) = 1 up to solver tolerance, and all other points have y\, g(x) \geq 1.

Key takeaways — chapter 4

  • The SVM minimizes \frac{1}{2}\|f\|^2 + C \sum_i \max(0, 1 - y_i g(x_i)); in feature space it maximizes a soft margin.
  • Its dual is a quadratic program in \alpha \in [0, C]^n with \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0, and \hat f = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i K_{x_i}.
  • Complementary slackness sorts the points: \alpha_i = 0 on or outside the margin, 0 < \alpha_i < C on it, \alpha_i = C on or inside it. Only support vectors matter.

5. Unsupervised kernel methods: kernel PCA, kernel k-means, kernel CCA

PCA, k-means and canonical correlation analysis use the data only through inner products and distances, so the kernel trick applies to each. Two details need care: centering in feature space, and regularization for CCA.

Kernel PCA

Kernel PCA (Schölkopf, Smola and Müller, 1998) runs PCA on the centred features \tilde\varphi(x_i) = \varphi(x_i) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n \varphi(x_j) . Their Gram matrix is the centred Gram matrix K_c = H K H, \qquad H = I - \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top .

Proposition 5.1 — Kernel PCA Let K_c = \sum_k \mu_k u_k u_k^\top with \mu_1 \geq \mu_2 \geq \dots \geq 0. For each k with \mu_k > 0, the k-th principal direction in feature space is f_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{k,i}\, \tilde\varphi(x_i), \qquad \alpha_k = u_k / \sqrt{\mu_k} .

It has unit norm, the directions are orthogonal, and the variance of the data along f_k is \mu_k / n. The coordinate of the training point x_i is \sqrt{\mu_k}\, u_{k,i}. For a new point, with the kernel \tilde K centred by the training means, \langle f_k, \tilde\varphi(x) \rangle = \sum_i \alpha_{k,i}\, \tilde K(x_i, x) .

Proof. The variance along a unit vector f is the mean of the squared projections \langle f, \tilde\varphi(x_i) \rangle^2. A component of f orthogonal to all \tilde\varphi(x_i) adds norm but no variance, so the optimal f lies in their span: f = \sum_i \alpha_i \tilde\varphi(x_i).

Then the projections are K_c \alpha and \|f\|^2 = \alpha^\top K_c \alpha. We maximize \alpha^\top K_c^2 \alpha subject to \alpha^\top K_c \alpha = 1.

Write \alpha = \sum_k c_k u_k. The objective is \sum_k \mu_k \cdot \mu_k c_k^2, under the constraint \sum_k \mu_k c_k^2 = 1.

This is a weighted average of the eigenvalues, with weights \mu_k c_k^2 summing to 1. It is maximal when all the weight is on \mu_1, that is c_1^2 = 1/\mu_1: the claim for k = 1.

The following directions are obtained in the same way, orthogonally to the previous ones: \langle f_k, f_l \rangle = \alpha_k^\top K_c \alpha_l = 0 for k \neq l. ∎

The directions found are curved in the input space. Mapping a feature-space point back to the input space (the pre-image problem) has no exact solution in general.

Example 5.2 — Two concentric circles On 400 points on two concentric circles, logistic regression on the raw coordinates scores 0.50. With a Gaussian kernel (\sigma = 0.25), the first kernel principal component alone separates the circles perfectly (code below).

Kernel k-means and spectral clustering

Kernel k-means minimizes, over partitions into clusters c_1, \dots, c_k, the within-cluster scatter in feature space: W(c_1, \dots, c_k) = \sum_{l=1}^k \sum_{i \in c_l} \big\| \varphi(x_i) - m_l \big\|^2, \qquad m_l = \frac{1}{|c_l|} \sum_{j \in c_l} \varphi(x_j) .

Algorithm 5.3 — Kernel k-means Start from a partition. Repeat until no point moves:

  1. for every point i and cluster l, compute \|\varphi(x_i) - m_l\|^2 from the Gram matrix with Proposition 1.7;
  2. move every point to its closest cluster.

Each iteration costs O(n^2) and does not increase W, by the argument of Lloyd’s algorithm. Like k-means, it can stop at a poor local minimum.

Proposition 5.4 — Trace form and spectral relaxation Let Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} with Z_{il} = 1 / \sqrt{|c_l|} if i \in c_l and 0 otherwise, so that Z^\top Z = I_k. Then W = \mathrm{tr}(K) - \mathrm{tr}(Z^\top K Z) .

Relaxing Z to any matrix with orthonormal columns, \mathrm{tr}(Z^\top K Z) is maximized by the k leading eigenvectors of K (Ky Fan’s theorem). Rounding the rows of this relaxed solution with k-means is spectral clustering.

Proof. Expanding with Proposition 1.7, the scatter of cluster c is \sum_{i \in c} K_{ii} - \frac{1}{|c|} \sum_{i, j \in c} K_{ij} .

Summing over clusters, the first terms give \mathrm{tr}(K) and the second ones give \mathrm{tr}(Z^\top K Z), by definition of Z. ∎

Applied to a normalized affinity matrix D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}, the same relaxation gives the algorithm of Ng, Jordan and Weiss (2002); Dhillon, Guan and Kulis (2004) show that normalized cut is a weighted kernel k-means objective. See also Graphs in Machine Learning.

Kernel CCA

CCA looks for maximally correlated projections of two views (x_i, y_i) of the same objects, such as an image and its caption. Kernel CCA uses functions f \in \mathcal{H}_1 and g \in \mathcal{H}_2.

Definition 5.5 — Empirical kernel CCA Let K_1 and K_2 be the centred Gram matrices of the two views. For f = \sum_i \alpha_i \tilde\varphi_1(x_i) and g = \sum_i \beta_i \tilde\varphi_2(y_i), the empirical correlation of f(x_i) and g(y_i) is \rho(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\alpha^\top K_1 K_2\, \beta}{\sqrt{\alpha^\top K_1^2\, \alpha}\; \sqrt{\beta^\top K_2^2\, \beta}} .

Proposition 5.6 — Unregularized kernel CCA is degenerate If K_1 and K_2 both have range \{\mathbf{1}\}^\perp, the maximal value of \rho is 1, whatever the data. This is the case for the Gaussian kernel and distinct points.

Proof. Pick any non-zero u \perp \mathbf{1} and set \alpha = K_1^{+} u, \beta = K_2^{+} u. Then K_1 \alpha = K_2 \beta = u, and \rho = u^\top u / (\|u\| \|u\|) = 1.

For the Gaussian kernel, K is positive definite on distinct points, so v^\top H K H v = v^\top K v > 0 for every non-zero v \perp \mathbf{1}. ∎

A correlation of 1 for any data carries no information. The remedy (Bach and Jordan, 2002) penalizes the RKHS norms, replacing each variance \alpha^\top K_1^2 \alpha by \alpha^\top (K_1 + \gamma I)^2 \alpha, which adds 2\gamma \|f\|^2 + \gamma^2 \|\alpha\|^2.

Proposition 5.7 — Regularized kernel CCA With R_j = K_j (K_j + \gamma I)^{-1}, the largest regularized canonical correlation is the largest singular value of R_1 R_2. If (a, b) are the top singular vectors, then \alpha = (K_1 + \gamma I)^{-1} a, \qquad \beta = (K_2 + \gamma I)^{-1} b .

Proof. Substitute a = (K_1 + \gamma I)\alpha and b = (K_2 + \gamma I)\beta; the constraints become \|a\| = \|b\| = 1.

Because K_j and (K_j + \gamma I)^{-1} commute, the numerator becomes a^\top R_1 R_2\, b. Its maximum over unit vectors is the largest singular value of R_1 R_2. ∎

The matrices R_j are the smoother matrices of kernel ridge regression (Proposition 3.4). As \gamma \to 0 they tend to projections and the correlation tends to 1, which is Proposition 5.6 again.

def kernel_kmeans(K, k, lab):
    while True:
        D = np.stack([np.diag(K) - 2 * K[:, lab == c].mean(1)
                      + K[np.ix_(lab == c, lab == c)].mean() for c in range(k)], 1)
        if np.array_equal(D.argmin(1), lab):
            return lab
        lab = D.argmin(1)

      % (ari(y, rand), ari(y, spec), ari(y, kernel_kmeans(K, 2, spec.copy()))))

    return K - K.mean(0) - Ktr.mean(1)[:, None] + Ktr.mean()

Key takeaways — chapter 5

  • Kernel PCA diagonalizes the centred Gram matrix HKH; the coefficients of the k-th direction are u_k / \sqrt{\mu_k}, and the variance it explains is \mu_k / n.
  • Kernel k-means needs only the Gram matrix; its objective is \mathrm{tr}\,K - \mathrm{tr}(Z^\top K Z), and relaxing Z gives spectral clustering.
  • Kernel CCA without regularization always reaches correlation 1; regularized kernel CCA is a singular value problem for R_1 R_2, built from KRR smoothers.

6. Mercer, Bochner and semigroup kernels

Choosing a kernel means choosing an RKHS, that is, a notion of smoothness. Three theorems recognize p.d. kernels and describe their RKHS: Mercer’s on compact domains, Bochner’s for translation-invariant kernels, and Schoenberg’s for kernels built from distances, which leads to kernels on histograms.

Mercer kernels

Let \mathcal{X} be a compact metric space, \mu a finite Borel measure whose support is \mathcal{X}, and K a continuous p.d. kernel. The integral operator of K acts on L^2(\mu): (T_K f)(x) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} K(x, t)\, f(t)\, d\mu(t) . It is compact, self-adjoint, and positive: \langle f, T_K f \rangle_{L^2} \geq 0.

Theorem 6.1 — Mercer (1909) There is an orthonormal family (\psi_k)_{k \geq 1} of L^2(\mu) of continuous eigenfunctions of T_K, with eigenvalues \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots > 0 tending to 0, such that K(x, y) = \sum_{k \geq 1} \lambda_k\, \psi_k(x)\, \psi_k(y), where the series converges absolutely and uniformly on \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X}.

Proof. Sketch. The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators gives the eigenpairs; positivity of T_K makes the eigenvalues non-negative, and continuity of K makes \psi_k = T_K \psi_k / \lambda_k continuous.

The remainder K - \sum_{k \leq N} \lambda_k \psi_k \otimes \psi_k is a continuous p.d. kernel, so its diagonal is non-negative and \sum_k \lambda_k \psi_k(x)^2 \leq K(x, x). Dini’s theorem makes the convergence on the diagonal uniform, and Cauchy–Schwarz extends it off the diagonal. Full proof: Steinwart and Christmann (2008), Theorem 4.49.

Corollary 6.2 — The RKHS in the Mercer basis With the notation of Theorem 6.1, \mathcal{H}_K = \Big\{ f = \sum_k a_k \psi_k \;:\; \sum_k \frac{a_k^2}{\lambda_k} < \infty \Big\}, \qquad \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_K}^2 = \sum_k \frac{a_k^2}{\lambda_k} .

The Mercer feature map sends x to the sequence \big(\sqrt{\lambda_k}\, \psi_k(x)\big)_{k \geq 1} of \ell^2.

Proof. Check of the reproducing property. K_x has coefficients a_k = \lambda_k \psi_k(x), and \sum_k a_k^2 / \lambda_k = K(x, x) < \infty, so K_x belongs to the space.

For f = \sum_k a_k \psi_k, the inner product with K_x is \sum_k a_k\, \lambda_k \psi_k(x) / \lambda_k = f(x). Uniqueness of the RKHS (Theorem 2.4) concludes. ∎

Eigenfunctions with small eigenvalues, typically the oscillating ones, are expensive in RKHS norm. This is the continuous version of Proposition 3.4: the eigenvalues of K / n approximate the \lambda_k when the x_i are drawn from \mu.

Example 6.3 — The Mercer expansion of the min kernel On [0, 1] with the Lebesgue measure, the kernel \min(x, y) of Example 2.8 has eigenfunctions and eigenvalues \psi_k(x) = \sqrt{2}\, \sin\big((k - \tfrac{1}{2}) \pi x\big), \qquad \lambda_k = \frac{1}{(k - \frac{1}{2})^2 \pi^2} .

  • Numerically: \lambda_1 = 0.4053, \lambda_2 = 0.0450, \lambda_3 = 0.0162, \lambda_4 = 0.0083; the code below recovers them from a 2000 \times 2000 Gram matrix.
  • The RKHS norm \sum_k a_k^2 (k - \frac{1}{2})^2 \pi^2 equals \int_0^1 f'^2, because the derivatives \psi_k' are orthogonal with squared norms (k - \frac{1}{2})^2 \pi^2.

This is the Karhunen–Loève expansion of Brownian motion.

Translation-invariant kernels

Bochner’s theorem characterizes the p.d. kernels of the form K(x, y) = \kappa(x - y) on \mathbb{R}^d.

Theorem 6.4 — Bochner A continuous function \kappa : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R} defines a p.d. kernel \kappa(x - y) if and only if it is the Fourier transform of a finite, non-negative, symmetric Borel measure \Lambda: \kappa(z) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{i\, \omega^\top z}\, d\Lambda(\omega) .

Proof. (⇐) For any points and coefficients, \sum_{j, l} a_j a_l\, \kappa(x_j - x_l) = \int \Big| \sum_j a_j\, e^{i\, \omega^\top x_j} \Big|^2 d\Lambda(\omega) \geq 0 . (⇒) is the harder direction; see Rudin, Fourier Analysis on Groups (1962), or Berg, Christensen and Ressel (1984).

Examples: the Gaussian kernel e^{-\|z\|^2 / 2\sigma^2} corresponds to a Gaussian measure \Lambda with covariance \sigma^{-2} I; the Laplace kernel e^{-|z| / \sigma} on \mathbb{R} corresponds to a Cauchy measure. Sampling \omega \sim \Lambda is the basis of the random features of ch. 9.

Negative definite and semigroup kernels

Schoenberg’s theorem says when exponentiating a distance or a divergence gives a p.d. kernel.

Definition 6.5 — Negative definite kernel A symmetric function \psi on \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} is negative definite (n.d.) when, for all points x_1, \dots, x_n and all c \in \mathbb{R}^n with \sum_i c_i = 0, \sum_{i, j} c_i\, c_j\, \psi(x_i, x_j) \leq 0 .

Example: \psi(x, y) = \|\varphi(x) - \varphi(y)\|^2 for any feature map. Expanding, the terms \|\varphi(x_i)\|^2 cancel because \sum_i c_i = 0, and what remains is -2 \|\sum_i c_i \varphi(x_i)\|^2 \leq 0.

Theorem 6.6 — Schoenberg (1938) A symmetric function \psi is negative definite if and only if \exp(-t\, \psi) \text{ is a p.d. kernel for every } t > 0 .

Proof. (⇐) Let \sum_i c_i = 0. The constant 1 then contributes nothing, so \sum_{i,j} c_i c_j\, \frac{1 - e^{-t\psi_{ij}}}{t} = -\frac{1}{t} \sum_{i,j} c_i c_j\, e^{-t\psi_{ij}} \leq 0 . Let t \to 0: the left-hand side tends to \sum_{i,j} c_i c_j \psi_{ij}, which is therefore \leq 0.

(⇒) Fix x_0 \in \mathcal{X} and set \tilde K(x, y) = \psi(x, x_0) + \psi(y, x_0) - \psi(x, y) - \psi(x_0, x_0) .

  • \tilde K is p.d.: given a \in \mathbb{R}^n, apply negative definiteness to the points x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n with coefficients (-\sum_i a_i, a_1, \dots, a_n), which sum to 0. Expanding gives exactly -\sum_{i,j} a_i a_j \tilde K(x_i, x_j) \leq 0.
  • Then, with f(x) = e^{-t\psi(x, x_0)}, e^{-t\psi(x, y)} = e^{t \psi(x_0, x_0)}\, f(x)\, e^{t \tilde K(x, y)}\, f(y) .
  • The exponential of a p.d. kernel is p.d., and so is f(x) \cdot f(y) times it (Proposition 1.4). ∎

With \psi = \|x - y\|^2 this proves again that the Gaussian kernel is p.d. Moreover \|x - y\|^p is n.d. for 0 < p \leq 2, so e^{-t \|x - y\|^p} is p.d.; it is not for p > 2, and the code exhibits a negative eigenvalue for p = 3.

Data such as histograms and measures form a semigroup (S, +), for instance S = \mathbb{R}_+^d. A semigroup kernel has the form K(x, y) = \phi(x + y), as in Berg, Christensen and Ressel (1984).

Proposition 6.7 — Semigroup kernels from Laplace transforms Let \nu be a non-negative measure on \mathbb{R}_+^d such that \phi(s) = \int e^{-\langle t, s \rangle} d\nu(t) is finite for every s \in (0, \infty)^d. Then K(x, y) = \phi(x + y) is a p.d. kernel on (0, \infty)^d.

Proof. \sum_{i,j} a_i a_j\, \phi(x_i + x_j) = \int \Big( \sum_i a_i\, e^{-\langle t, x_i \rangle} \Big)^2 d\nu(t) \geq 0 . ∎

Under regularity conditions, every bounded p.d. function on this semigroup has this form (Berg, Christensen and Ressel). For instance 1 / (x + y) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t(x + y)} dt is p.d. on (0, \infty).

Proposition 6.8 — Kernels on histograms On histograms with positive entries, x, y \in (0, \infty)^d:

  1. the intersection kernel K(x, y) = \sum_k \min(x_k, y_k) is p.d.;
  2. the \chi^2 distance below is n.d., so e^{-t\, \chi^2(x, y)} is p.d. for every t > 0: \chi^2(x, y) = \sum_k \frac{(x_k - y_k)^2}{x_k + y_k} .

Proof.

  1. For a, b \geq 0, \min(a, b) is the inner product of the indicators of [0, a] and [0, b] in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+), hence p.d.; sum over k.

  2. Write each term as \frac{(a - b)^2}{a + b} = a + b - \frac{4ab}{a + b} .

  • The part a + b contributes 0 when \sum_i c_i = 0.
  • The part ab / (a + b) is p.d.: 1/(a + b) is p.d. (Proposition 6.7), and multiplying by a \cdot b keeps it p.d. (Proposition 1.4).
  • So -4ab/(a + b) contributes a non-positive amount; summing over the bins k keeps negative definiteness, and Theorem 6.6 concludes. ∎

These kernels were the standard tool of image classification with bags of visual words, histograms of quantized local descriptors (Zhang, Marszałek, Lazebnik and Schmid, 2007). Cuturi, Fukumizu and Vert (2005) extend the construction to measures.

Key takeaways — chapter 6

  • Mercer: on a compact domain, K = \sum_k \lambda_k \psi_k \otimes \psi_k, and \|f\|^2 = \sum_k a_k^2 / \lambda_k; the RKHS norm penalizes eigenfunctions with small eigenvalues.
  • Bochner: \kappa(x - y) is p.d. iff \kappa is the Fourier transform of a non-negative measure; ch. 11 reads the RKHS in the frequency domain.
  • Schoenberg: e^{-t\psi} is p.d. for all t > 0 iff \psi is negative definite; with semigroup arguments this gives the \chi^2 and intersection kernels on histograms.

7. Kernels for strings and graphs, kernels on graphs

Sequences and graphs motivated kernel methods in computational biology, text processing and chemistry. This chapter builds kernels for them by counting substructures and by diffusing along a graph, and shows that some natural kernels are intractable.

Kernels for strings

Let \mathcal{A} be a finite alphabet (4 nucleotides, 20 amino acids, or letters) and \mathcal{X} = \mathcal{A}^* the set of finite strings.

Definition 7.1 — Spectrum kernel (Leslie, Eskin and Noble, 2002) For u \in \mathcal{A}^k, let \varphi_u(x) be the number of occurrences of u as a contiguous substring of x. The k-spectrum kernel is K(x, y) = \sum_{u \in \mathcal{A}^k} \varphi_u(x)\, \varphi_u(y) .

With a hash table or a suffix tree, K(x, y) costs O(|x| + |y|) for fixed k, although the feature space has dimension |\mathcal{A}|^k.

Example 7.2 — A 2-spectrum kernel Let x be ACGTACG and y be TACGT.

  • The 2-mers of x are AC, CG, GT, TA, AC, CG, so AC and CG appear twice, GT and TA once.
  • The 2-mers of y are TA, AC, CG, GT, each once.

Hence K(x, y) = 2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 = 6 .

The mismatch kernel (Leslie, Eskin, Cohen, Weston and Noble, 2004) also counts k-mers within m substitutions, which suits mutations in protein families.

Definition 7.3 — Gap-weighted subsequence kernel (Lodhi et al., 2002) Fix a length p and a decay \lambda \in (0, 1]. An occurrence of u \in \mathcal{A}^p in s is a tuple of indices i_1 < \dots < i_p such that s_{i_1} \cdots s_{i_p} = u.

Its span is \ell(i) = i_p - i_1 + 1. The features and the kernel are \varphi_u(s) = \sum_{i \,:\, s[i] = u} \lambda^{\ell(i)}, \qquad K_p(s, t) = \sum_{u \in \mathcal{A}^p} \varphi_u(s)\, \varphi_u(t) .

Gaps are allowed but penalized: “cat” occurs in “cart” with span 4, hence weight \lambda^4.

Enumerating the occurrences costs \binom{|s|}{p} operations; a dynamic program avoids it. Write sa for the string s followed by the letter a, and t[1{:}j] for the prefix of length j.

Proposition 7.4 — Dynamic programming for the subsequence kernel Define auxiliary kernels K'_i, which weight each common subsequence by its span from its first letter to the end of both strings. They satisfy K'_0 = 1, K'_i(s, t) = 0 if \min(|s|, |t|) < i, and K'_i(sa, t) = \lambda\, K'_i(s, t) + \sum_{j \,:\, t_j = a} K'_{i-1}\big(s, t[1{:}j{-}1]\big)\, \lambda^{|t| - j + 2} .

The kernel follows, with K_p(s, t) = 0 if \min(|s|, |t|) < p: K_p(sa, t) = K_p(s, t) + \lambda^2 \sum_{j \,:\, t_j = a} K'_{p-1}\big(s, t[1{:}j{-}1]\big) .

The cost is O(p\, |s|\, |t|^2), and O(p\, |s|\, |t|) with one more auxiliary table.

Proof. Key step. Split the common subsequences of sa and t according to whether they use the final letter a of sa.

  • Those that do not use it are common subsequences of s and t. Appending a lengthens their span to the end of the first string by one: factor \lambda in K'_i, and no change in K_p, which measures spans only between the first and last matched letters.

  • Those that use it match a with some t_j = a. Their first i - 1 letters form a common subsequence of s and t[1{:}j{-}1], counted by K'_{i-1} up to the end of these prefixes.

    Extending the spans to the ends of sa and t adds 1 + (|t| - j + 1), hence the factor \lambda^{|t| - j + 2}. In K_p the last letter closes the span, which leaves \lambda^2. ∎

Kernels for graphs

A kernel for graphs compares two graphs, for instance two molecules. A feature map that identifies graphs up to isomorphism is out of reach.

Proposition 7.5 — Hardness of complete graph kernels (Gärtner, Flach and Wrobel, 2003)

  1. If \varphi is injective on isomorphism classes, computing K(G, G') = \langle \varphi(G), \varphi(G') \rangle is at least as hard as deciding graph isomorphism.
  2. The kernel whose features count, for every graph F, the subgraphs of G isomorphic to F, is NP-hard to compute.

Proof.

  1. G \cong G' if and only if \varphi(G) = \varphi(G'), if and only if K(G, G) - 2K(G, G') + K(G', G') = 0 . Three kernel evaluations decide isomorphism.

  2. Sketch. From such kernel values one can extract the number of subgraphs isomorphic to the path on n vertices, which is positive iff G has a Hamiltonian path, an NP-complete property. ∎

Practical graph kernels therefore count simpler substructures: walks, paths or subtrees. Let G and G' have vertex labels (atom types, for instance).

Definition 7.6 — Product graph and random-walk kernel The product graph G_\times has one vertex per pair of vertices of G and G' with the same label. Two pairs are adjacent when both components are adjacent, in G and in G' respectively.

Let A_\times be its adjacency matrix; without labels, A_\times = A \otimes A'.

A walk of length k in G_\times is a pair of walks of length k in G and G' with the same label sequence. For 0 < \lambda < 1 / \rho(A_\times), the random-walk kernel is K_\times(G, G') = \sum_{k \geq 0} \lambda^k\, \mathbf{1}^\top A_\times^k\, \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}^\top (I - \lambda A_\times)^{-1} \mathbf{1} .

It is p.d.: the k-th term is \langle \varphi_k(G), \varphi_k(G') \rangle, where \varphi_k counts the walks of length k by label sequence.

For graphs with n vertices, a direct solve costs O(n^6); Sylvester-equation and conjugate-gradient methods bring it to about O(n^3) (Vishwanathan et al., 2010).

Algorithm 7.7 — Weisfeiler–Lehman subtree kernel (Shervashidze et al., 2011) Start from the vertex labels l_0(v). For h = 1, \dots, H:

  1. give each vertex the pair formed by its label l_{h-1}(v) and the sorted multiset of its neighbours’ labels l_{h-1}(u), u \in \mathcal{N}(v);
  2. compress each distinct pair into a new label l_h(v).

The feature vector \varphi(G) counts every label at every iteration 0, \dots, H, and K(G, G') = \langle \varphi(G), \varphi(G') \rangle. One graph costs O(H m) for m edges.

The kernel is fast and strong on molecule benchmarks, but graphs that the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman isomorphism test cannot tell apart get the same features. Message-passing graph neural networks share this limit (Xu, Hu, Leskovec and Jegelka, 2019; Morris et al., 2019).

Graph kernels also serve image analysis. A segmented image is a graph whose vertices are regions, labelled by their colour histograms and joined when the regions touch. Harchaoui and Bach (2007) compare such segmentation graphs with walk-based kernels, using a histogram kernel of ch. 6 between the labels of matched regions, and classify the images with an SVM.

Kernels on graphs

A kernel on a graph compares the vertices of one graph, for instance genes in an interaction network; it is an n \times n PSD matrix. Recall that the Laplacian L = D - A is PSD, with quadratic form f^\top L f = \sum_{uv \in E} (f_u - f_v)^2 .

Definition 7.8 — Diffusion and regularized Laplacian kernels For \beta > 0, the diffusion kernel (Kondor and Lafferty, 2002) and the regularized Laplacian kernel are K_\beta = e^{-\beta L} = \sum_{k \geq 0} \frac{(-\beta)^k}{k!}\, L^k, \qquad K'_\beta = (I + \beta L)^{-1} .

Both are PSD, with eigenvalues e^{-\beta \mu_i} and 1 / (1 + \beta \mu_i) for the eigenvalues \mu_i of L.

The diffusion kernel solves the heat equation \frac{d}{d\beta} K_\beta = -L K_\beta: entry (u, v) is the mass found at v at time \beta when heat starts at u.

Proposition 7.9 — RKHS norms on a graph (Smola and Kondor, 2003) Let K = \sum_i r(\mu_i)\, u_i u_i^\top with r > 0, where (\mu_i, u_i) are the eigenpairs of L. Its RKHS is the space of functions on the vertices, with \|f\|_K^2 = f^\top K^{-1} f = \sum_i \frac{\langle f, u_i \rangle^2}{r(\mu_i)} .

For the regularized Laplacian kernel, this norm is a smoothness penalty along the edges: \|f\|_{K'_\beta}^2 = \sum_{v} f_v^2 + \beta \sum_{uv \in E} (f_u - f_v)^2 .

Proof. Check the reproducing property: the column K_x = K e_x satisfies \langle f, K_x \rangle = f^\top K^{-1} K e_x = f_x. For K'_\beta, K^{-1} = I + \beta L, and the quadratic form of L concludes. ∎

Regression with these kernels spreads labels known on a few vertices to their neighbours; Vert and Kanehisa (2003) used diffusion kernels on gene networks.

            f["".join(x[i] for i in idx)] += lam ** (idx[-1] - idx[0] + 1)
        return f

    @lru_cache(maxsize=None)

            return 1.0
        if min(a, b) < i:
            return 0.0

        return r

        return r

8. Multiple kernel learning and kernel mean embeddings

Multiple kernel learning chooses the kernel itself, as a combination of candidate kernels learned with the predictor. Kernel mean embeddings represent probability distributions as points of an RKHS, which gives a distance between distributions, the MMD, and a two-sample test.

Multiple kernel learning

Given kernels K_1, \dots, K_M (one per data source, per feature group, or per bandwidth), consider the combinations K_\eta = \sum_{m=1}^M \eta_m K_m, \qquad \eta \in \Delta_M = \Big\{ \eta \geq 0,\; \sum_m \eta_m = 1 \Big\} . Multiple kernel learning (MKL; Lanckriet et al., 2004; Bach, Lanckriet and Jordan, 2004) minimizes the regularized risk jointly over \eta and f \in \mathcal{H}_{K_\eta}.

Proposition 8.1 — The RKHS of a sum of kernels If all \eta_m > 0, then \mathcal{H}_{K_\eta} is the set of sums f = \sum_m f_m with f_m \in \mathcal{H}_m = \mathcal{H}_{K_m}, and \|f\|_{K_\eta}^2 = \min \Big\{ \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{\|f_m\|_{\mathcal{H}_m}^2}{\eta_m} \;:\; f = \sum_m f_m \Big\} .

Proof. Sketch. Equip \mathcal{F} = \bigoplus_m \mathcal{H}_m with the norm \sum_m \|f_m\|^2 / \eta_m and map (f_m)_m to \sum_m f_m. With the quotient norm (the minimum above), the image is a Hilbert space of functions.

Key step: g_x = (\eta_m K_{m, x})_m represents evaluation at x, since \langle (f_m), g_x \rangle_{\mathcal{F}} = \sum_m f_m(x), and it maps to K_{\eta, x}. So the reproducing kernel of the image is K_\eta (Aronszajn, 1950). ∎

Theorem 8.2 — MKL is a group-sparse problem For any loss \mathcal{L} of the values f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n), \min_{\eta \in \Delta_M} \min_{f \in \mathcal{H}_{K_\eta}} \mathcal{L}(f) + \lambda \|f\|_{K_\eta}^2 = \min_{f_1, \dots, f_M} \mathcal{L}\Big(\sum_m f_m\Big) + \lambda \Big( \sum_{m=1}^M \|f_m\|_{\mathcal{H}_m} \Big)^2 .

Proof. By Proposition 8.1, the left side is the joint minimum over \eta and (f_m)_m of \mathcal{L}\Big(\sum_m f_m\Big) + \lambda \sum_m \frac{\|f_m\|^2}{\eta_m} . For fixed (f_m), Cauchy–Schwarz gives \Big( \sum_m \|f_m\| \Big)^2 = \Big( \sum_m \frac{\|f_m\|}{\sqrt{\eta_m}} \sqrt{\eta_m} \Big)^2 \leq \sum_m \frac{\|f_m\|^2}{\eta_m} \cdot \sum_m \eta_m , with equality for \eta_m \propto \|f_m\|. Minimizing over \eta first gives the right side. ∎

The penalty \sum_m \|f_m\| is a block \ell^1 norm, as in the group lasso. At the optimum many blocks vanish, together with their weights \eta_m: MKL selects kernels.

Proposition 8.3 — The MKL objective for kernel ridge regression With the squared loss, the inner minimum over f has the closed form J(\eta) = \min_{f} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda \|f\|_{K_\eta}^2 = \lambda\, y^\top (K_\eta + n\lambda I)^{-1} y .

J is convex on \Delta_M. With \alpha = (K_\eta + n\lambda I)^{-1} y, its partial derivatives are \frac{\partial J}{\partial \eta_m} = -\lambda\, \alpha^\top K_m \alpha .

Proof. With \alpha from Proposition 3.2, the residual is y - K_\eta \alpha = n\lambda\, \alpha. The two terms of the objective add up to \frac{1}{n} \|n\lambda\alpha\|^2 + \lambda\, \alpha^\top K_\eta \alpha = \lambda\, \alpha^\top (K_\eta + n\lambda I)\, \alpha = \lambda\, y^\top \alpha .

Convexity: y^\top X^{-1} y = \sup_v \big( 2 v^\top y - v^\top X v \big) is a supremum of affine functions of X, and X = K_\eta + n\lambda I is affine in \eta.

Gradient: d(X^{-1}) = -X^{-1} (dX) X^{-1} with dX = K_m\, d\eta_m. ∎

Gradient methods on the simplex therefore solve MKL; SimpleMKL (Rakotomamonjy, Bach, Canu and Grandvalet, 2008) uses an SVM in the inner loop. The code uses exponentiated gradient, with one kernel per input feature; the target ignores feature 3:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, lam = 200, 1e-2
X = rng.uniform(-2, 2, size=(n, 3))                 # feature 3 is pure noise
y = np.sin(2 * X[:, 0]) + 0.5 * X[:, 1] ** 2 + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=n)
y = y - y.mean()
gram = lambda a: np.exp(-(a[:, None] - a[None, :]) ** 2 / 2)
Ks = [gram(X[:, m]) for m in range(3)]              # one kernel per feature

def J_and_grad(eta):                                 # Proposition 8.3
    alpha = np.linalg.solve(sum(e * K for e, K in zip(eta, Ks)) + n * lam * np.eye(n), y)
    return lam * y @ alpha, np.array([-lam * alpha @ K @ alpha for K in Ks])

eta = np.ones(3) / 3
for it in range(300):                                # exponentiated gradient on the simplex
    J, g = J_and_grad(eta)
    eta = eta * np.exp(-g / np.abs(g).max())
    eta /= eta.sum()
    if it in (0, 10, 299):
        print(f"iteration {it:3d}: J = {J:.4f}, eta = {np.round(eta, 3)}")

The weights converge to about (0.63, 0.37, 0), the minimum found by a grid search: the noise kernel is switched off.

Kernel mean embeddings

Let K be a measurable kernel with \sup_x K(x, x) = \kappa^2 < \infty.

Definition 8.4 — Kernel mean embedding The mean embedding of a probability distribution P on \mathcal{X} is the element \mu_P \in \mathcal{H} such that \langle f, \mu_P \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\, f(X) \qquad \text{for all } f \in \mathcal{H} .

It exists by Riesz’s theorem, because |\mathbb{E} f(X)| \leq \kappa \|f\| (Proposition 2.2). As a function, \mu_P(y) = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P} K(X, y).

Definition 8.5 — Maximum mean discrepancy The MMD between P and Q is the RKHS distance between their embeddings, which is also the largest difference of expectations over the unit ball: \mathrm{MMD}(P, Q) = \|\mu_P - \mu_Q\|_{\mathcal{H}} = \sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}} \leq 1} \Big( \mathbb{E}_P f(X) - \mathbb{E}_Q f(Y) \Big) .

Expanding the square norm, with X, X' \sim P and Y, Y' \sim Q all independent: \mathrm{MMD}^2(P, Q) = \mathbb{E}\, K(X, X') + \mathbb{E}\, K(Y, Y') - 2\, \mathbb{E}\, K(X, Y) .

With the linear kernel, \mathrm{MMD}(P, Q) = \|\mathbb{E} X - \mathbb{E} Y\| compares only the means. A kernel is characteristic when P \mapsto \mu_P is injective, so that \mathrm{MMD}(P, Q) = 0 forces P = Q.

Theorem 8.6 — Characteristic translation-invariant kernels Let K(x, y) = \kappa(x - y) on \mathbb{R}^d with Bochner measure \Lambda (Theorem 6.4), and let \phi_P, \phi_Q be the characteristic functions of P and Q. Then \mathrm{MMD}^2(P, Q) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \big| \phi_P(\omega) - \phi_Q(\omega) \big|^2\, d\Lambda(\omega) .

If the support of \Lambda is \mathbb{R}^d, the kernel is characteristic. The Gaussian and Laplace kernels are characteristic.

Proof. By Bochner’s theorem and Fubini, \mathbb{E}\, K(X, Y) = \int \mathbb{E}\big[e^{i \omega^\top X}\big]\, \overline{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{i \omega^\top Y}\big]}\, d\Lambda(\omega) = \int \phi_P\, \overline{\phi_Q}\, d\Lambda . The other two terms are computed in the same way, and expanding |\phi_P - \phi_Q|^2 gives the formula.

If the MMD is 0, then \phi_P = \phi_Q \Lambda-almost everywhere; by continuity they agree on the support of \Lambda, that is everywhere, and P = Q. Sriperumbudur et al. (2010) prove that full support is also necessary. ∎

Proposition 8.7 — Estimating the embedding and the MMD Let x_1, \dots, x_m \sim P and y_1, \dots, y_n \sim Q be independent samples.

  1. The empirical embedding \hat\mu_P = \frac{1}{m} \sum_i K_{x_i} satisfies \mathbb{E} \|\hat\mu_P - \mu_P\|^2 \leq \kappa^2 / m, in any dimension.
  2. The following statistic is an unbiased estimator of \mathrm{MMD}^2(P, Q): \widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}{}^2_u = \frac{\sum_{i \neq j} K(x_i, x_j)}{m(m-1)} + \frac{\sum_{i \neq j} K(y_i, y_j)}{n(n-1)} - \frac{2}{mn} \sum_{i, j} K(x_i, y_j) .

Proof.

  1. The terms K_{x_i} - \mu_P are independent and centred in \mathcal{H}, so the cross terms vanish and \mathbb{E} \|\hat\mu_P - \mu_P\|^2 = \frac{1}{m}\, \mathbb{E} \|K_X - \mu_P\|^2 = \frac{1}{m} \big( \mathbb{E} K(X, X) - \|\mu_P\|^2 \big) \leq \frac{\kappa^2}{m} .

  2. Each sum averages a kernel over independent pairs, so its expectation is the corresponding term of Definition 8.5. ∎

The kernel two-sample test (Gretton, Borgwardt, Rasch, Schölkopf and Smola, 2012) rejects P = Q when the statistic is large. Under the null hypothesis the pooled sample is exchangeable, so permutations give an exact p-value.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def mmd2_u(K, m):                     # Proposition 8.7 from the Gram matrix of [x; y]
    Kxx, Kyy, Kxy = K[:m, :m], K[m:, m:], K[:m, m:]
    n = len(K) - m
    return ((Kxx.sum() - np.trace(Kxx)) / (m * (m - 1))
            + (Kyy.sum() - np.trace(Kyy)) / (n * (n - 1)) - 2 * Kxy.mean())

def mmd_test(x, y, n_perm=500):
    z = np.r_[x, y]
    d2 = (z[:, None] - z[None, :]) ** 2
    K = np.exp(-d2 / (2 * np.median(d2[d2 > 0])))  # Gaussian kernel, median heuristic
    stat = mmd2_u(K, len(x))
    null = [mmd2_u(K[np.ix_(p, p)], len(x))
            for p in (rng.permutation(len(z)) for _ in range(n_perm))]
    return stat, (1 + np.sum(np.array(null) >= stat)) / (1 + n_perm)

m = 200
x = rng.normal(0, 1, m)
for name, y in [("N(0,1) vs N(0,1)", rng.normal(0, 1, m)),
                ("N(0,1) vs Laplace", rng.laplace(0, 1 / np.sqrt(2), m))]:  # same mean and variance
    stat, p = mmd_test(x, y)
    print(f"{name:18s}: MMD^2_u = {stat:+.4f}, permutation p = {p:.3f}, "
          f"t-test p = {stats.ttest_ind(x, y).pvalue:.2f}")

With equal means and variances, a t-test sees nothing (p = 0.64); the MMD test detects the Laplace shape (p = 0.002) and does not reject for two Gaussian samples (p = 0.13).

Key takeaways — chapter 8

  • The RKHS of \sum_m \eta_m K_m has norm \min \sum_m \|f_m\|^2 / \eta_m; optimizing \eta turns MKL into a group-sparse problem that selects kernels.
  • For KRR, the MKL objective \lambda\, y^\top (K_\eta + n\lambda I)^{-1} y is convex in \eta, with gradient -\lambda\, \alpha^\top K_m \alpha.
  • The mean embedding \mu_P = \mathbb{E} K_X turns distributions into points; the MMD \|\mu_P - \mu_Q\| is a metric for characteristic kernels, estimated at rate 1/\sqrt{m} and tested by permutation.

9. Large-scale learning with kernels

Exact kernel methods need O(n^2) memory and O(n^3) time; with n = 10^5 points the Gram matrix alone takes 80 GB. This chapter replaces it by low-rank approximations, random features or Nyström, and states how small the rank can be without losing accuracy.

Both methods build an explicit feature map z : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^D with z(x)^\top z(y) \approx K(x, y). KRR then becomes ridge regression on z, with weights w = (Z^\top Z + n\lambda I_D)^{-1} Z^\top y, where Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times D} stacks the z(x_i)^\top. This costs O(nD^2 + D^3) time and O(nD) memory.

Random Fourier features

Let K(x, y) = \kappa(x - y) with \kappa(0) = 1. By Bochner’s theorem (Theorem 6.4), \Lambda is then a probability distribution, and since it is symmetric, \kappa(z) = \mathbb{E}_{\omega \sim \Lambda} \cos(\omega^\top z) .

Proposition 9.1 — Random Fourier features (Rahimi and Recht, 2007) Draw \omega_1, \dots, \omega_D \sim \Lambda and b_1, \dots, b_D \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 2\pi] independently, and set z(x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{D}}\, \big( \cos(\omega_j^\top x + b_j) \big)_{j = 1, \dots, D} . Then z(x)^\top z(y) is an unbiased estimator of K(x, y).

Proof. Each term is 2 \cos(\omega^\top x + b) \cos(\omega^\top y + b) / D. By the product formula, 2 \cos(u + b) \cos(v + b) = \cos(u - v) + \cos(u + v + 2b) . The second term has mean 0 over b. The first has mean \kappa(x - y) over \omega, by the formula above. ∎

For the Gaussian kernel, \Lambda = \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^{-2} I): the features form a one-hidden-layer network with random, fixed first-layer weights.

Proposition 9.2 — Pointwise concentration For fixed x, y and every \varepsilon > 0, \mathbb{P}\Big( \big| z(x)^\top z(y) - K(x, y) \big| \geq \varepsilon \Big) \leq 2 \exp\Big( -\frac{D \varepsilon^2}{8} \Big) .

Proof. z(x)^\top z(y) is the average of D independent variables 2 \cos(\omega_j^\top x + b_j) \cos(\omega_j^\top y + b_j), each in [-2, 2]. Hoeffding’s inequality for a range of 4 gives 2 \exp(-2D\varepsilon^2 / 16). ∎

A union bound over a net extends this to all pairs in a compact set of diameter \ell. A number of features D of order d\, \varepsilon^{-2} \log(\sigma_\Lambda \ell / \varepsilon) gives a uniform error \varepsilon, where \sigma_\Lambda^2 is the second moment of \Lambda (Rahimi and Recht, 2007). The error decreases only like D^{-1/2}, which is why the statistical analysis below matters.

The Nyström method

The Nyström method (Williams and Seeger, 2001) picks m landmarks \tilde x_1, \dots, \tilde x_m, usually a uniform subsample. With K_{nm} the kernel between data and landmarks and K_{mm} among landmarks, it approximates K \approx \tilde K = K_{nm}\, K_{mm}^{+}\, K_{mn} .

Proposition 9.3 — Nyström is a projection Let P be the orthogonal projection of \mathcal{H} onto \mathrm{span}\{K_{\tilde x_1}, \dots, K_{\tilde x_m}\}. Then \tilde K(x, y) = \langle P K_x, P K_y \rangle_{\mathcal{H}} . In particular K - \tilde K is PSD: it is the Gram matrix of the residuals (I - P) K_x.

Proof. Write k_m(x) = (K(\tilde x_j, x))_j. The projection P K_x = \sum_j c_j K_{\tilde x_j} satisfies the normal equations K_{mm}\, c = k_m(x), so c = K_{mm}^{+} k_m(x). Then \langle P K_x, P K_y \rangle = c(x)^\top K_{mm}\, c(y) = k_m(x)^\top K_{mm}^{+}\, k_m(y) . Finally, \langle K_x, K_y \rangle is the sum of the inner products of the projections and of the residuals, because the cross terms vanish: (I - P) K_y \perp P K_x. ∎

Proposition 9.4 — Nyström kernel ridge regression Minimizing the KRR objective over f = \sum_j \beta_j K_{\tilde x_j} gives \big( K_{mn} K_{nm} + n\lambda\, K_{mm} \big)\, \beta = K_{mn}\, y, at a cost of O(nm^2 + m^3) time and O(nm) memory.

Proof. On this subspace, the fitted values are K_{nm} \beta and the squared norm is \beta^\top K_{mm} \beta. Setting to zero the gradient of the objective \frac{1}{n} \|y - K_{nm}\beta\|^2 + \lambda\, \beta^\top K_{mm} \beta gives the system. ∎

FALKON (Rudi, Carratino and Rosasco, 2017) solves this problem by preconditioned conjugate gradient, in about O(n \sqrt{n}) time and O(n) memory.

How many features are enough?

Theorem 9.5 — Computational regularization (informal statement) Consider KRR with a bounded kernel and a regression function in the RKHS, where exact KRR with \lambda \asymp n^{-1/2} attains an excess risk of order n^{-1/2}. Then:

  • Nyström KRR with m of order \sqrt{n} \log n uniformly sampled landmarks attains the same order (Rudi, Camoriano and Rosasco, 2015);
  • KRR on random features with D of order \sqrt{n} \log n features attains the same order (Rudi and Rosasco, 2017).

Under stronger assumptions on the eigenvalue decay and on the regularity of the target, fast rates hold with correspondingly adapted m or D.

Proof. Idea. The approximation error only needs to be small in the directions that KRR does not already shrink, about \mathrm{df}(\lambda) of them (Proposition 3.4); concentration inequalities for random operators control it. See the cited papers, and Bach (2013) for leverage scores.

The rank acts as a second regularization parameter.

import time
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d, n, s, lam = 5, 3000, 1.5, 1e-4
f_star = lambda X: np.sin(X[:, 0]) + np.cos(X[:, 1] * X[:, 2]) + 0.5 * X[:, 3]
X, Xt = rng.normal(size=(n, d)), rng.normal(size=(1000, d))
y = f_star(X) + 0.2 * rng.normal(size=n)

def gauss(A, B):
    d2 = (A**2).sum(1)[:, None] + (B**2).sum(1)[None, :] - 2 * A @ B.T
    return np.exp(-d2 / (2 * s**2))

def report(name, pred, t0):
    err = np.mean((pred - f_star(Xt)) ** 2)
    print(f"{name:22s} test MSE {err:.4f}   time {time.time() - t0:.2f} s")

t0 = time.time()                                       # exact KRR: O(n^3)
alpha = np.linalg.solve(gauss(X, X) + n * lam * np.eye(n), y)
report("exact KRR", gauss(Xt, X) @ alpha, t0)

for D in [50, 200, 800]:
    t0 = time.time()                                   # random Fourier features
    W, b = rng.normal(scale=1 / s, size=(d, D)), rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, D)
    z = lambda A: np.sqrt(2 / D) * np.cos(A @ W + b)
    Z = z(X)
    w = np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ Z + n * lam * np.eye(D), Z.T @ y)
    report(f"random features D={D}", z(Xt) @ w, t0)

    t0 = time.time()                                   # Nystrom KRR, Proposition 9.4
    land = X[rng.choice(n, D, replace=False)]
    Knm, Kmm = gauss(X, land), gauss(land, land)
    beta = np.linalg.solve(Knm.T @ Knm + n * lam * Kmm + 1e-8 * np.eye(D), Knm.T @ y)
    report(f"Nystrom m={D}", gauss(Xt, land) @ beta, t0)

With n = 3000, exact KRR is by far the slowest and reaches a test error of 0.024. Nyström with m = 800 nearly matches it (0.025) in a fraction of the time; random features, sampled independently of the data, reach 0.036 at D = 800.

Key takeaways — chapter 9

  • Random Fourier features sample the Bochner measure: \sqrt{2/D}\cos(\omega^\top x + b) gives an unbiased, D^{-1/2}-accurate approximation of a translation-invariant kernel.
  • Nyström projects the features onto the span of m landmarks; the approximation K_{nm} K_{mm}^{+} K_{mn} is PSD-dominated by K, and Nyström KRR solves an m \times m system.
  • About \sqrt{n} \log n features or landmarks keep the n^{-1/2} rate of exact KRR: the rank is a regularization parameter.

10. Deep learning with kernels

Kernels and neural networks meet in two ways. Multilayer kernels, once approximated, are networks: convolutional kernel networks. Conversely, infinitely wide random networks are kernel machines: the arc-cosine kernel and the neural tangent kernel.

Compositional and arc-cosine kernels

Proposition 10.1 — Composing kernels Let K_1 be a p.d. kernel with K_1(x, x) > 0, and let \kappa(t) = \sum_{k \geq 0} b_k t^k with all b_k \geq 0 and convergence on [-1, 1]. Write \bar K_1(x, y) = K_1(x, y) / \sqrt{K_1(x, x) K_1(y, y)}. Then the following kernel is p.d.: K_2(x, y) = \sqrt{K_1(x, x)\, K_1(y, y)}\;\; \kappa\big( \bar K_1(x, y) \big) .

It is the dot-product kernel \|u\| \|v\|\, \kappa(\langle u, v \rangle / \|u\| \|v\|) applied to the feature vectors u = \varphi_1(x) and v = \varphi_1(y).

Proof. \bar K_1 is p.d. by Proposition 1.4 (item 4, with f = K_1(x, x)^{-1/2}). Its powers, their non-negative combinations and their limits are p.d. (items 1–3), hence \kappa(\bar K_1) is p.d. Multiplying by f(x) f(y) with f = \sqrt{K_1(x, x)} concludes. ∎

Iterating gives a multilayer kernel K_{l+1} computed from K_l alone. The Gaussian kernel restricted to the unit sphere is of this form, since there e^{-\|u - v\|^2 / 2\sigma^2} = e^{(\langle u, v \rangle - 1)/\sigma^2} .

Theorem 10.2 — The arc-cosine kernel (Cho and Saul, 2009) Let w \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) and let \theta be the angle between x and y. Then \mathbb{E}\big[ \mathrm{relu}(w^\top x)\, \mathrm{relu}(w^\top y) \big] = \frac{\|x\| \|y\|}{2\pi} \big( \sin\theta + (\pi - \theta) \cos\theta \big), \mathbb{E}\big[ \mathbb{1}[w^\top x > 0]\; \mathbb{1}[w^\top y > 0] \big] = \frac{\pi - \theta}{2\pi} .

So a one-hidden-layer ReLU network with D random Gaussian weights w_j computes random features, whose kernel converges to the first expression as D \to \infty: \frac{1}{D} \sum_{j=1}^D \mathrm{relu}(w_j^\top x)\, \mathrm{relu}(w_j^\top y) \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{E}\big[ \mathrm{relu}(w^\top x)\, \mathrm{relu}(w^\top y) \big] .

Proof. Only the projection of w on the plane of the two inputs matters; it is a standard Gaussian vector of \mathbb{R}^2. In polar coordinates, write w = r(\cos\phi, \sin\phi), x = \|x\|(1, 0) and y = \|y\|(\cos\theta, \sin\theta).

The angle \phi is uniform on the circle and independent of r, and \mathbb{E} r^2 = 2.

  • Both inner products are positive exactly when \phi \in (\theta - \pi/2, \pi/2), an arc of length \pi - \theta. This gives the second formula.
  • For the first, compute \int_{\theta - \pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos\phi\, \cos(\phi - \theta)\, d\phi = \frac{1}{2} \big( \sin\theta + (\pi - \theta) \cos\theta \big), and multiply by \mathbb{E}r^2 / (2\pi) \cdot \|x\| \|y\| = \|x\| \|y\| / \pi. ∎

If the output weights are also Gaussian, the network output is, in the limit of infinite width, a Gaussian process with this covariance (Neal, 1996). The same holds for deep networks with the compositional recursion above (Lee et al., 2018).

Convolutional kernel networks

An image is a map x : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^p on a grid, whose value at each position is a patch. A convolutional kernel compares all pairs of patches with a dot-product kernel, and its feature map is approximated by projection, as in ch. 9.

Definition 10.3 — A convolutional kernel network layer (Mairal et al., 2014; Mairal, 2016) Fix a dot-product kernel on patches, with \tilde z = z / \|z\|, k(z, z') = \|z\|\, \|z'\|\; \kappa(\langle \tilde z, \tilde z' \rangle), and anchor points Z = [z_1, \dots, z_m] on the unit sphere. The layer maps each patch z to \psi(z) = \|z\|\; \kappa(Z^\top Z)^{-1/2}\; \kappa(Z^\top \tilde z) \in \mathbb{R}^m , with \kappa applied entrywise, then applies a linear Gaussian pooling over positions. The next layer extracts patches from the pooled map.

By Proposition 9.3, \psi(z)^\top \psi(z') is the Nyström approximation of k(z, z') with landmarks z_j: here K_{mm} = \kappa(Z^\top Z) and k_m(z) = \|z\|\, \kappa(Z^\top \tilde z). The layer is therefore a CNN layer in disguise:

  • a convolution with the filters z_j;
  • a pointwise non-linearity \kappa;
  • a 1 \times 1 linear map \kappa(Z^\top Z)^{-1/2};
  • pooling.

The anchors can be learned without labels (k-means on patches) or end-to-end by back-propagation, which gives the supervised CKN of Mairal (2016).

Bietti and Mairal (2019a) show that these representations are stable to small deformations, and that the RKHS norm controls the complexity of CNNs.

The neural tangent kernel

Let f(x; \theta) be a network with parameters \theta \in \mathbb{R}^p, initialized at \theta_0. Its neural tangent kernel (NTK) is the kernel of the feature map \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_0), p.d. by Proposition 1.2: \Theta(x, x') = \big\langle \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_0),\; \nabla_\theta f(x'; \theta_0) \big\rangle .

Proposition 10.4 — Training the linearized network is kernel regression Let f_{\mathrm{lin}} be the linearization of the network at \theta_0, f_{\mathrm{lin}}(x; \theta) = f(x; \theta_0) + \nabla_\theta f(x; \theta_0)^\top (\theta - \theta_0), trained by gradient flow on the loss \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n \big( f_{\mathrm{lin}}(x_i; \theta) - y_i \big)^2 .

Let \Theta be the NTK Gram matrix on the training points, assumed invertible, and u(t) = f_{\mathrm{lin}}(X; \theta_t) - y. Then u(t) = e^{-t\Theta} u(0), and for every x: f_{\mathrm{lin}}(x; \theta_t) = f(x; \theta_0) + \Theta(x, X)\, \Theta^{-1} \big( I - e^{-t\Theta} \big) \big( y - f(X; \theta_0) \big) .

As t \to \infty, this is kernel regression without regularization, with the NTK, on the residuals of the initial network.

Proof. Let J \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} have rows \nabla_\theta f(x_i; \theta_0)^\top, so that \Theta = J J^\top. The gradient flow is \dot\theta = -J^\top u, hence \dot u = J \dot\theta = -\Theta u and u(t) = e^{-t\Theta} u(0).

For a new point, f_{\mathrm{lin}}(x; \theta_t) - f(x; \theta_0) = -\Theta(x, X) \int_0^t e^{-s\Theta} u(0)\, ds = -\Theta(x, X)\, \Theta^{-1} (I - e^{-t\Theta})\, u(0), and u(0) = f(X; \theta_0) - y. ∎

Jacot, Gabriel and Hongler (2018) show that, as the width grows with a suitable scaling, the NTK becomes deterministic and constant during training: the trained network is the kernel regressor above.

Chizat, Oyallon and Bach (2019) show that this lazy regime, in which the features barely move, is an effect of the scaling of the model. Networks that leave it and learn features can outperform any fixed kernel (ch. 12).

Example 10.5 — The NTK of a two-layer ReLU network Take a two-layer network of width m, with a_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) and w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d): f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}} \sum_{j=1}^m a_j\, \mathrm{relu}(w_j^\top x) .

  • The gradient in a_j is \mathrm{relu}(w_j^\top x) / \sqrt{m}; its contribution to \Theta tends to the first formula of Theorem 10.2.
  • The gradient in w_j is a_j\, \mathbb{1}[w_j^\top x > 0]\, x / \sqrt{m}; since \mathbb{E} a_j^2 = 1, its contribution tends to x^\top x' times the second formula.

In the limit, \Theta_\infty(x, x') = \frac{\|x\| \|x'\|}{2\pi} \big( \sin\theta + (\pi - \theta)\cos\theta \big) + x^\top x'\, \frac{\pi - \theta}{2\pi} .

The code checks Theorem 10.2 by Monte Carlo, and Example 10.5 with PyTorch on a network of width 20,000:

import numpy as np
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
d, m = 3, 20000
X = torch.randn(5, d, dtype=torch.float64)

def arccos_terms(X):                       # the two formulas of Theorem 10.2
    nrm = X.norm(dim=1)
    cos = (X @ X.T / torch.outer(nrm, nrm)).clamp(-1, 1)
    th = torch.arccos(cos)
    k1 = torch.outer(nrm, nrm) * (torch.sin(th) + (np.pi - th) * cos) / (2 * np.pi)
    return k1, (np.pi - th) / (2 * np.pi)

k1, k0 = arccos_terms(X)
F = torch.relu(X @ torch.randn(d, 200000, dtype=torch.float64))
print("arc-cosine kernel: max |Monte Carlo - formula| = %.4f" % (F @ F.T / 200000 - k1).abs().max())

W = torch.randn(m, d, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
a = torch.randn(m, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
net = lambda x: a @ torch.relu(W @ x) / np.sqrt(m)
G = torch.stack([torch.cat([g.ravel() for g in torch.autograd.grad(net(x), (W, a))]) for x in X])
ntk_emp, ntk_inf = G @ G.T, k1 + (X @ X.T) * k0     # Example 10.5
print("NTK: relative error, width %d vs infinite width = %.4f"
      % (m, (ntk_emp - ntk_inf).norm() / ntk_inf.norm()))

The Monte Carlo error is below 0.01, and the finite-width NTK is within 1 % of its limit; its fluctuations are of order m^{-1/2}.

Key takeaways — chapter 10

  • Dot-product functions with non-negative Taylor coefficients compose kernels layer by layer; ReLU random features give the arc-cosine kernel.
  • A convolutional kernel network layer is the Nyström approximation of a patch kernel: convolution, non-linearity, linear mixing and pooling.
  • The NTK is the kernel of the gradient features; training the linearized network is kernel regression with it, and infinitely wide networks under the NTK scaling stay in this lazy regime.

Why the RKHS norm controls overfitting (complement to ch. 3)

The ball \mathcal{F}_B of radius B in \mathcal{H} is infinite-dimensional, yet its complexity on a sample is small. The empirical Rademacher complexity, with i.i.d. random signs \varepsilon_i, is \hat{\mathfrak{R}}_n(\mathcal{F}_B) = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}_B} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \varepsilon_i\, f(x_i) .

Proposition 11.1 — Rademacher complexity of an RKHS ball \hat{\mathfrak{R}}_n(\mathcal{F}_B) \leq \frac{B}{n} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n K(x_i, x_i)} \leq \frac{B \kappa}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad \text{if } K(x, x) \leq \kappa^2 .

Proof. By the reproducing property, \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \varepsilon_i f(x_i) = \Big\langle f, \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \varepsilon_i K_{x_i} \Big\rangle . The supremum over the ball is \frac{B}{n} \| \sum_i \varepsilon_i K_{x_i} \|, by Cauchy–Schwarz with equality.

Jensen’s inequality bounds its expectation by the square root of \mathbb{E} \Big\| \sum_i \varepsilon_i K_{x_i} \Big\|^2 = \sum_{i,j} \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i \varepsilon_j]\, K(x_i, x_j) = \sum_i K(x_i, x_i) . ∎

The bound does not depend on the dimension of the feature space. Combined with the standard Rademacher generalization bound for Lipschitz losses (Bartlett and Mendelson, 2002), it shows that the gap between empirical and true risk over \mathcal{F}_B is of order B\kappa / \sqrt{n}. The regularization \lambda \|f\|^2 is the Lagrangian form of the constraint \|f\| \leq B.

Gaussian processes and other losses (complement to ch. 3)

Two connections

  • Gaussian processes. If f is a centred Gaussian process with covariance K and y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i with noise variance n\lambda, the posterior mean of f(x) is exactly \hat f(x). See Bayesian Machine Learning.
  • Other losses. The representer theorem holds for any loss. With the logistic loss \log(1 + e^{-y f(x)}), Newton’s method on \alpha solves a weighted KRR problem at each step: this is kernel logistic regression.

Consistency and solvers for the SVM (complement to ch. 4)

The hinge loss is a convex upper bound of the 0–1 loss \mathbb{1}[y\, g(x) \leq 0]. With a universal kernel such as the Gaussian kernel, the SVM is consistent: its risk converges to the Bayes risk for a suitable C = C_n (Steinwart, 2001).

Solving the dual The dual is a quadratic program with one equality constraint. Sequential minimal optimization (SMO, Platt, 1999) updates two multipliers at a time, which keeps \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 and has a closed-form solution. Each step costs O(n) kernel evaluations with a cached Gram matrix; this is the algorithm of LIBSVM, behind scikit-learn’s SVC. Multiclass problems are usually solved by combining binary SVMs (one-versus-rest or one-versus-one).

The RKHS of a translation-invariant kernel (complement to ch. 6)

Bochner’s theorem (Theorem 6.4) also describes the RKHS of \kappa(x - y): its norm is a weighted energy in the frequency domain.

Proposition 11.2 — The RKHS of a translation-invariant kernel Suppose \kappa is integrable with a positive Fourier transform \hat\kappa. With \hat f(\omega) = \int f(x) e^{-i \omega^\top x} dx, the RKHS is the space of continuous f \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^d) with finite norm \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_K}^2 = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^d} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \frac{|\hat f(\omega)|^2}{\hat\kappa(\omega)}\, d\omega .

Proof. Check of the reproducing property. The Fourier transform of K_x = \kappa(\cdot - x) is \hat\kappa(\omega) e^{-i \omega^\top x}. The inner product associated with the norm gives \langle f, K_x \rangle = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^d} \int \hat f(\omega)\, e^{i \omega^\top x}\, d\omega = f(x) by Fourier inversion. Uniqueness of the RKHS concludes. ∎

The penalty 1 / \hat\kappa(\omega) is a frequency weighting. For the Gaussian kernel it grows like e^{\sigma^2 \|\omega\|^2 / 2}: functions of its RKHS are extremely smooth. For the Laplace kernel on \mathbb{R}, \hat\kappa(\omega) = 2\sigma / (1 + \sigma^2 \omega^2), and Plancherel’s identity turns the norm into a Sobolev norm: \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_K}^2 = \frac{1}{2\sigma} \int f^2 + \frac{\sigma}{2} \int f'^2 .

More kernels for sequences (complement to ch. 7)

Other sequence kernels

  • Convolution kernels (Haussler, 1999) sum products of kernels over all decompositions of two objects into parts; the subsequence kernel is one of them.
  • The Smith–Waterman alignment score is not a p.d. kernel. The local alignment kernel (Saigo, Vert, Ueda and Akutsu, 2004) sums e^{\beta \cdot \text{score}} over all alignments instead of taking the best one, and is p.d.
  • The Fisher kernel (Jaakkola and Haussler, 1999) uses the gradient of the log-likelihood of a generative model (such as an HMM) as the feature vector.

Uses of mean embeddings (complement to ch. 8)

Uses of mean embeddings

  • Independence testing: the Hilbert–Schmidt independence criterion is the MMD between a joint distribution and the product of its marginals, with a product kernel (Gretton, Bousquet, Smola and Schölkopf, 2005).
  • Generative models: the MMD to the data distribution can be minimized over the parameters of a generator (Li, Swersky and Zemel, 2015; Dziugaite, Roy and Ghahramani, 2015).
  • Conditional embeddings represent P(Y \mid X = x). They support kernel filtering for dynamical systems (Song, Huang, Smola and Fukumizu, 2009) and a kernel version of Bayes’ rule (Fukumizu, Song and Gretton, 2013). Muandet et al. (2017) survey the field.

Key takeaways — chapter 11

  • An RKHS ball of radius B has Rademacher complexity at most B\kappa / \sqrt{n}, whatever the dimension of the feature space.
  • KRR is the posterior mean of a Gaussian process; the SVM with a universal kernel is consistent and is solved by SMO.
  • For a translation-invariant kernel, the RKHS norm weights frequencies by 1/\hat\kappa: Gaussian RKHS functions are very smooth, the Laplace kernel gives a Sobolev space.
  • Convolution, alignment and Fisher kernels extend the string kernels of ch. 7; mean embeddings support independence tests, generative models and conditional inference.

12. Research directions and open problems

This chapter is for self-study. It surveys active research questions that build on chapters 3–10, with pointers to papers whose authors and year are given in the references. It is a map, not a review: each item says what the question is and where to start reading.

Kernel methods at the scale of modern data

  • Preconditioned solvers made exact-accuracy kernel regression practical on millions of points: FALKON (Rudi, Carratino and Rosasco, 2017) and its multi-GPU implementation for billions of points (Meanti, Carratino, Rosasco and Rudi, 2020). EigenPro (Ma and Belkin, 2017) follows a related route with preconditioned stochastic gradient.
  • Open questions include the choice of landmarks beyond uniform sampling (leverage scores, Bach, 2013; El Alaoui and Mahoney, 2015), kernel learning at this scale, and the trade-off between precision, memory and communication on distributed hardware.

Interpolation and generalization

  • Belkin, Ma and Mandal (2018) observed that kernel machines that interpolate noisy training data can still generalize, as deep networks do. Liang and Rakhlin (2020) analysed minimum-norm interpolation with kernels in high dimension.
  • A precise picture of when interpolation is benign, as a function of dimension, sample size and kernel spectrum, is still being worked out; random matrix theory is one of the tools (see Large Random Matrices).

Beyond the lazy regime

  • The NTK explains some aspects of wide networks but not feature learning. Chizat, Oyallon and Bach (2019) characterized lazy training; Ghorbani, Mei, Misiakiewicz and Montanari (2021) quantified the gap between linearized and trained two-layer networks in high dimension.
  • Bietti and Mairal (2019b) studied the inductive bias of the NTK (smoothness, stability to deformations). How much of the success of deep networks a well-designed kernel can recover remains open.

Kernels for distributions, sampling and generation

  • The MMD has become a training criterion and a geometry on distributions. Arbel, Korba, Salim and Gretton (2019) studied the MMD gradient flow and its convergence; Bińkowski, Sutherland, Arbel and Gretton (2018) analysed MMD GANs.
  • Kernel Stein discrepancies (Chwialkowski, Strathmann and Gretton, 2016; Liu, Lee and Jordan, 2016) test goodness of fit without normalizing constants, and Stein variational gradient descent (Liu and Wang, 2016) turns them into a particle sampler. Langevin sampling, studied in Stochastic Calculus in Machine Learning, and the gradient flows of Optimal Transport are the natural points of comparison.

Kernels as models of non-negative functions and for optimization

  • Marteau-Ferey, Bach and Rudi (2020) represent non-negative functions as quadratic forms in feature space, f(x) = \varphi(x)^\top A\, \varphi(x) with A \succeq 0, which keeps a representer theorem and convexity. Densities and global optimization are natural applications (Rudi, Marteau-Ferey and Bach, 2020).

Structured data and attention

  • Convolutional kernel networks have been extended to sequences (Chen, Jacob and Mairal, 2019) and graphs (Chen, Jacob and Mairal, 2020). Graph kernels and graph neural networks share the Weisfeiler–Lehman limit (Morris et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019).
  • Attention in transformers can be read as a kernel smoother (Tsai et al., 2019), and random features give linear-time approximations of softmax attention (Choromanski et al., 2021).

Questions to explore

  1. Can the kernel itself be learned from data with guarantees comparable to those of fixed kernels, beyond the convex combinations of ch. 8?
  2. What is the best rank–accuracy trade-off for kernel approximation when the data lie near a low-dimensional manifold?
  3. How should a kernel encode known invariances (rotations, permutations, deformations) while keeping a tractable RKHS norm?
  4. When is the MMD the right discrepancy for generative modelling, and how should its bandwidth adapt during training?

Key takeaways — chapter 12

  • Preconditioned Nyström solvers make kernel methods competitive at very large scale; the choice of landmarks is still an active topic.
  • Interpolation, lazy training and feature learning are where kernel theory meets deep learning theory.
  • Mean embeddings, Stein discrepancies and non-negative kernel models extend kernels to sampling, generation and optimization.

    Solution: (a) with the mean feature \bar\varphi = \frac{1}{n} \sum_l \varphi_l, expanding gives \langle \varphi_i - \bar\varphi, \varphi_j - \bar\varphi \rangle = K_{ij} - \frac{1}{n} \sum_l K_{il} - \frac{1}{n} \sum_l K_{lj} + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{l, k} K_{lk} = (HKH)_{ij} .

    1. H\mathbf{1} = 0 gives \mathbf{1}^\top K_c = 0, and the scores are K_c \alpha_k, so they sum to 0.

    2. Replace \varphi_j by \varphi(x) in (a): \tilde K(x_i, x) = K(x_i, x) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_l K(x_l, x) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_l K(x_i, x_l) + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{l, k} K_{lk} .

  9. ★★ Regularized kernel CCA. (a) Show that the regularized canonical correlation of Proposition 5.7 is strictly less than 1 when \gamma > 0. (b) Show that, as \gamma \to \infty, the canonical directions tend to those of the singular value problem for K_1 K_2, and interpret.

    Solution: (a) R_j has eigenvalues \mu / (\mu + \gamma) < 1, so \|R_1 R_2\| \leq \|R_1\|\, \|R_2\| < 1.

    1. \gamma R_j = K_j (I + K_j / \gamma)^{-1} \to K_j. So \gamma^2 R_1 R_2 \to K_1 K_2, and the singular vectors of R_1 R_2 tend to those of K_1 K_2.

    The criterion becomes the covariance \alpha^\top K_1 K_2 \beta under \|\alpha\| = \|\beta\| = 1: strong regularization turns kernel CCA into a kernel partial least squares, which maximizes covariance instead of correlation.

  10. ★★ The Laplace kernel through Schoenberg. For x \in \mathbb{R}, let g_x = \mathbb{1}_{[0, x)} if x \geq 0 and g_x = -\mathbb{1}_{[x, 0)} if x < 0. Show that |x - y| = \|g_x - g_y\|_{L^2}^2.

    Deduce that e^{-t |x - y|} and e^{-t \|x - y\|_1} are p.d. on \mathbb{R} and \mathbb{R}^d.

    Solution: whatever the signs of the two points, g_x - g_y equals \pm 1 between x and y and 0 elsewhere. Its squared norm is therefore |x - y|.

    A squared distance between feature vectors is negative definite (Definition 6.5), so Theorem 6.6 gives e^{-t|x - y|} p.d. On \mathbb{R}^d, \|x - y\|_1 = \sum_k |x_k - y_k| is a sum of negative definite kernels, hence negative definite, and Theorem 6.6 applies again.

  11. ★★ Unlabelled walk kernels are weak. Without labels, show that \mathbf{1}^\top (A \otimes A')^k \mathbf{1} is the product w_k(G)\, w_k(G'), where w_k(G) = \mathbf{1}^\top A^k \mathbf{1} counts the walks of length k in G.

    Deduce that all d-regular graphs on n vertices have the same random-walk features, and explain the normalized values close to 0.99 printed in ch. 7.

    Solution: the mixed-product rule gives (A \otimes A')^k = A^k \otimes A'^k, and \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}_n \otimes \mathbf{1}_{n'}. Conclude with the identity (u \otimes v)^\top (M \otimes N)(u \otimes v) = (u^\top M u)(v^\top N v) .

    For a d-regular graph, A\mathbf{1} = d\mathbf{1}, so w_k = n d^k for every such graph.

    The kernel is \sum_k \lambda^k w_k(G) w_k(G'), an inner product of walk-count sequences. These sequences grow geometrically and are nearly parallel for graphs of similar size, hence normalized values close to 1.

  12. ★★ The MKL objective and its optimality conditions. Starting from Proposition 8.3, write the optimality conditions of \min_{\eta \in \Delta_M} J(\eta) and interpret them.

    Solution: J is convex and differentiable on the simplex. So \eta is optimal iff, for some t, every active kernel (\eta_m > 0) has \partial J / \partial \eta_m = -t and every inactive one has \partial J / \partial \eta_m \geq -t.

    With the gradient of Proposition 8.3, all active kernels reach the same maximal value of \alpha^\top K_m \alpha, and inactive kernels have a value no larger.

    In words, \alpha^\top K_m \alpha = \|f_m\|^2 / \eta_m^2 measures how much kernel m correlates with the dual residual \alpha = (y - \hat y)/(n\lambda). Only the kernels that explain the residual best are switched on.

  13. ★★ Random features: variance, in Python. (a) Show that a single term 2\cos(\omega^\top x + b)\cos(\omega^\top y + b) has variance 1 + \kappa(2\delta)/2 - \kappa(\delta)^2, where \delta = x - y.

    1. For the Gaussian kernel with \sigma = 1 and \|\delta\| = 1, compare the predicted standard deviation of z(x)^\top z(y) with a simulation over 2000 draws, for D \in \{10, 100, 1000\}.

    Solution: (a) write the term as \cos(\omega^\top \delta) + \cos(u + v + 2b) with u = \omega^\top x, v = \omega^\top y. The two parts are uncorrelated, with second moments (1 + \kappa(2\delta))/2 and 1/2. Subtract the squared mean \kappa(\delta)^2.

    1. Here \kappa(\delta) = e^{-1/2} and \kappa(2\delta) = e^{-2}, so the variance of one term is 0.700. The predicted standard deviations are 0.265, 0.084 and 0.026. A simulation over 2000 draws matches them up to its own Monte Carlo error of a few percent (one run gave 0.266, 0.084 and 0.026). The error decreases like D^{-1/2}.

  14. ★★★ The neural tangent kernel of a two-layer network. (a) Derive the formula of Example 10.5 from Theorem 10.2. (b) With the code of ch. 10, measure the relative error between the empirical and the limiting NTK for widths m \in \{10^2, 10^3, 10^4, 10^5\} and estimate the rate. (c) Train the network by gradient descent with a small step on 20 points of a one-dimensional regression problem, and compare its predictions with Proposition 10.4 as m grows.

    Hints and expected result: (a) sum the contributions of a and of W, using independence of a_j and w_j and \mathbb{E} a_j^2 = 1. (b) The error decreases roughly like m^{-1/2}, about a factor 3 per decade of width.

    1. For large widths, the trained network and the NTK predictor agree closely and the weights barely move (lazy regime). For small widths they separate. Report the relative change \|W_t - W_0\| / \|W_0\| as a function of m.

  15. ★★★ Kernels on a graph for semi-supervised classification. On two-block stochastic block models with within-block probability 0.2 and between-block probability q \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.1\}, compare the diffusion kernel and the regularized Laplacian kernel for kernel ridge regression from 2 labels per class. Study the accuracy as a function of \beta and explain the limits \beta \to 0 and \beta \to \infty.

    Hints and expected result: as \beta \to 0, both kernels tend to the identity, so nothing propagates and unlabelled vertices get the prediction 0. As \beta \to \infty on a connected graph, e^{-\beta L} \to \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top, and every vertex gets the same prediction. The accuracy is therefore maximal at an intermediate \beta, comparable to the inverse spectral gap of the graph.

    It degrades as q grows and the communities blur. Report curves averaged over 20 random graphs.

  16. ★★★ Choosing the bandwidth of an MMD test. For the Gaussian-versus-Laplace problem of ch. 8, estimate the power of the permutation test at level 0.05 as a function of the bandwidth \sigma \in \{0.1, 0.3, 1, 3\} and of the sample size m \in \{50, 100, 200\}. Compare with the median heuristic, then implement a selection of \sigma on half of the data and testing on the other half.

    Hints and expected result: the two distributions differ in the tails and in the peak, so small and medium bandwidths are more powerful than very large ones.

    With a very large \sigma, expand the kernel in powers of 1/\sigma^2. The first term of the squared MMD is \frac{(\mathbb{E}X - \mathbb{E}Y)^2}{\sigma^2} .

    More generally, when the moments of order below r agree, the first non-zero term is proportional to the squared difference of the moments of order r, at order \sigma^{-2r}.

    Here the means, variances and third moments agree, so the signal is of order \sigma^{-8} (about 1.6\, \sigma^{-8}), far below the estimation noise, and the power falls towards the level.

    Selecting \sigma on held-out data and testing on the rest keeps the level exact. It may beat the median heuristic despite halving the sample, which is the idea of Gretton et al. (2012b) and of later work learning deep kernels for testing (Liu et al., 2020).

Projects and assessment

The official assessment, per master-mva.com, combines one final exam (40%), a data challenge (40%) and 3 homework assignments submitted online (20%); the Université Paris-Saclay course detail classifies it as “Evaluation Continue non Intégrale” (partial continuous assessment). The solo subjects below are sized like a homework assignment extended into a short study. The group subjects rehearse the data challenge. The written exam proposal shows the kind of questions a 40% final exam can ask; none of these proposals is official material.

Solo projects

  1. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Random features versus Nyström: a paper study. Reproduce the main experiments of Rahimi and Recht (2007) and the comparison of Rudi, Camoriano and Rosasco (2015).
    • Implement: kernel ridge regression with random Fourier features and with Nyström landmarks, from NumPy.
    • Data: a public regression set such as California housing (scikit-learn) or YearPredictionMSD (UCI), subsampled to fit a laptop.
    • Success criterion: plots of test error and time against D and m, showing where each method reaches exact-KRR accuracy, and checking the \sqrt{n} scaling of Theorem 9.5 on 3 values of n.
    • Deliverables: code, a 4–6 page report, a 10-minute talk. Chapters 3, 6, 9.
  2. [Solo · ≈ 20 h] Kernel two-sample tests: a paper study. Study and reimplement the MMD test of Gretton, Borgwardt, Rasch, Schölkopf and Smola (2012).
    • Implement: the unbiased statistic, the permutation test, and the linear-time statistic of the same paper.
    • Data: synthetic problems (Gaussian against Laplace, mixtures with a shifted component, the “blobs” problem in two dimensions).
    • Success criterion: empirical level within 0.05 \pm 0.02 under the null, and power curves against m and against the bandwidth that reproduce the qualitative findings of the paper.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 6, 8.
  3. [Solo · ≈ 15 h] Subsequence string kernels for text. Implement the gap-weighted subsequence kernel of Lodhi et al. (2002) with the O(p\, |s|\, |t|) recursion, and test it against brute force.
    • Task: classify two topics of the 20 Newsgroups corpus (scikit-learn) with an SVM on a precomputed kernel. Use at most 400 documents cut to their first 250 characters, and vectorize the recursion in NumPy (or compile it with Numba) so that the Gram matrix takes minutes, not hours.
    • Success criterion: an accuracy comparison with the bag-of-words and spectrum kernels, with timings and the effect of p and \lambda.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 4, 7.

Group projects

  1. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Data-challenge rehearsal: classifying DNA sequences with hand-made kernel methods. The rule is that every learning algorithm is implemented by the group, without a machine-learning library.
    • Data: the public UCI “Molecular Biology (Splice-junction Gene Sequences)” data set (3190 sequences of 60 nucleotides), or synthetic sequences with planted motifs.
    • Work split: one member writes the kernels (spectrum, mismatch, gap-weighted subsequence); one writes the solvers (KRR, kernel logistic regression by Newton’s method, an SVM dual solver); one runs model selection, kernel combination (ch. 8) and the final submission.
    • Success criterion: a held-out accuracy above a k-mer logistic-regression baseline, with cross-validated hyperparameters.
    • Deliverables: code, a 6-page report, a leaderboard-style submission, a 15-minute talk. Chapters 3, 4, 7, 8.
  2. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Graph kernels for molecules: a paper study. Reproduce part of the experimental table of Shervashidze et al. (2011).
    • Data: MUTAG and ENZYMES, two of the data sets of that table, from the public TU Dortmund graph benchmark collection.
    • Work split: one kernel per member (Weisfeiler–Lehman subtree, shortest-path, random-walk with labels), plus a shared nested cross-validation harness and SVM.
    • Success criterion: accuracies within 2 points of the published ones for the WL kernel, and a timing comparison that reproduces the paper’s ranking of the kernels.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 4, 7.
  3. [Group of 2–3 · ≈ 30 h each] Convolutional kernel networks on small images. Study Mairal et al. (2014) and Mairal (2016), and build a one- and two-layer CKN as in Definition 10.3.
    • Data: the 8×8 digits of scikit-learn, then Fashion-MNIST (public) on a laptop CPU.
    • Work split: patch extraction and Nyström layer with k-means anchors; pooling and the second layer; comparison with a Gaussian-kernel SVM on raw pixels, random features, and a small CNN of similar size.
    • Success criterion: a table of test accuracy against the number of anchors, showing where the two-layer CKN beats the one-layer one.
    • Deliverables: code, report, talk. Chapters 4, 9, 10.

Written exam (proposal)

Format: 3 hours, one handwritten A4 sheet allowed, no electronic device; four exercises, independent of each other.

  1. Course question (4 points). Define a positive definite kernel and a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. State and prove the representer theorem.

    Expected: Definitions 1.1 and 2.1; Theorem 3.1 with the orthogonal decomposition onto the span of the K_{x_i}, the invariance of the data term, and the strict increase of the norm term.

  2. Computation (6 points). Kernel ridge regression with the kernel K(x, y) = \min(x, y) + 1 on [0, 2], the points x = (0, 1, 2), the targets y = (0, 1, 0) and n\lambda = 1.

    1. Show that the kernel is p.d. (b) Compute \alpha and \hat f(1). (c) Compute the degrees of freedom.

    Expected: (a) on [0, 2], \min(x, y) is the L^2 inner product of the indicators of [0, x] and [0, y], hence p.d. (Proposition 1.2); the constant 1 is p.d., and so is the sum (Proposition 1.4).

    1. The Gram matrix has rows (1, 1, 1), (1, 2, 2) and (1, 2, 3); solving (K + I)\alpha = y gives \alpha = \tfrac{1}{13}(-2, 7, -3), \qquad \hat f(1) = \tfrac{6}{13} \approx 0.46 .
    2. From the eigenvalues of K, or from the trace of (K + I)^{-1}: \mathrm{df} = \mathrm{tr}\, K (K + I)^{-1} = 3 - \mathrm{tr}\,(K + I)^{-1} = \tfrac{19}{13} \approx 1.46 .

  3. Short proof (5 points). Show that if \psi is negative definite, then e^{-t\psi} is p.d. for all t > 0. Deduce that e^{-t \chi^2(x, y)} is p.d. on histograms.

    Expected: the auxiliary kernel \tilde K of Theorem 6.6 with its positivity proof, the factorization of e^{-t\psi}, and Proposition 6.8.

  4. Problem (5 points). Let \hat\mu_P and \hat\mu_Q be the empirical mean embeddings of two samples of size m, from P and Q, for a kernel bounded by \kappa^2.

    1. Show that \mathbb{E} \|\hat\mu_P - \mu_P\|^2 \leq \kappa^2 / m. (b) Deduce a bound on the expected error of the plug-in estimate of the MMD: \mathbb{E}\, \Big| \|\hat\mu_P - \hat\mu_Q\| - \mathrm{MMD}(P, Q) \Big| .
    2. Explain why a permutation test has the exact level.

    Expected: (a) Proposition 8.7. (b) The triangle inequality and Jensen give 2\kappa / \sqrt{m}. (c) Under the null hypothesis the pooled sample is exchangeable, so the observed statistic is uniformly ranked among the permuted ones.
